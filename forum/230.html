Dinesh Mon May 23, 2011 12:34 pm #2
> Chanwoo: I want a multi-image reflection holgoram that usable white light.

Depends on what you mean by "multi-image". Do you mean slits like a lenticular or multiplex, or do you mean one image of several separate objects shot separately ?

Multi image white holograms already exist where a rainbow hologram is made with several components/images. Each component/image is made into a separate H1/master/laser transmission hologram. These H1/master holograms are slitted and then imaged onto a second H2/copy plate, with H2 being exposed with a different reference angle for each separate H1. The different reference angles then recreate slits of a different colour at the eye position under white light and the viewer sees images at different colours. If you look at the "Gallery" section of this forum and look for some of Jeff Weil's work, you'll see good examples of this technique.

> Chanwoo: Is it impossible?

No

Dinesh Tue May 24, 2011 1:31 pm #4
> Chanwoo: I expected H2 will be reflection hologram.

Yes, you're right, it should be a reflection. How are you reconstructing the H2? Also, how far away is the H1 image from the H2? If the H1 images on the H2, you should see this in reflection in white light.

I did this many years ago and I found that the H2 is very, very bright. I also got a lot of noise, so it was difficult to see the image through the noise. So, a few things occur to me:

1. The image is very noisy in white light, but not so noisy in coherent light. Try reconstructing with a led. Leds have a better coherence than white light and less than a laser, so you may kill the noise a bit with a led.

2. The image may be too far away from the H2 image plane

3. The slits may be too far or too near or too narrow band. In a multiplex, the actual "image" is a set of slits, behind which is an image. In a transmission multiplex, the transmission geometry allows dispersion, by which you get a multiple of slits - the rainbow. However, in a narrow band reflection geometry, you may not get any (significant) dispersion. Thus you get only one or two slits (depending on how narrow band the material is). If this slit is not at the eye position, then you may not see anything. However, the laser will reproduce a divergent image and so the laser shows the image. When I did this, I did it on Agfa 8E75 and I got a narrow range of slits, but not the whole spectrum.

Some researchers from the Kyoto Institute of Technology sent me a paper on this geometry sometime around 1996 or 1997. Let me find it and I will scan it and put it up.
