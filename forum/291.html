Hey There,

You can take one off your list right away. The laser stand.

Anything before the beam splitter can move all it wants without problems. Ok, for the picky, if the laser moves enough it won't make it though the spatial filter. I'm not talking about that much movement. I'm talking about micro movements.

If your doing single beam stuff the "beam splitter" becomes the plate/object combo. Those cannot move but the laser can. If your doing split beam work anything before the splitter can move. Lasers, mirrors, etc....

Dinesh Wed Aug 03, 2011 10:52 am #3
Assumptions:

This is dcg.

This is not old dcg

You did not shoot it at 457.

If so, blue generally means that it was over exposed or that it was a little hot in your studio, or that the space where you dried the plate was hot (perhaps a heat source underneath the settling table?).

The reason it's blue is that the emulsion hasn't swelled enough, in fact, it's shrunk (again assuming you were not shooting at 457). The reasons that dcg emulsions shrink is because either the emulsion was already largely cross linked in the container before you applied it to the plate, or that it cross linked while drying on the plate. This mean that the dcg is already hardened and that the hardened portions will not take a latent image. Very old plates will also show this kind of blue shift because dark reaction accomplishes the same thing as heat - large portions of the emulsion have already cross linked.

Assuming it's silver, then the blue may be because of overexposure and/or bad ratio. If it's a Slavich emulsion, I'd strongly suspect it to be because of bad ratio; especially since you say the object was pewter, which means the majority of the scattered light may not have hit the plate. Slavich emulsions, in my experience, tend to be very sensitive to ratio, if the ratio is slightly off, the image is very weak. The fact that the beak and the right wing tip is slightly brighter than the surrounding image also leads me to suspect ratio. It implies that the eagle was tilted slight, causing the right wing tip to be nearer to the plate and causing more light from the right wing tip to hit the plate. I've seen this kind of blue with VRP-M when my ratio was off. Overexposure also causes this blue shift because overexposure hardens the gelatin.

If this is a single beam Denisyuk, check that you're at p polarisation.

> holomark: I did not use distilled water with the developer (used culligan treated tap water)

Unlikely. Impurities in the water may cause scattering, resulting in a noisy image, or they may cause a discolouration of the entire emulsion, but I doubt that it would cause this deep blue, but still relatively clean, image.

Hi Holomark,

I started out using a very similar laser/film/developer combination as you are using. The reason your holograms are blue (mine tended to bluish green) is because the JD-2 developer kit (including the dichromate bleach) tends to remove some material from the emulsion during processing/bleaching. So, when you dry your plate it shrinks a little, bringing the fringes closer together and thus playing back at a slightly shorter wavelength..... hence blue holograms. The effect is made worse if you try to rapidly dry your plates using, for example, a hot air gun. When I switched to D19 developer/ ferric EDTA bleach my holograms shot with 532 nm light stayed a beautiful shade of green (until I messed with them, that is ). Having said that, I do think the JD-2 kit results in somewhat brighter, crisper holograms than the D19. Ron.

Hello Ron,

The D-19 will also shrink the emulsion a bit. Its got a ton of sodium sulfate in it as a preservative. That sulfate is also an elemental silver solvent. So as the halide is converted into black silver it's eaten away, leaving less bulk in the emulsion which shows as shrinkage and a blue shift.

Its not as much shrinkage as the dicromate bleach is giving you but its still there and if you want to make a reflection master that little bit will cause the holo to play back less bright under the laser.

There's a mix out there called D-19 minus. It's the Kodak recipe without most of the sulfate. If you like D-19 that's the way to go.

Jeffrey Weil Tue Aug 09, 2011 10:24 pm #19
> holomark: I don't really know what you mean by that. Why is the information smaller?

Hello Holomark,

The size of the fringes you record are based on a few things. The two main variables are the frequency of the laser your using and the angle in which they meet when they interfere with each other.

The shorter the wavelength, the smaller the interference it produces.

Also, as the angular difference between those two beams increases, the interference that is produced gets smaller. This has a much larger effect on the size of the interference pattern that's produced than the color of the laser your using.

Imagine you have a clock. The two hands are two different beams coming together at the center of the clock and interfering with each other and producing fringes.

At 12:00 both hands are lined up. There is no angular difference between them. In this case the interference is so large you can easily see it with your naked eye. You would see something like this with a Michaelson interferometer, or when an expanded beam is bounced off a second surface mirror and you can see the “bands”.

If on our clock its 12:55 the two beams are now interfering at 60 degrees when they meet in the center of the clock face. That interference is too small to be easily seen even with an optical microscope.

Even though its very small its still a transmission hologram.

Now, lets get to refection time. Imagine the clock is now set for 9:10. The beams now have much more angular difference than the earlier examples. The beams are now coming from different sides of the center of the clock where they meet.

Just like your object and reference beam when you make a reflection hologram.

If going from 12:00 to 12:55 could make the fringes so much smaller that they go from being visible to hard to see with equipment how much smaller would 9:10 be!

That is why a reflection holograms fringes are much smaller and therefore require and equal added amount of stability.

Meters, there is no reason to not have one. I'm sure there are many designs on this board or the wiki to help you build one cheaply. It doesn't have to meter is cm2 or anything like that. You only need it for relative readings. How strong is this compared to this. Or to read the reference so you can put all your power into either beam for tweaking and then get back to where you started.

You don't need to do math for exposures. Just learn how to judge how dark something should develop to or do test exposures and see what looks best.

Sometimes its best to put away the calculator and just make some holograms and do some experiments.

Holomark,

Seriously though, the broad description (definition?) between reflection and transmission is that in the former both beams come from the same side of the plate and in the latter both beams come from opposite sides of the plate. As Jeff says, the angle between the two determines the fringe spacing, actually the sin of the angle. If your object beam is at right angles to the plate, ie the object is directly in front of the plate, then the greatest angle you can have and still shoot a transmission is 90 degrees (right up against the side of the plate). If you shoot in reflection, the reference is greater than 90 (across to the other side). The other factor is where exactly these fringes are. In a transmission, the "fringes" are (very) tiny lines across the surface of, and penetrating a little way into, the plate; imagine a mountain range with hills and valleys. The actual hologram is nothing more than a photograph of these fringes (except, of course, you're actually standing inside the camera, rather than holding the viewfinder to your eyes). As with any photograph, if the subject moves, the picture gets blurred. In holography, however, the brightness and clarity of the hologram depends on how faithfully the camera picks up these tiny lines. If the lines get blurred, the edges of the fringes are no longer as sharp as they could be. In this case, the diffraction from these lines gets muddied and the image quality suffers. The sharpness of the lines defines the sharpness of the diffraction. The sharpness of the diffraction determines the sharpness of the image.

In a refelction hologram, the "fringes" are actually planes within the emulsion. Imagine a layered cake or a trifle. The colour of a reflection hologram is the spacing between these planes; the narrower the spacing the bluer the image, the wider the spacing the redder the image. These planes are the Bragg planes. When you shoot a hologram, the Bragg plane spacing is determined by the laser wavelength and the angle. When you shoot, but before you develop, there is already a set of Bragg planes within the emulsion, this is called the "latent image". It's too faint to see anything. Development enhances the latent image by magnifying the structure some 100,000 times. Unfortunately, though, wet processing changes the entire emulsion, causing the Brag planes to shrink or expand along with the emulsion. This is why your holograms are blue, there was a drastic shrinkage. The real question is, what caused such a drastic shrinkage? Something chemical, ie in the development process, or something physical.

As for ratio and exposure, all emulsions have a "sensitivity". This is the amount of light energy they take to create a latent image. Too little light and there's no, or very small, latent image. The development can't magnify what ain't there. Too much exposure and the entire emulsion alters, not just the planes or lines. Since there's no structure, there's no diffraction. The other factor is the variation between fringe (plane) and not-fringe (not-plane), the "height" of the hills as opposed to the depth of the "valleys" in a transmission hologram. This is known as "fringe modulation". Theory tells us that the higher the modulation, the better the image, up to a point. Beyond a certain level - too much modulation - and the fringe structure loses it's structure - the fringes end up looking like a castle wall or turret rather than rolling hills and valleys.. This depends on the ratio, too high of a ratio and you get a castle turret, too low of a ratio and the hills become too shallow.

Most commercial materials state their sensitivity on their websites. If you get your film from Alec Jeong, he also states the senstivity on the Integraf website. Generally, the older the film or the warmer the storage environment, the less sensitive it is, so you add about 20% on top of the stated sensitivity. Ratio is a more difficult problem. Generally - very generally - you need about 4:1 for transmission and 1:1 for reflection. But, these are ratios at the plate. The beam splitter setting is not (usually) 50:50 or 20:80, since the various mirrors etc all lose light, the actual ratio at the plate is not (usually) the splitter setting. How to determine the correct ratio? There are various ways. I use the "finger shadow" method. I wiggle my fingers in the ref beam and note the shadow. If the ratio is correct, the shadow has a grey-ish appearance. Too black of a shadow and there's too much refrence, too light of a shadow and you've got too little reference. The reflectivity of the object determines the ratio at the plate, so you adjust the illuminating beam until you get the desired ratio at the plate. So, if the object is highly reflective (not necessarily metal, I don't like shooting metal), then you set your splitter to throw more light at the reference to get your 4:1 or whatever.

Jeff, and more experienced holographers, have a "feel" for the correct ratio and exposure, but this comes with experience. My suggestion would be to use a meter on the ref (plenty of circuits for meters for holography on the web), read the power at the plate, and divide that power by the sensitivity. So, if your meter reads 300 mW at the plate and the sensitivity is 150 mJ, then the exposure time is 300/150 = 2 seconds. A couple of things to watch out for: most commercial power meters have an aperture of 1 cm^2, so usually the "per centimetre squared" is left out. If you're home-built meter does not have a 1 cm sq aperture, determine what the power per sq cm would have been and calculate accordingly, also, the above formula does not take into account the object beam, so you need to add a little more than the power reading from the meter, eg add 1/4 on top for a 4:1 ratio. After a little while you'll get a feel for the ratio and exposure and have an idea on how to bracket your exposures for tests, just like a photographer.

Dinesh Thu Aug 18, 2011 8:45 am #26
> holomark: First am I measuring total exposure at plate or am I only measuring the exposure from the reference beam?

Ideally, you should be measuring both. The total energy the plate receives comes from both reference and object. However, it's not easy to measure the object because the object beam is expanding outwards from the object. By the time the light gets to the plate it's coming from all different directions and expanding outwards. Most detectors aren't designed to detect this kind of light, they measure light coming in directly into the aperture. So, for object light, you have to deduce (or observe) the intensity hitting the plate. For a transmission you need somewhere between a 3:1 to 5:1 (Bob says he goes 10:1 to reduce noise, I've never gone that high) One way of doing this is to observe the shadow of something from the ref , say your fingers, on a white card in the plateholder while illuminated by the object. The object light will illuminate the shadow caused by the reference and so will cause it to become a grey-ish colour. Once you see this grey-ish shadow, you've probably got a reference that's between 1/3 and 1/5 times the reference, so you add R + R/ratio, so if your ratio is 4:1 the light energy is R + R/4.

For a single beam Denisyuk you need 1:1. One technique to get this is to put a white card in the plateholder and make a small hole in the middle, say a one inch diameter hole in a 4x5 card. The reference illuminates the front of the card (the side facing the reference), goes through the hole and illuminates the object (the model). The scattered light from the model will hopefully throw light all over the back of the card. Now you should be able to see the ratio by observing the light intensity on the front and back of the card. You have to bear in mind that you only illuminate a small part of the object with this technique, so you have to extrapolate what light you would have got with the full reference beam hitting the object. But, the method will give you a ballpark ratio.

> holomark: Second for the purposes of estimating the reference beam at the plate - what is the formula to determine mW of expanded beam at plate after optics. For example I have a 100 MW beam (I suppose beam size may matter?)

The basic idea is that the emulsion needs to receive a certain amount of energy in order to cause the physical changes necessary to record the image. The power that the plate receives is the amount of energy the the plate receives per second. So, if the plate is getting 1 mW of power, the plate is receiving 1mJ of energy every second. If the plate needs 150 mJ of power (the sensitivity), then you need to keep the beam coming in for 150 seconds. So your laser is giving out 100 mW/sq cm. and if the beam were spread out over 2 sq cm, then each sq cm would receive 50 mW/sq cm of power and so 50 mJ of energy/sec. A 4x5 plate is 125 sq cm, so the 100 mW beam is spread over 125 mW giving 100/125 = 80uW/sq cm, or 80 uJ/cm^2/sec. This would be a 2 second exposure for a Slavich VRP, for example. This is not exactly right because the beam is Gaussian and doesn't expand uniformly. Generally, the edges are about 1/3 as bright as the centre.

> holomark: I have on reference side - 1 expanding lense, 2 steering mirrors, 1 collimating mirror, and 1 final steering mirror to plateSo for example if I used the 50/50 I have 50mW *(expansion) - mirror losses = mW of expanded beam

The basic idea is correct, but determining mirror, or any, losses, the second expression in your equation should read just "losses", is not easy. It's dependent on angle of reflection, polarisation and how clean the mirrors are, dust in the air, spurious reflections off posts etc. Also, by how much the light is expanding at the lens. You could look up curves for reflectivity and formulae in books etc. A far easier method is to either measure or do exposure tests. Assume you've lost 50% due to losses, so your 50mW ref is now 25mW, calculate the power density at the plate - so 25mW over a 4x5 plate gives 200uW/sq cm.If your doing a single beam Denisyuk and you've got a good ratio, then you've got 400 uW/sq cm total at the plate. Assuming your plate sensitivity is 150 uJ/sq cm, then your exposure is 150/400 = 0.375 seconds - about a 1/3 of a second. So, get a black card the size of your plate with slits that are individually removable. Remove the first slit, expose for 1/3 second, replace the first slit, remove the second slit and expose for 2/3 second etc . After 4 such slits, your exposure will have ranged from 1/3 second (your calculated ideal based on 50% loss) to 8/3 of a second. If your image is still weak, then your losses are greater than the assumed losses so repeat, but start the next set at 8/3 seconds.

> holomark: ?? But - How do I check for polarization??

A diffuse object will depolarise anyway, so the ratio is deceptive since the fringe intensity falls off as the angle between the two polarisations (of the ref and object beams) varies. But, if you're shooting a single beam Denisyuk and you're coming in at Brewsters but at s polarisation, then you'll lose 20% of light at the first surface before it gets to the object. Brewster's angle only "works" for p polarisation (remember - "p" passes" and "s"skips") . To check if you have p polarisation. let the raw, unexpanded beam hit a glass surface - say a piece of 4x5 glass - at normal incidence, ie "head on". Then slowly rotate the glass surface in a horizontal plane (assuming you've got a side ref) and note the reflected spot. At some point the reflected spot will slowly fade to zero if you've got p polariation. If the beam intensity never fades, you've got s polarisation. If the spot intensity never gets completely dark, then you've got a mixture of polarisation states.
