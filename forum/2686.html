BobH Sat Aug 28, 2004 4:20 pm #3
I think you've voiced one of the main reasons why holography hasn't been accepted in the general public. Even when properly installed and lit, it's very difficult to find holograms (especially large ones) which fit easily into home decor.

The light source needed to make the hologram work its magic is essentially an intense highlight. The use of LEDs makes the hologram look better, but not the wall (too saturated in my opinion). Veiling reflections can easily compete with the image, especially during the daytime. And as you point out, the object is essentially a black plate with grey "swirrels" on it when viewed outside the viewing zone.

The best solution I've seen is when holograms are incorporated into sculpture, instead of being framed and put on the wall. It's a more appropriate setting for the three dimensional imagery, because sculpture makes a much better interface between the holographic space recorded and the real world around it.

For the wall, however, I think the "Portholes" made by Laser Reflections is the best solution I've seen for image-planed reflection holograms. Also, Denisyuk holograms work much better on the wall because of their super-wide viewing zone. I like Nancy Gorglione's sculpturally framed work as well (I'd call it visual jazz). Certainly there are other examples, but none would simply be framed flat plates.

BobH Mon Aug 30, 2004 4:32 pm #8
To increase the viewing zone of a "subject" hologram, how about making a a double exposure HOE as follows:

A) a common reference beam diverges from a point at normal incidence and a distance equal to the H1 to H2 distance of the "subject" hologram,

B) the first exposure "object" beam diverges from a point off to the right and at the same distance as the reference beam and in the horizontal plane,

C) the second exposure "object" beam diverges from a point off to the left and at the same distance as the reference beam and also in the horizontal plane, and

D) the resulting HOE is placed in front on the "subject" hologram for display.

The effect is that the image (viewing zone) reconstructed by the "subject" hologram becomes the conjugate of the reference beam of the HOE, which then diffracts the image into two similar images off to the sides. I remember making this a very long time ago and honestly can't remember where the idea came from (it may be published somewhere), but it did work. The horizontal distance between the reference and "object" point sources should equal the width of the viewing zone of the "subject" hologram.

BobH Tue Aug 31, 2004 4:37 am #10
> The returned, reconstructed object beam now goes through the HOE and diffracts left and right, since the reconstructed object beam forms the reference for the HOE. Correct?

Correct, except your sentence should read "since the reconstructed object beam forms the reconstruction for the HOE" at normal incidence.

> wouldn't it cause massive distortions in the reconstructed reflection hologram, unless the H2 were originally made with the correct angle matched to the HOE.

The reconstruction beam for the "subject" hologram comes in from the top and therefore goes right on through the HOE without modification (because it's way off Bragg for the HOE, it only diffracts horizontally). The reconstructed object of the "subject" hologram is the H1, which is at the same distance from the H2 as the HOE's reference point source (and both are at normal incidence). The H1 is then imaged to the left and right, which means the image at the H1 plane is also seen (undistorted) to the left and right.

The HOE must be of very high transparency and diffraction efficiency. Maybe someone on the forum can identify the originator of this technique, it wasn't me. Possibly Steve Benton, early to mid '80s?
