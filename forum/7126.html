BobH Mon Jan 28, 2008 7:30 pm #3
I'd think the resolution would be limited by the reconstruction source, not the optics used to magnify the insect. If you use a laser, you'll be limited because of speckle. If you use a filtered micro-short arc lamp or LED, you'll be limited by the source size and spectral bandwidth blurring.

The strong lens or lenses you use to magnify the image will distort it, but I guess you'll get pretty good results if you can accept a restricted viewing zone. Aiming good images toward one or both eyes only, like in the popular microscope or stereopticon images is another option, with a number of ways to implement. Stereogramming them is probably the best but most expensive way to go, but I think I'd like the analog approach.

Bob, I built a interferometric microscope that used speckle to provide wide field illumination, the laser was purposefully passed through a diffuser which was then spun at high spee. Because the constantly evolving speckle was sent to both the sample and reference arm you still got interference fringes, the fact that the diffuser was spinning was averaged out by the CCD camera, the reason I did it was to allow wide field imaging but it also got rid of all that horrible unwanted interference. I wonder if something similar could be done for holography, of course vibration would be a killer. I used a brushless motor spinning a disk of laser jet film, the stuff used for printing PCB masks on to as my diffuser, that was boxed to reduce air movement.

How about this as a low tech alternative?

Make the best h2 reflection hologram of a mounted bee as posible then place a magnifying glass over the finished hologram for viewing. This allows for equal magnification in all three axis which a hologram is not able to do. If this is done carefully the resolution can be surprising.

Here is the holographic Microscopy I did and it took about 15 minutes to set up.

[http://www.holograms3d.com/PhotoGal/General/AntHolo.JPG](http://www.holograms3d.com/PhotoGal/General/AntHolo.JPG)

I took a dead ant and cut him in half. I then took an old pinhole that was clogged in the magnetic mount for a spatial filter and glued the ant on it at its abdomin and coming out of the surface. I placed the magnetic mount back in the spatial filter. I then flipped the objective around the other way in the spatial filter and was able to use the x, y, z adjustment of the spatial filter to fine tune everything. I then placed my largest lens between the ant mount and the plate to try to get the largest field of view possible.

This is pretty close to what I did.

[http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustration ... scopy3.jpg](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/HoloMicroscopy3.jpg)

While it is not an ellaborate set up and surely could have been taken to the next level (mostly a couple more object beams, it was really cool seeing the ant magnified.
