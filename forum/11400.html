It's not woodgrain. It's probably object motion.

Antihalation coatings are designed to prevent "ringing" of the laser beam, as it hits the back of the material, reflects, and hits the front, and then reflects again to hit the back. These continuous reflections will expose silver halide, creating a set of lines that look like woodgrain - the grain in a piece of wood. If you're using polymer, it's unlikely to be woodgrain; woodgrain manifests itself by a darkening of the material ever time it's hit with light during the ''ringing" process. The actinic reaction of polymers is not absorption-dependent.

Light entering an interface between two media at the Brewster angle with TM polarisation will inhibit reflections, as per the Fresnel equations. But, it's necessary to know the index between the two materials to calculate the Brewster angle. However, bear in mind that the polarisation state depends on the plane of incidence. If the plane of incidence is twisted, you cannot get pure TM polarisation.

However, there is no such thing as "Fresnel reflection", as opposed to any other kind of reflection; the phenomena of reflection is defined by the equation:i = r ( angle of incidence = angle of reflection ). The Fresnel equations determine the intensity - well, actually, the amplitude - of reflected light, based on the polarisation state of the light, assuming the polarisation state is either TM or TE. Any other plarisation state is not covered by the Fresnel equations, though the equations can be used for linear polarised light that is not TM or TE, by taking a weighted average. Elliptically polarised light - most forms of light - will not obey the Fresnel equations. But, again, the TM/TE state is based on the plane of incidence; if the plane of incidence is twisted, the Fresnel equations will not apply without an averaging process. The Brewster effect is due to the fact that if the E vector of the reflected light is perpendicular to the refracted light, the reflectivity drops to zero because the resulting intensity is the dot product of the the two vectors.

The Fresnel equations for the reflectivity of TM and TE polarisation are:

R(TE) = |{n(1) cos(θ(i)) - n(2) cos(θ(t)}/n(1) cos(θ(i)) + n(2) cos(θ(t))}|²

R(TM) = |{n(1) cos(θ(t)) - n(2) cos(θ(i)}/{n(1) cos(θ(t)) - n(2) cos(θ(i))}|²

where n(1) and n(2) are the indices of the two media, θ(i) is the angle of incidence and θ(t) is the angle of transmission.

Of course T = 1 - R.

Also, notice that if n(1) cos(θ(t)) = n(2) cos(θ(i) - the Brewster condition - the reflectivity, R(TM) = 0.

By the by, there are interesting effects of the Fresnel equations. On a rainbow, If you calculate the light hitting the back of a raindrop, the Fresnel equations predict that the TM component will vanish, leaving only the TE component ( [http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hb ... owpol.html](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/atmos/rbowpol.html) ). However, nobody calls a rainbow a "Fresnel reflection". Well, almost nobody.

If the sun is about an hour or two before sunset, light reflected off a body of water - say an ocean - will be polarised as per the Fresnel equations. But, nobody refers to sunlight reflecting off the ocean as a "Fresnel reflection".

> Alan Sailerwrote:↑Thu Dec 19, 2024 12:17 pm: Object motion is possible. But wouldn't the fringes from an interference hologram stay put?

No, they wouldn't. You get these interference lines because the light from the object at one position (the object light) will interfere with the light from the object from another position, causing the object light to interfere with itself if the motion is small (about half a wavelength) creating interference lines on the image. However, a complex object moves in complex ways. So, if you look at the object at some angle, you'll see a specific set of interference lines, dependent on how the object moved in the viewing direction. If you move your head and see the image from another direction, you'll see a different set of lines, depending on how the object moved with respect to the new viewing direction. If the motion is continuous, the lines seem to move.

Not sure what you mean by "interference holograms" There is a technique of non-destructive testing known as 'holographic interferometry". The idea is that you make a hologram of some object (no motion lines!), then you replace the plate as accurately as you can while leaving the original object in place. Now, any small motion of the object will create interference lines between the real object and the image - provided the motion is within about a wavelength or two. It's used for architectural modelling, musical instruments and failure modes of some model, like a bridge for example. If you want to try this, remember that the movement must be very small. These days, this technique uses digital holography, so the interference pattern can be captured. I would say, almost certainly, laser instability. I would wager that you're not seeing this effect with a helium-neon laser. Anyone who has made holograms with laser diodes probably knows this situation well. I have seen it frequently with laser diodes, and also occasionally with OPSL's like Sapphire. They are called "contour fringes" because they seem to follow the contours of the object. The laser is losing its coherence during the length of the exposure, and the coherence length corresponds to the light/dark contour bands across the object. With some diode lasers (which are prone to mode-hopping) the width of the bands can be something like 1 to 3mm wide. On other lasers like Sapphire, the bands can be more like 4 or 5mm. This loss of coherence can be unpredictable for some lasers, such as some types of Sapphire. With my 488-200 (not the "single frequency" version), I would say this happens about 10% of the time. Not good of course, but I can live with it. I have seen this reported on this forum even with such lasers as the Melles-Griot 457nm laser (58-BLD-605) which isn't known to mode-hop (mine didn't). Someone also reported that even a 532nm ring-cavity laser would occasionally mode-hop. I have a paper in which the authors use this effect to generate contour-fringes, deliberately. They varied the size of the fringes by making small changes to the wavelength of a dye laser.

At any rate, when you're recording a hologram, the beam ratio is important, as is polarisation because you're creating a diffractive structure. You're creating a structure - the diffractive structure - based on the modulation, the variation between the actinic to non-actinic reaction. Polarisation is important in these circumstances because the modulation is proportional to the cosine of the polarisation angles of the two beams. If, for example, the polarisation of the (two) beams were perpendicular, then, since cos(90) = 0, there'd be no modulation. Also, if either beam is elliptically polarised there'd be no modulation. But, the important point here is that you're creating a diffractive structure, there is no diffractive structure until the process is complete. Even then, modulation is very low if further processing is required. So, for example, the modulation for unprocessed silver and DCG holograms is very, very low and is known as the 'latent image'. In silver halide, for example, development amplifies the modulation by some 10,000,000 times. The only parameters in this interchange is the energy of the light and the modulation of Bragg structure. The efficiency of the energy interchange - the 'diffractive efficiency' - is based on how much energy goes to the new light field and is defined as the ratio of the energy of the original lightfield and the new lightfield.
