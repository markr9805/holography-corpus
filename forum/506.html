Dinesh Tue Mar 27, 2012 1:03 pm #2
There are mainly two ways of shooting any hologram: traditionally and compuationally; I include dot matrix as computational because the angle between the beams is calculated, while the hologram itself is shot using the traditional method. In the traditional method, an object and/or transparencies is illuminated and the reflected light (or transmitted light, in the cases of a shadowgram or a transparency) is recorded with a reference beam. In the computational methods, the "scene" is calculated, ie the light field that a real scene would have generated is calculated by the computer in terms of brightness and direction. This calculated scene is then recorded on the hologram. The hologram is recorded either by being generated as individual rays in given directions, as per the calculation, and then a reference beam is added pixel by pixel, or by direct writing. In the case of direct writing, the combined light field of both the reference and the object is calculated and then the interference pattern, as calculated by the computer, is imaged directly on the hologram.

For a colour hologram, this process is simply done three (or more) times, for the three (or more) components. In traditional holograms, three (or more) lasers are used and three (or more) object beams illuminate the object with three (or more) reference beams. Each object and reference beam will record it's own pattern and , ideally, each set of patterns will independantly reconstruct it's own colour. So, when illuminated with white light, each component of the white light reconstructs its own image and a coloured scene results. In practice it's not so simple and you'll get crosstalk and, sometimes, lack of registration. Also, if the film swells, you've lost colour rendering.

For a colour hologram created computationally, the computation is simply carried out three times. Then either the whole ensemble is imaged directly or three (or more) lasers are used to generate three (or more) sets of rays with each ray having its own reference in its own colour. Examples of computational colour holograms are RabbitHoles, MIT and Zebra amongst others. There are a lot of Japanese groups also working on computational colour holograms.

In traditional colour holography, several lasers are used. There are several opinions on exactly how many wavelengths and which ones. Several papers point out that there are "best" wavelengths for colour holography. It all has to do with colour rendition and the appropriate gamut on the CIE. Personally, I don't believe that the 1931 or the 1976 CIE chart are appropriate for colour holograph, but that's another issue.

> holorefugee: I had a very instructive talk with Hanz about three color Denisyuk holograms. As long as you have even illumination of all three colors you can make a beautiful hologram. This requires matching the beam diameters and dispersions through a single spatial filter. There is some math one can do to sort this out.


True. The reason being that the reconstruction beam is white light with all the relevant components "inline", ie along the ray direction of the white light. This assumes, of course, no dispersion of the white light source. For the sun this would be true since gases are not dispersive (well, not much anyway). However, if you use an incandescent source such as a filament or halogen then there is some dispersion. In addition, there is a colour temperature mismatch. In order to solve these problems, you need to take the statistical distribution of the Bragg plane separations and probably do a convolution over the colour tempaerature curve of the source and/or the photopic curve.

Thus, say for example the hologram contained exactly 10 "red" planes, 10 "green" planes and 10 "blue" planes (This is unlikely! the actual distribution would depend on the sensitivity for a given wavelength and the exposure at that wavelength) and you were illuminating with a source whose R:G:B ratio were 1:2:3, ie three times as much blue as red and twice as much green as red, then the 10 "red" planes would diffract 1 unit of red light, the 10 "green" planes would diffract 2 units of green light and the 10 "blue" planes would diffract 3 units of blue light. The 1 unit of red light would add to the 2 units of green light and so the yellow would probably not be the yellow of the object. It would be quite a coincidence if the actual yellow were 2:1 in red and green by addition. Also, the 2 units of green would be perceived as brighter than the 3 units of blue, since the eye is more sensitive to green.

> PinkysBrain: Hmm, with horizontal only parallax I guess they might get away with using a mirror backed transmission hologram ... it would also explain why the colours might be a bit off on viewing, it would essentially be a rainbow hologram with accurate colours from only a single viewing height.

The Rabbit Holes and Zebra holograms are true reflection, there's no mirror backing. The colours in the hologram are irrespective of angle of view, within the permissible angle of view given by the "virtual" H1.

> zipsister: Maybe I'm wrong but I've always assumed that when making these digital holograms that the reference angle has to be slightly adjusted accordingly (each hogel being recorded with a slightly different angle) so that the finished hologram can be lit with a broad white light from the usual reconstruction angle (somewhere in the 45-55degrees ballpark).

You may be thinking of pseudo-colour. In a pseudo-colour reflection hologram you have to deliberately swell the emulsion to get the various colours. But, if you swell the emulsion you twist the Bragg planes and so you throw the recon angle off slightly. To compensate for this, you shoot the various different "colours" at slightly different angles. There is a paper by someone (McGrew?) that showed a calculation that allowed you to calculate how much to twist the angle by to get specific colours.

> PinkysBrain: Transmission holograms have a fairly relaxed angular selectivity, so in theory instead of changing the angle of the reference beam you could simply "rotate" the object pattern you record for it.

The angular selectivity depends on the slit size. But, if you rotated the image, you'd get aberrations. This would be true for a single diffracted ray in a true Raman Nath diffractive system. Rainbow holograms are not true Raman Nath diffraction, unless you recorded such that the fringe separation were more than ten times the emulsion thickness (roughly); actually even then there would be some Bragg effects. Raman Nath assumes an infinitesimal emulsion thickness, and also the diffraction is a sinusoidal function. However, a real image is not a set of rays but a wavefront and therefore the extremal rays of the wavefront, obeying a sinusoidal law, would distort.

Dinesh Fri Jul 13, 2012 5:18 pm #21
> PinkysBrain: PS. Dinesh, some geometric/intensity distortion is not a big deal in a digital hologram ... you can correct for those to an extent.

Actually, that's one of the reasons I'm interested in finding out about digital and computerised techniques. In the late 80's there was a lot of work done on correcting for multiplexed transmission holograms by pre-distorting the image data. I know Francis Tuffy wrote a paper on it and I rememeber that Mitch Henrion also developed an algorithm. However, these were for correcting distortion with only one "colour", ie, there was only one fringe set which, in white light, produced one set of slits. I wondered about multiple sets of slits to create what Peter Miller described as "pseudo-colour transmission", ie the recording of three or more sets of slits so that at one position the image would appear as "true colour". But, I showed in my paper at ISDH that if you reconstructed the hologram with a diverging white light source (and you usually do if you view it with a halogen source), the the different slit components magnify both in the transverse and longitudinal directions. That is, the slits of different colours are different widths and at different positions along a diagonal (This is the basis of the so-called "Benton achromat angle"), but if you reconstructed with a collimated source such as the sun, then there was no lateral magnification but there was still longitudinal magnification. The slits would still be arranged along a diagonal, but they would be the same size. So, is it possible to pre-distort the source of each slit set individually so that the combined slit set would show no aberration at one given viewing angle for a particular viewing geometry?
