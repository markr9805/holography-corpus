What effect does underexposure or overexposure have on the image in a hologram?
Tony Tue Oct 19, 2004 9:29 pm #2
The exposure has a lot less effect than you'd think. You can vary the exposure by factors of 2-4 under and over and still get a hologram. Optimum brightness doesn't change much over a 2:1 exposure range, I'd say. Expose/develop for the recommended density (~1 for transmission, ~2 for reflection). If it's not bright enough, suspect stability issues.
eric343 Tue Oct 19, 2004 9:40 pm #3
Good to know. Does exposure affect the image quality at all, or is it a there or not there kind of thing?
Tony Wed Oct 20, 2004 12:54 am #4
Image Quality is not affected by exposure much at all, really. Other factors which have much higher affect on the image are, in approximate order:

1. Stability

2. Stability

3. (you guessed it, stability)! Actually, there are multiple sources of problems here, such as vibration, table isolation, laser mode hopping, thermal effects, etc.

4. Object/Reference ratio

5. Lighting of Object

6. Using a spatial filter on the reference beam

7. Cleanliness of optics

8. Being close to Brewster to control reflections, woodgrain

9. Carding off excess/unwanted light

Others may put things in a different order...

MichaelH Wed Oct 20, 2004 8:28 am #10
> eric343: So why care about exposure at all then?.

Think of it like cooking a frozen dinner in the microwave.

Heat it only a little and the meal will get hot in spots and you could eat those but you'd still have some frozen bits that are inedible.

Over-heat it and you end up burning it and your mouth if you attempt to eat it.

Somewhere between those two and you have a good meal. For most frozen dinners that range can be pretty big.

Exposure and development are something like that. There's a pretty large range of exposure times where you can adjust your development in order to get a "good" image. Go too far into the lower range and you won't have enough information burned into the film for the developer to bring out. Go too far over the other end and you'll end up overdeveloping and turning the fringes crispy. Note that for the purists out there I know this isn't a literal description of what's happening.

What constitutes "good" depends on what you're trying to do with the hologram (display vs scientific work). If you start by following the recommendations for your film/developer combination you should get acceptable results as long as you've got the stability issue taken care of. After that, you should start experimenting to work out your own methods.
Dinesh Wed Oct 20, 2004 6:04 pm #11
"Exposure" in a hologram is basically two-fold. You takes the light in and then you alter the garins with it. If you don't alter the grains enough - no image. If you alter the grains too much - no image.

Any material has what's called a "sensitivity". Different materials have different sensitivities dependant on type of material, thickness, photoreceptors etc etc. The idea is that any photographic material works by altering some physical property of the material. In the case of Silver Halide, the emulsion,a layer of material that's coated onto the glass or plastic substrate, is a white crystal. If you know any chemistry, the crystal is a crystal of the compound Silver Bromide (plus other salts for you purists out there ) There is a class of compounds comprising of Fluorides, Iodides, Bromides and Chlorides that are generally called "halides" (for historical reasons). The silver salts of these halides have the peculiar property that when zapped by light, they undergo a "photochemical" or "actinic" reaction (any reaction that's activated by light) and reduce to the bare element silver. Thus, when hit by light some silver halides become silver. Silver at these tiny levels is black. Hence, where light hits the emulsion, it goes black. However, there is a threshold energy for this to happen. Too little light and the reaction won't go. The halide remains stubbornly a halide. The idea of photography is to j-u-u-st hit exatcly convert those silver halide molecules that get hit by light and then stop exposing. If you continue exposing then silver halide molecules not hit by light also go dark. Now you no longer have an exact replica of your image - a one-to-one relationship between the darkness of the film and the light from the subject. So, too little exposure and no conversion of silver halide, too much exposure and parts of the emulsion that are not hit by light start to blacken.

In holography you're trying to take a photograph of very, very tiny lines. Also, these lines have a specific "light profile" that nbeeds to be accurately reproduced in the darkness of the emulsion. If you can imagine these "interference lines" just hanging in space, the lines are pretty intense at their center and fall off in intensity at the edges. So, the emulsion is trying tp capture tiny lines which are bright at the center and fall off at the edges. So, too little exposure and you won't capture all those lines conpletely, too much exposure and you'll get the wrong "profile" of the lines (in the emulsion, the lines will look "fat"). The brightness of a hologram has a direct bearing on the capturing of these lines. If the lines are too thin (underexposure) the hologram will be dim to non-existant, if the lines are too fat, there's no contrast between lines and not-lines and so again the hologram may be dim and non existant (yes, it'll have orders, but I'm trying to keep this simple ). If the subject or the beams move during exposure, the lines will "blur" just like any photograph of a moving object. Again, the line profile is not accruately transferred to a darkness profile in the emulsion.

Having said this, there's a pretty broad range over which youcan expose. Most film manufacturers give sensitivity curves. Here, for example, is the sensitivity of VRP PFG-01: [http://www.geola.com/products_frame.htm](http://www.geola.com/products_frame.htm) and you can see that they both peak at 10^(-3) microJ/sq cm but at different wavelengths. (actually, I think the units are wrong - it should be 10^(-3) J</> /sq cm or about a millijoule). To decide on your exposure, determine the sensitivity of your film (usually the manufacturer has it posted on their site) and determine the power of your beam. Now divide the sensitivy by the beam power. So, if you're beam is 10mW/sq cm and the film sensitivity is 1 mJ/sq cm, then the exposure is 1/10 seconds. As I said, you can be off quite a bit. It also depends on beam ratio, which, as Colin stated, is very important.
