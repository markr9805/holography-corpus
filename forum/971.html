Dinesh Sat Nov 29, 2014 12:53 pm #2
It depends on what you mean by "efficiency". Efficiency, as a numerical parameter, is based on a theoretical model, which determines the basis by which you extract information, for example the efficiency, from a system,. So, for example, in holography, the theoretical model used is Kogelnik (there are other theories, bu Kogelnik is usually used). In the Kogelnik theory, the hologram is modeled as a pair of coupled differential equations derived from Maxwell's equation. There is a physical basis, but you have to read the paper, as it's too involved to state here. However, the theory then solves the coupled differential equations and so extracts expressions from the solutions for efficiency figures, given as a ratio between the S and the R waves (such as the notorious, but incorrect, tanh squared function for the efficiency of a reflection hologram - this is usually the wrong expression). In classical optics, 'efficiency' generally refers to the reflectivity of a surface, and this is based on a theory of ray optics and is given by the Fresnel equations, derived from the ray optic model. In the field of fibre optics, the efficiency depends on the theoretical model used for the optical transmission through the fiber, so that, for example, a non-linear fiber will have a different efficiency to a linear fibre. So, the "efficiency" of a Lippmann photograph depends on the theory of the Lippmann photograph. I don't know if there is already a theory, usually, Lippmann photographs are described in a sort of hand-waving way as a series of laminar planes, but there is no theory of these planes, as far as I know. Without a theory, you cannot have an efficiency figure.

Dinesh Sun Nov 30, 2014 10:41 am #6
> Martin: Such holograms may actually mimic the reflections coming from an aluminum or silver object.

If you mean that the mechanisms of reflection from a metallic object are similar to the mechanisms of a hologram, then it's not true. The broad band basis of reflectivity from a metal is due to the large number of free electrons in a metal and the amorphous nature of a metal. The colour(s) of an object are due to the vibrational modes of the electrons in the substance. The E wave of the light field hits an atom which sets the electron oscillating, then the attraction of the nucleus creates a restoring force and draws the electron back, This to-and-fro motion of the electron generates a new light field. But, if the electron is free (not attached to any atom), then there is no restoring force and the light field simply dissipates. So, in a metal, only a few atomic layers at the surface of a metal are responsible for the propagation of light, which is reflected back. Since only a few atomic layers are generating light, there are not enough oscillators to selectively absorb any given wavelength, thus giving rise to a broad band reflectivity.

In a hologram, there is coupling between the R wave and the S wave (in the Kogelnik theory). The strength of this coupling varies as a result of propagation through the sinusoidal periodic medium. But, the coupling is strongly dependent on the ratio between the wavelength of the light and the grating frequency (and various other factors such as the tilt of the planes). Thus, the selectivity of the hologram is a result of the coupling strength of a specific ratio between the wavelength and the grating frequency. Of course, if there are more than one grating frequencies present, there is more than one coupling and so bandwidth is created.

True, you can always measure and plot results. But, these are observations of a specific mode of reconstruction in a specific geometry. Without a theory, all you can derive is what you have already derived. There is no predictive power in just a measurement. For example, if I make a hologram with, say, a HeNe at 30 degrees and zero degrees. Then I reconstruct it, measure the input power and the output power. I now have a number. Does it mean anything? No. It simply means that you have diffraction occurring. Without the theory, the number in and of itself means nothing. Supposing you make such a hologram, then you measure that 10 units of light go in and 4 units come out - 40% efficiency. Does it mean that in general all holograms made with a HeNe at zero and thirty will be 40% efficient? If someone comes along and says, "I did the same thing and I got a 70% efficiency" Are they wrong? You cannot decide these questions until you know what to expect of the efficiency. Once you know that the hologram is supposed to be, say 73% (according to Kogelnik), then you can say, "Oh, I made a weak hologram" You can repeat this for a number of angles and exposure conditions (I did this for the early Polygramma polymers, took me three days!), but you still have observational conditions. Without a theory, you haven't got any connection to value, only observation.

> unixboy: Usually, people measure such figures and plot the curve before proposing a theory.

No. Kogelnik modeled his coupled wave theory according to an existing theory of acoustic propagation. The experimental verification came later. Generally, if you have a new model, you use mathematics to create a new theory, then you verify your theory by experimentation. The Scientific process is: hypothesis, theory, confirmation, prediction, further hypothesis.

Dinesh Mon Dec 01, 2014 1:14 pm #13
> Martin: I actually meant that a broadband hologram of say a coin may actually look very much the same as the metal object

The reason is the nature of metallic reflection. Metals reflect mostly in the forward direction, and they reflect broadband, as explained earlier. This produces a relatively large amount of energy in the forward direction from a large number of frequencies. This creates a fairly strong latent image in the forward direction, at the expense of weaker latent images in the 'off-axis' directions (the 'axis' being defined as the normal to the face of the metal). Because of the large frequency spread, and the (much) stronger forward reflection, the Bragg structure pretty much has a reflectivity profile similar to the metal itself. However, bear in mind, this also has to do with the sensitivity of the emulsion. So, for example, even though a nickel coin reflects across the spectrum, a hologram of a nickel coin in dcg will only record strong Bragg structures in the blue-green areas of the spectrum. Thus the coins appear an orange colour (shown below). Also bear in mind that it's important to process it so as to bring out the latent image fully. Any partial processing will not process all the components of the latent imge. By the way, a propos of Lippmann's being bright due to the mercury, the same reasoning applies; mercury is also a metallic reflector. In the Lippmann process, the mercury plays a subsidiary role also.

I don't think they are either.

There is this general thinking that a Lippmann is, quite literally, a Denisyuk hologram. It's not. The two are related, but not the same. A Lippmann photograph does not capture phase, it 'captures' the nodes and anti-nodes of a standing wave pattern. In the case of a plane mirror behind the emulsion, the nodes and anti-nodes are the same as the phase relationships of the 'object' (the mirror), assuming that collimated light was used, because the phase of a plane beam is also planar. Divergent light will not produce nodes and anti-nodes by reflection from a plane surface - the light has to be retro-reflected back along it's path. So, in the case of a plane mirror, a Lippmann photograph is the same as a Denisyuk. However, the moment you place an actual diffuse object behind the emulsion, then the Lippmann will not capture phase, just intensity by way of the amplitude ('modulation') of the standing wave components between the incoming and reflected light. In this case, the "efficiency" of the Lippmann will depend on the relative intensity of the forward (illuminating) light and both the direction and intensity of the return light. If the intensity (the 'ratio') of the return light is strong, but the direction is not retro-reflected, then there will be no standing waves - there might be weak standing waves due to an 'almost' retro-reflection if the directions are close. If a narrow band source is used for the Lippmann, then, assuming retro-reflection, there will be only one component of a standing wave pattern (this is a rough interpretation, since the pattern will also have a 'bandwidth' equal to the bandwidth of the source). The reconstruction should not depend greatly on the bandwidth of the recon source, since you basically have an interference filter, which does eliminate light of the wrong frequency. If the recording is carried out with a broadband source, then there will be many components in the standing waves, and the effect is more complicated, again assuming that the direction of the 'recon' light is right. The intensity should be higher, but the efficiency should be carefully defined. In the one case, the measurements are photometric and in the other, they're radiometric. The two don't easily mix from a human perceptual point of view. In the end, it's important to notice that the nodes and anti-nodes of a standing wave pattern is not the same as the interference surfaces of two interfering beams, except in special cases.

I'm not sure why the book says that there will be this displacement of the interference stack system depending on whether the back reflection is on mercury or gelatin. The nodes and anti-nodes depend on the pi/2 phase reversal on reflection from a surface of higher index, so perhaps the fact that gelatin is a di-electric, and so there is not just a complete pi/2 phase reversal on reflection, but an additional phase shift, may be what they're getting at.
