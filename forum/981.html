I would like to pose a question concerning reference beams. I guess the standard reference beam in holography is from a spatial filter, but I've often wondered how much spatial coherence is needed.

Say for example, we bounce a laser beam off an aspirin tablet. The beam will be about 2mm in diameter, and the resulting reflected beam will be highly diverging. I tried this, and the spread beam is quite clean. Due the high divergence of the beam, the aspirin tablet will be quite close to the hologram, say 10cm (4 inches). I am wondering if this will work for a reference beam.
Steven Wed Dec 24, 2014 3:33 pm #2
Seasons greetings Joe,

I think you will find that the Aspirin tablet will depolarize your ref beam.

Do a check using polarized sun glasses.

I don't use a spatial filter when shooting DCG.

I'm using a 9.5mm focal length concave mirror to expand my ref beam.

I do have to hunt around to find a reasonable clean spot on the mirror, it's not perfect.

I can't see a noticeable effect in the finished hologram.

The only way to see if you need spatial filtering (for display holography) is to project the expanding ref beam onto a white screen/large piece of paper and look at it. I look for a reasonably smooth area of even illumination without bulls eyes, or other artefacts.

I think that very short focal length objectives produce poor quality ref beams if not using a spatial filter, unless you find a clean spot that may be off axis.

Steven.

Ed Wesly Fri Dec 26, 2014 8:35 am #8
Being the experimentalist that I am, I would say go ahead and shoot one, what the heck.

I have seen holograms shot (at Lake Forest College Summer Holography Workshops) using the scatter from a ground glass used as the reference beam. But there was a speckle pattern on the final hologram, but that was from the ground glass. I didn’t shoot it, a participant did, and we were surprised that it worked. How well, I can’t say, not having a sample in front of me, but we were amazed that it worked.

However the theoretical side of me is thinking of that over a 2 mm diameter spot it could be imagined there are many point sources, each making their own interference pattern system. Would these overlapping systems “fill in” each others’ fringes (a bright fringe from one falling into another’s dim fringe) so their contrast is lowered, possibly to an unusable level?

This could be proven geometrically, if one had the time to indulge in such fun pastimes. I don’t at the moment. The reason the above experiment worked was that the angular separation between the extreme “reference points” was small, as the ground glass with a similar sized laser spot was about a meter away from the SBR hologram. Your scenario has a much closer spot, and 2 mm is a quite large displacement when we consider the size of the recording waves to be .000532 and .000632 mm!

So why not shoot one and see what happens!

Joe Farina Sun Dec 28, 2014 9:59 am #13
It seems that the opal has better performance because it's a volume material, and the scattering effect is compounded many times as the light passes through the thickness of the opal. With etched glass, the scattering effect is produced from essentially a "single" irregular surface.

As a test, I airbrushed multiple layers of a matte varnish on the back surface of a ball lens, while the front surface remained uncoated. When this was inserted into the laser beam, the results were very good. The beam is first diffused through a "volume" diffusion coating, enters and exits the ball lens, is focused, then spreads out again. As the number of airbrushed layers of matte varnish was increased, the speckle was reduced. There is still speckle, but when the ball lens is very close to the plate, say 3 inches, the speckle is rather slight. If a surface is further away, say 4 inches, the speckle is more noticeable. Light transmission is about 45% and quite a bit of polarization is maintained (the green laser pointer I was using was not fully polarized to begin with, so it was difficult to judge).

I think this method has a lot of promise for very up-close reference beams, which would enable a very compact display for holograms. But more development work is needed regarding the volume diffusion coating on the back of the ball lens. Particularly, what kind of material will produce the best kind of scattering effect, what the thickness should be, and how much light it will transmit. I didn't keep track of the number of airbrushed coatings I used, but it may he been around 30 light coatings.

I don't know what material was used in the matte varnish. It wasn't the opaque pigment titanium dioxide, although this is the most common white pigment, and produces a very high level of scatter. TO2 may be an option, if used in very small quantities, although it's well-known for its opacity. A more transparent white pigment is barium sulfate, which may be a good candidate.

> Joe Farina: I would like to pose a question concerning reference beams. The "cleanliness" of a beam has no bearing on the spatial coherence of a beam. The spatial coherence of a beam depends on the source size of the beam. Ed is correct when he says that you can model the spatial coherence of a beam as the individual rays from the source each creating their own set of fringes, slightly displaced as a result of the small angular deviation of the ray direction over the surface are of the source. However since you're recording wavefunctions, to get an exact measure of the spatial coherence of the wavefunction of the beam from the source geometry, you need Van Cittert Zernike theorem, which basically gives the spatial coherence of the source as a function of the fourier transform of the source. In the case of your aspirin, the source size is fairly large because the aspirin scatters, so being a new source derived from the laser. This will result in a fuzzy image. The polished opal may show more promise.

In regards of the image you want to create, this might interest you. The light is an led approximately an inch and a half from the base of the hologram. The image is a Medal of Honor standing about two inch from the plate, ie the image plane is two inches in front. This was an award for a Medal of Honor recipient who was being honored at a dinner at the Navy Medical Center in San Diego.

BobH Sat Jan 17, 2015 12:16 pm #21
I don't think so Joe. Recording a diffuse object with a point reference source means recording a unique fringe pattern for every point on the object surface. Recording with a diffuse reference source means recording a unique fringe pattern of each point on the object surface as above for each point in the reference aperture. There's also going to be interference patterns recorded by each point in the reference aperture with all other points in it. That's waaaaaaaaay more information than practical materials can record. Those unrecorded patterns will degrade the object recording.
