Dinesh Wed Oct 02, 2013 2:50 pm #6
You need to make a prototype on dcg to make sure that all the parameters are satisfied. Then you can transfer to polymer for the real product. As Sergio points out, the polymer actually is promoted by the heat and uv exposure, provided you pick the right material (as Phil says, a material issue). However, one other problem is that for dcg, the swell rate of the dcg would have to be taken into account. Therefore, the recording geometry would have to be altered to take into account the lack of swelling in a polymer. If the specs are very tight, the swell factor (or lack of) can cause notice-able aberrations. In addition, as the polymer is exposed to the sunlight, slow polymerisation may continue. This means that the swell/shrinkage rate is a function of age and environmental conditions. Thus the shift rate will have to be built into the parameters. Then, of course, there's the azimuthal aberrations, which also will need to be taken into account.

Dinesh Wed Oct 02, 2013 6:15 pm #11
> Jeffrey Weil: I think it would depend on the app. The dispersion could also be compensated for. Like Voxel did on their viewer. But like I said, it would depend on the application.

Yes, true, up to a point. Voxel used silver. However, that's not the point. The Voxel technique can work on resist, but not well. One problem is that resist is weak, the best you can get a la Kogelnik is 33.3% and the HOEs I have made on resist is about 15 - 20% (not metallised, of course). Whereas with the Voxel technique, You need a high dynamic range and fairly high brightness. For a high dynamic range, you need a large spatial frequency range (resist has a spatial frequency range of approximately 1500 l/mm). One other problem is that the sensitivity is low . So, if you're recording a large number of frames, the lower sensitvity of silver is a great advantage. That's not to say that you can't use resist for HOEs (I have actually used resist for some clients), but, again as you say, it depends on the app. I would put resist more in the display category than the HOE category.

But, your claim is that dispersion compensation can simulate Bragg selectivity. The problem with dispersion compensation is that with the usual method of creating an inverse grating, you compensate for all the wavelengths - the full spectrum "collapses" into a greyscale. The so-called "Benton angle achromat" is a form of dispersion compensation. However, if you want simply a 30nm bandwidth, say 530 - 560nm, then it's extremely difficult (but not impossible) to compensate for just the bandwidth you want and reject the rest. That's to say, diffract only the 530 - 560 along some particular line and diffract the surrounding parts of the spectrum somewhere else. The rejection is also difficult to get rid of, because the rest of the spectrum will cause an aura around the compensated wavefront. There's also the point that resist works by Raman Nath diffraction and so is not angle sensitive, all wavelengths get diffracted. Bragg selectivity can select specific wavelengths for specific angles.
