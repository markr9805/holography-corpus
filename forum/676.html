I don't venture onto this site very often these days as there seems to be almost no one out there discussing holography as anything other than an experiment in (take your choice):

- opto-mechanics

photochemistry

laser massaging

Never the less, I recently saw a video that Ian McAllister had contributed to the MIT conference and I feel obliged to respond. The paper was some fifteen minutes in length and decidedly pessimistic from start to finish. It began with a listing of failed companies and proceeded with a litany of reasons (according to Ian) as to why. Not once was there mention (other than in passing) of image composition as having any real bearing on success. In my own mind I equate holography to photography: I show with photographers and feel the two techniques are tied by a number of commonalities. In examining Ian's treatise, I had to wonder: how many talks in display photography would dismiss composition as anything more than a footnote? Are there really people out there in Holotown that still don't get it? That the reason display holography has failed is that the holography offered-up is largely trash. From my own perspective, the single most difficult obstacle in establishing my work as fine art, erotica or portraiture is the almost laughable body of work which has proceeded me: a collection of largely gratuitous technology that begs comparisons with fantasy.

I recently had the opportunity to work with one-time holographer from the Chicago Art Institute Paul Roustan in creating "Purity" an image showcasing his award winning skills in body painting as well as his models' skills in fashion design. The project as explained on Paul's website [http://www.roustanbodypainting.com](http://www.roustanbodypainting.com) was a big success and points to similar projects showcasing what can be called "impermanent art" to include makeup and tattoo artistry. As Paul will I'm sure concur: forget limited parallax; forget the need for true-color; forget difficulties in lighting; never mind about size nor price point (within reason). It's all about the imagery. If you as a holographer can produce a truly compelling image - that's all that matters to the viewing, buying public and it should be enough to satisfy you. From an artists standpoint - isn't that what really counts?

I think you have a point. Economic success for art media does not necessarily imply commercial success for the same media in retail. However, if holography had been seen as serious form of art, then perhaps the public would have accepted holography as a display medium over and above a pure art form. For example, a lot of cinema, especially early cinema, was primarily an art form. However, moving pictures impressed the public enough that some of the early protagonists saw a business opportunity to widen the scope of cinema. If early cinema had been as schlocky as some current display holography is (in Ron's view anyway), would cinema had evolved into today's commercial movie business?

My feeling is that the public's interest in holography waned because the content wasn't there. A lot of the early holography in the 70's and 80's were simply representational objects projected into space. The holographers themselves were captivated by pulse holograms of Denisyuk and Gabor, but to the public, these people meant nothing, and they were captivated by the names behind some of the holograms, also unknown to an uncaring public. To the public, a Margaret Benyon was no more forceful or captivating than a Joseph P Finkelstein. If you take these personality-based holograms out of the picture, what remains is a lot of standard objects projected out in space - a razor blade, some forks, dragons, train sets etc etc. These are impressive for the first time, but quickly lose their allure after you've seen a few of them. I think it's a bit like those 3D movies in the 50's, where you had spears and axes thrown at you. In the beginning the public loved all that, but pretty soon they got tired of it.

I think that Ron O's point, which I agree with, is that if the imagery had evolved into something a little more sophisticated, a little more of an art form, then the public would have accepted the medium morphing into a commercial medium. However, right now, holography has retreated into even more of a representational form. We now see cats, skulls etc and only other holographers are thrilled by all this. The public sees far more impressive 3D on their monitors, laptops and in the movies. I think that's why the public are taking to "holograms" which are basically projections. They expected, and were promised, that holography would be an impressive form of 3D imagery, they don't see 4x5 plates with green/orange images as impressive, but they do see these giant 3D projections as impressive. If some promoter says that these are "holograms", the public is far more apt to believe that they are holograms, as opposed to green cats and orange skulls on a 4x5.

lobaz Wed Feb 13, 2013 2:45 pm #14
Hi,

I have a different view on the subject.

Let us compare holography and photography. Photography was invented, say, in 1820's and it took about 50 years to evolve somehow, and maybe another 50 years to become really widespread. In the beginning, only technical geeks were interested. Then people began to use photography to document - faces, landscapes, life. Photography was introduced in technical use. And many many years later (not before 1900) it was widely recognized as a form of fine art. Note that it took so long and photography had no direct competitor, just traditional paintings.

Look at photography now. Majority of photographs taken are for saving memories, family photos of people and places. Large amount of photography is used in news and advertisement. Photography has many technical uses. And somewhere far far away is a small group of photographs as an art. Photography would never evolve to the current state by relying on its artistic use.

I think we should do the same with holography. Everyone here says that most of the holograms are just plain images of objects. Why not? Holograms, even single color, can be so realistic that any other technology is a clear loser. Imagine for example a catalogue of expensive jewellery for the best custemers. No photograph can express a diamond sparkling effect. A student of medicine would appreciate images of the bones etc. in a full 3-D form. Did you try to understand for example anatomy of the inner ear from flat images? A biologist could use holograms of insects to study them. I this case admit that macro photographs are useful as well but why not to have both images: a true size hologram and magnified details in photographic form.

We do not need to capture real objects. With the help of current 3-D printers we can print whatever object and make hologram of it. A student of crystallography would really appreciate to see molecular structures of minerals in a 3-D form. A student of medicine would appreciate to see structures reconstructed from CT scan in 3-D form. And so on. Yes, it is possible today to print 3-D objects for personal use, e.g. for study, but holograms are much more compact to store.

The limiting factor here is - of course - the price. A master hologram has to be shot and replicated in relatively small run, say 1000 pieces. Why? The world of printing industry is shifting more and more to small runs to keep stock low.

I have little experience with holography so I cannot answer the basic question. Is it possible to prepare an object, shoot a master, create 1000 copies say 4"x5" on a material that can be included in a book, say photopolymer film - and ask the customer less than 100 USD (i.e. 10 cents per hologram)? If not, why not? Remember that running offset machine is highly demanding work as well but offset print prices are so low today. Personally, I believe it could be done but I cannot prove it. What do you think?

Petr

You make the point that photography in the 19th century was mainly for documentation. Then, in the early 20th century it became a form of fine art. True. However, photography did not need special viewing circumstances. Anyone could see a photograph. No need for reconstruction beams, no need to make sure that the reconstruction beam be as close to the original reference beam, no need to ensure no aberrations. So, the display of photographs immediately grabbed the attention of a lot of people because viewing was so easy. Even today, the competition for holograms are lenticulars: full colour, no special lighting and cheap. The problem with holography, especially in the areas you cite - jewellery, images of bones, molecular structures etc - is that if you light a hologram badly, the bones are distorted and the diamond does not sparkle. In addition, only a few viewers can see the hologram at any one time, usually not more than two. Holograms have got to be passed around an audience and lit by a strong light. Lenticulars can do exactly what you describe with no special lighting and in any colour you like, real colour as well as artificial colour. Medical holograms are being used by the medical field, especially the holograms of Holorad (previously Voxel), so such a thing is possible, but no art will come from it. Hi, Dinesh,

I agree with you in most points. Today, there are alternatives to holograms, such as lenticulars, that are cheaper and have some other advantages. On the other hand, I have never seen anyone to be fooled with lenticulars. I have a small collection of holograms for educational purposes, and one of them fools almost anyone - a 4"x5" tabletop single beam Denisyuk hologram of jewels. This hologram lies on a black box and a lot of people believed that the jewels are actually below the glass surface. The hologram is single colour, the quality is inferior (many holographers would throw it away due to uneven drying), but still - the people are very surprised when I show them is is just an illusion.

The holograms I am talking about are not meant for wide audience, such as paintings. When studying a book, there is often just one reader, so hologram suitable for one viewer is perfectly OK. When studying, the reader is prepared for some degree of discomfort, for example a big format book has to lie in front of you on a table and has to be carefully illuminated if it is printed on a glossy paper. A "book" with holograms would require something similar; yes, there is some discomfort you are talking about (carefully set illumination etc.) but I think it is not so terrible.

The problem remaining is the price. Yes, I know that mastering embossed holograms is expensive and it is affordable in a large quantity only. The question is: is there any fundamental problem why it has to be expensive? I don't think so. You don't need any expensive materials for production such as radium or gold. Yes, optical equipment is not cheap, but production printing machines cost millions of USD as well. OK, the staff has to be skilled, but a print machine operator has to be very skilled as well. Well, maybe embossing has some hard limit that cannot be avoided. So, is there any other technology for production hologram copying? I know about photopolymer H1-H2 optical transfer only; is there anything else? Is it possible to make affordable runs of 4"x5" photopolymer holograms?

Now it is a deadlock problem. Holograms are expensive today => no one really wants to buy them. There is no market for holography => technology cannot evolve and become as cheap as traditional print. Can we break this deadlock?

You're right. You don't need expensive materials for production. But, the industry is very conservative. In such a situation, mass production holographers use photoresist. There are only one or two companies that make photoresist and they charge quite a lot for it. Roughly, I believe it's about 100 dollars for an 8x10in (20 x 25cm) and you probably need about 5 plates for any job (for tests etc). Dinesh, the reason no one wanted to switch to dcg has nothing to do with them being conservative. It's much more practical.

There is NOTHING better about dcg in a production environment. It's just as slow but it would require totally changing their existing method and equipment for no advantage. In fact, it's worse then resist. I bet the results on the final foil would be too. Casting is not as good as metalization and the whole DCG surface relief process is experimental. Resist is proven. Why switch for no advantage?

Photoresist is made to very very high, exacting standards. The coated plated don't change much for a year on the shelf. DCG has NO consistency when compared to resist. Everyday the plate changes when stored and coating that material is much harder then resist. After a short time the plates are no longer even good.

Plus, the humidity changes all the time and so does the performance. Sure, make your big production lab totally climate controlled and use chart recorders to watch for trends, etc...All so you can try to match the consistency of resist.

The cost of resist is meaningless in embossed production. The cost for a master is nothing compared to the embossed run and it's value. Most companies nowadays give the master away for free, even if they have hired someone from outside that company like myself to master it and they're charged.

Hello Lobaz,

Sure it's possible, and many have done it. All have gone out of biz. Polaroid, Dupont, Krystal and a British silver firm I can't remember the name of right now. Probably more that I don't even know of.

The problem with those machines and their markets are a few. One, it's expensive. The cost of the film or photopolymer is at least 10 cents per sq. inch. That price is the raw material, it doesn't include processing or making the masters. It might not sound like a lot but it is. Embossed is less then a penny per sq inch for the final product, often much less.

Next is speed, very few clients want 500 copies of something. They either want only a few, or a million. No optically recording mass production machine could ever do that many. Next is chemistry and consistency. Very hard to do with film holograms. Embossed, other then the master, is not optically produced. Once you have that machine set up and running the holograms all come out the same. Very important to advertising, promotion and security clients.

Forget using any film based material for security, so those markets are out. Lets say you want to make bank checks. If you use hot stamping foil not only do you have a practical way to apply them quickly on a web but they have no apparent height. Film is thick. If you take a piece of film that's been laminated to a check you could just cut it out with some scissors and re-laminate it down to a new check. No one would notice the slight bit of extra height.

If you did the same with hot stamping foil you would see it immediately.

And the last problem. Applying the film holograms to the final product. There's no 20 thousand foot rolls of optical recording material, and their never will be. Too hard to do in a practical real world condition. Wide web material is embossed up to 60 inches wide, some even more. It can then be quickly cut down to many, multi thousand foot rolls. Every splice on a big job slows everyone down, the embosser, the die cutter, the adhesive coating machines, sequential numbering, etc...All the way down the line.

So, even if you could make many film holograms, producing and applying them would be a nightmare....and cost prohibitive.

When you start to produce hundreds of thousands of images you have to deal with many problems other then holography.

Hi Petr

Jeff is right concerning making and applying film hologram to products. I also agree that machines to mass replicate holograms have not been successful so far, and there have been many of them.

However, as I understand your question/point, you are asking if it's possible to make free-standing holograms, film or glass, as an H1/H2 transfer? In other words, these holograms are not attached to any product as a label (for example), but something you might see on a shelf or on a counter in a bank, or on the desk of a receptionist of an office? So, for example, if Bank X has a promotion for car loans, all the branches of the bank will have a hologram (perhaps 4x5 on glass) of a car with dollar signs floating in front and this will be put in a frame and be placed on the bank counter. In this situation, there may be say 200 branches of the bank in Southern California, so they want 200 hologram.

> lobaz: Make e.g. glass silver halide or DCG master and then - using quite simple machine - make common H1-H2 transfers to a roll of a suitable film? The mastering facility should be equipped with setup for taking masters of objects up to 3", so it should take about 30 minutes to make H1. Is it possible?

Yes, this is possible, depending on laser, quantity, form and style. You can make the master in perhaps one day (depending on the complexity of the H1). Now, at this point, you need to choose medium, size and material. There are three choices for material: glass, plastic or film. There are (usually) three choices for medium: polymer, silver or dcg. Size will only be affected in terms of loading the plate holder, let's assume 4x5.

If the material needs only to be exposed, with almost no processing (polymer), then, typically, polymer takes an exposure of about 20mJ. In our case, for example, we have a 5W all lines Argon with about 450 mW at 488nm. Let's assume we use this. Then, again typically, we'll get about 0.5mW at the H2 plate, which means about a 50 second exposure. If there is a minimum development process - say a minute of processing - then you can make a hologram every 2 minutes. Take in time to load new plates and settling time, this means about 10 H2s every hour. In one day (8 hours) you can make 80 H2's.

If we're using silver, then typical exposure for silver is about 200 uW, so exposure is down to about half a second. Typically, development takes about 10 minutes, so you're down to 5 holograms an hour, or 40 holograms a day.

If you're shooting on dcg, then exposure is about 50 mJ, so exposure is about 100 seconds and development is about 15 minutes, so you're down to 3 holograms an hour. However, dcg holograms have to be laminated, which is a slow process. So, with dcg, you're down to about 10 a day.

There are other methods. Holorad transfers MRI data from computer to hologram in an automated process. They receives the MRI data from the hospital, load the data into a computer, which is connected to the laser table and to exposure devices, and simply turn it "on". The computer runs through the process of transferring the data to the table and shooting the hologram. At the end of the shoot, you have a roll of film. Now, they place the roll onto a spool in an automatic film processor (commonly used by photographic houses for mass processing of standard film), turns the processor "on" and the processor processes the entire roll, which they ships back to the hospital. In this way, they can make several hundred holograms a day. We were asked to make hologram coasters for a large dinner with 400 attendees. We had one month. Assuming a 90% success rate, this meant 450 holograms in 20 days, actually the success rate was slightly worse than this, so we had to make 500 holograms in 20 days. It was obvious that we could not make one, develop one, seal one and so on. We made a cradle that could hold 6 plates, we shot 6 plates one after the other, put all 6 into the cradle and transferred the cradle through all the development process; this allowed us to shoot and develop 6 holograms faster than we would normally have done. There are some holographer (well, one holographer) who contact copies by scanning with a raw beam. That is, he makes the first H2, then lays this on top of fresh unexposed film and raster scans with a raw laser beam. This technique is much faster than standard contact copying.

So, technically, it is possible to make hundreds of holograms a week. Whether advertisers would accept these is another problem. My experience is that advertisers don't want to risk their clients' money on an untried technique when they know that traditional methods actually work quite well.

The point is that we scientists and tech folk simply don't see "art" as artists see it. Some people here know that for a few years now, I've asked "what is art" (ad nauseam, to some!). What I'm trying to say is that an image, by and for itself is not art, as far as I can see. Simply making a little white mouse with a big nose, even if technically perfect, is not what I think is conceived of as "art". A lot of the time people make holograms of concrete objects, and then call that "art". I think it is no more "art" than a stick drawing I may make of a human figure. I'm not very good at drawing, so I probably can't draw human faces or figures, but I can draw stick figures. However, if I draw a stick figure and a recognised well known artist draws a stick figure, the well known artist places his/her stick figure in a context that evokes something, or creates narrative around the stick figure - something my stick figure does not do. The point is: in both cases, no drawing skill is involved, the artist has also drawn a crude, simple figure. But, the context of the crude simple drawing is the art, not just the stick figure.

In a similar vein, the ability to shoot a near-perfect little white mouse with a big nose is not art per se. An artists may make a noisy hologram, but the noise becomes part of the art. You can simply shoot a set of interlaced cubes, some random curves, even points of light, but the context creates the art, not just the image. Heck, you can take a glass rod, throw it into a fire for a little while till it distorts and perhaps melts a little. Then you can wrap aluminium foil around the twisted, bent glass rod and shoot it and you have art - noise and all!

I think this is the point Ron is making. The display holographers are trying to create exact replicas of real-world objects, and I think that, without any context or meaning, or narrative an exact real-world replica of a lizard, or a skull is dull and lifeless. It may be a wonderful thing to test your skill as a holographer, but I think that Ron is saying that it does not captivate the public.

Let me relate a story. Back in 1982, I was working with Peter Miller on pseudo-colour holograms (Peter had just introduced the technique in Britain). This technique involves dunking the plate in trieth, then squeegying the emulsion side, then dabbing the glass side with a paper towel. Peter always insisted I work in total dark, so all this had to be done by touch (yes, and licking the plate to tell which side the emulsion was on!). Anyway, one time I dried the wrong (emulsion) side with the paper towel. As a result, the entire hologram had the pock-marked pattern of the paper towel superimposed on the image. I was just about to throw it out when Paul Newman, a real, honest-to-god artist, grabbed it from the rubbish bin and asked if he could have it. "But", I said, "it's ruined. It's got these bloody great pock marks all over!" Paul replied, "But it looks great! It's art!"

What Paul really meant to say in the heat of his enthusiasm was..."But it looks great! I could turn that into art." And what he would do to turn it into art would be to change its reference from being a technically spoilt hologram to something that he would want to be judged with aesthetic references. This is called appropriated art, a corner stone Duchamp's line of thinking; take something out of context and give it a new identity and frame of reference. Yes it's the same hologram but attached to it are a new set of interpretations.

Senario #1 It's a bicycle wheel, look how well it is made, how well it functions, how people appreciate its construction and the way it obeys the laws of physics. Everybody clap hands.

Senario #2 It's the same bicycle wheel but in an art gallery (but it could equally be in my lounge room) and I have now decided that I want people to admire it as an artwork: I want you to notice the beautiful symmetry of those spokes, the optical patterns generated when it rotates, the tonal shadows it displays on the floor, its cold mechanical nature reflecting human ingenuity and our preoccupation with transport and human mobility, its inorganic touch and smooth texture...I want you to look at it in a way you most probably would not, I want you to discover a new bicycle wheel other than the one you take for granted as a device that helps you get from A to B.
