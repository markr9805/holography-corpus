walschuler Mon Jun 11, 2012 11:21 am #8
I too have done many of the objects listed above. Very light ones, like feathers, are

problematic if there are any air currents. I have had students succeed with light objects.

For example, one who did beautiful models of Chinese junks built of toothpicks and paper.

If the object is moist, it is also problematic.If the object has a very different moisture content

than the air, it may shrink or swell as they equillibrate, causing motion to occur, though this is rare.

My setup, which is for class, puts the object in contact with (sitting on top of) the emulsion

in most shots. In that case, if the object is moist enough, the part in contact will be the center of

water-induced swelling, and a spectrum ring will be left in the image after development.

It will be red at the outer edge and bluer as you go to the contact point. If the object is

very damp, the center will be black, where over-swelling shifts the image into the

invisible ultraviolet. This is TEA-less pseudo-color, rather neat, but not easy to control.

The key to using glue, putty etc. to anchor an object (short term) is to NOT have a layer of glue between the

object and base, but to have the object in direct contact with the base with adhesive used only

around the edges to keep it from sliding around. For glue, if you want the best mechanical bond and can

wait for a few hours, then just glue it the obvious way. But it is always best to avoid having the object floating

on a layer of Blu-Tac putty etc. That will never stabilize. Even so, I have committed this and worse

sins against stability with no bad effects. In a stable environment you can get away with insanely flimsy setups.
