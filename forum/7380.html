Hans Sun Jun 22, 2008 7:44 pm #12
I am a member of a telescope makers club here in Holland. They regularly make parabolic mirrors with diameters up to 50cm and they have their own equipment for vacuum depositing aluminum + silicon oxide layers. I know some people have experimented with spin techniques and epoxy. But unfortunately unsuccessful. I think they had trouble with ripples on the surface due to perhaps the rotational speed not being constant enough.

But it is not very difficult at all to make a glass mirror. Especially a spherical one because you don't have to bother with the lengthy process of figuring the mirror to a parabolic shape after polishing. I have always wondered if a spherical mirror surface would be good enough for holography especially since we are using the mirrors off axis.

Also, I wonder if a holography mirror needs to be accurate to 1/8 of a wavelength because we are shooting off axis anyway. Perhaps, as long as the polish is good enough a lesser accurate mirror would perform equally well for our purposes.

(I became a member of this club with the intent to make my own mirror and will start doing so soon.)

walschuler Thu Jul 03, 2008 5:47 pm #14
Oh, my....This takes me back to my roots!

I made my first telescope when I was in 8th grade. I ground the pyrex mirror blank to the proper radius, then fine ground it through successively finer abrasive grades, then polished it with rouge on a pitch lap tool. It was easy to maintain its spherical surface shape, as Hans and Colin have alluded. Then I changed the polishing stroke I was using to convert the shape to a parabola. The shape was tested using Foucault's knife edge test. This "figuring" task was delicate and took more than one try. There is a quite excellent literature for amateur telescope making, including books by Thompson, Texereau and a 3 volume set from Scientific American that has all sorts of information on optics, mounts, coatings, etc., though they are dated, as not having anything on interference coatings, lasers, laser alignment, or HOEs. I don't know the current best references.

There is a specialised astronomical instrument for wide field astro photos called the Schmidt Camera, invented in the 1930s. In its original form it had a spherical mirror (easy to make) with a correcting lens in front (hard to make) at the center of curvature. The lens was aspherical, with a front surface shape in cross section like the section of a bell with a turned up rim, and the back was flat. I started a simplified version which would have been just a spherical mirror with an aperture stop at the center of curvature. This works nearly as well as the original. The image is formed on a spherical focal surface so all the large astro Schmidt Cameras have vacuum plate holders to conform the plates to their curved focal surfaces. The largest is 48'' in diameter, is at Mt. Palomar, and is, I believe still, in use. It gives superb images over a multi-degree-wide field.

Roger Angell at the University of Arizona pioneered really large spun-cast glass Astronomical mirrors some years ago. Some are in use. There is at least one recent spun-mercury mirror telescope. It must rest horizontal, and thus sees only the sky in a narrow patch overhead, and can track objects only by moving the receiver at its focus, and this only for a short period of time. But you can make really large parabolic mirrors this way for very little money compared to doing them in glass or quartz or Zerodur.

Most new huge telescopes these days are made of many facets, each individually controlled in real time by a computer fed by an image sensor to create a combined nearly perfect image. These are called "rubber mirrors" in the trade even though they are glass. The control allows them to correct the incoming astronomical wavefronts on the fly, "de-twinkle" the stars, and make image resolution about 80% as good as from orbit above the atmosphere. Some day this technology will make it to amateur telescopes...
