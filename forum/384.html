Johnfp Thu Nov 03, 2011 5:12 am #5
Good morning Tony,

First, it is easy to check polarization with some polarized sunglasses or even a piece of glass. Withe the first, rotate the sunglasses till little or no light comes out. Then rotate in the other beam and insure when little or no light comes out, the glasses are in the same orientation. WIth the latter, the glass, just tilt it and look at the reflection till it goes dim, do the same in the other beam and make sure the tilt is in the same axis (top to bottom, or side to side).

The most valuable tool a holographer can have is his Michaelson Interferometer. You don't need to disturb your existing set up as long as you have a few extra mirrors available (2 mirrors, a beam splitter - even a piece of glass, and a lens). In your case, I would set up the following interferometer at equal path lengths, study them, then change the path lengths and gradually and see what happens.

You do know in your case, if the beams are compatible and you are going to use both of them, then the laser itself is the beam splitter. With that I mean, you have to forget all that stuff about the laser can be off the table. As soon as the 2 beams exit the laser, they have to stay relative to one another. So the laser would have to be on the table and then possibly a set up like this. Definitely worth time and trouble for quite a bit more mW in my opinion. In single beams, to have that extra light would really brighten the holograms.

When you make a hologram and it doesn't turn out, or one aspect - in your case the brightness - doesn't turn out, there may be a number of failure modes, one of which may be the fringe stability. There doesn't need to be any delay. The diagram of the Mach Zender I got was for a specific application involving determining the shifting of fringes due to phase mismatch. Dinesh Sun Nov 06, 2011 8:07 pm #17
> Tony: Was the fan on the laser head a problem??I've heard of some who switch it off during an exposure but I'd rather not.

It's impossible to make a definite statement because it depends on a lot of factors. If you're in a noisy area, then the small effect of the fan may be swamped by the much larger sources of noise in your locale. If you're in a quiet area, the small effect of the fan may not matter. If the noise in your area is just right on the edge of acceptability, removing the fan may help. Remember the fan is there for a reason, so you need to make a judgment of the consequences of removing the fan as opposed to the instability caused by the fan for your particular surroundings. If the laser overheats, you may lose coherence and/or power, so if you're on the edge of coherence you have to balance the effect of instability against the effect of incoherence. It also depends on your exposure. The longer the exposure, the greater the stability requirement.

I would make that judgment by looking at an interferogram with and without the fan to see the difference over a period roughly equal to your exposure. Also is it possible to clamp the laser? What vibration mode is the fan transferring to the laser?
