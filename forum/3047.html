After the months of researches the formula of new SAFE developer for PFG-03m emulsion has been found . This new developer was named as "Open Developer" (OD-1), like open-source operation system Linux. Let everybody improve this developer!

Author of the formula - Sergey Vorobyov. Copyright (c) 2005. Holography.Ru.

OD-1 formula:

Metol 2 g.

Sodium sulfite, anhyd. 25 g.

Hydroquinone 5 g.

Borax cryst. 2 g.

Sodium thiosulphate 6 g.

Water to 1 l

jeff-blyth Sat Jan 22, 2005 2:58 am #13
Personally I think physical developers like this are a can of worms. You are relying on a fine balance between getting the silver bromide to develop in the bright fringes and neighboring dark fringe silver bromide to "move over" one fringe width by going into solution and depositing itself as silver onto the developing silver in the bright fringe. After a few plates have gone through you may notice silver depositing itself around your dish ,--that represents a loss of your precious diffracting medium. If you get OK results with one batch of film/plates then a different batch of different hardness can give you a NOT OK result because that balance is upset, to say nothing of the importance of keeping temperature the same each time and the degree of agitation. No thank you.

If you want the brightest non-red holograms shot with a red laser you cannot beat a TEA preswelling followed by a chemical only dev with no "physical dev" constituents like thiosulfate, thiocyanate and even common or garden sulfite (you keep ariel oxygen out by using the floating dish method instead of using damned sulfite) . Hi Colin!

If you take 2 typical plastic photographers dishes they are always close-fitting when the lower one is only about 1/4 full of developer. The top one floats with no air gap other than a very narrow one around the sides. So you stick your plate in and agitate by rocking the top dish and lifting it like a hinge to inspect and remove plate. The remarkable thing is that if you just leave this system undisturbed for a few days, the oxygen only gets in and darkerns the developer just around the edges between the dishes, the bulk of the dev is fine still. Experiments show that this appears to be due the density of oxidized developer being slightly less than fresh developer. So dead developer just sits there on top in a very narrow band between the 2 dishes. {This is nicely proved if you use an unusual developer containing a fair proportion of alcohol , then the oxidized dev is more dense than the unoxidised and the whole lot goes off

under an hour inspite of the floating dish method.)Jeff

Ed Wesly Tue Jan 25, 2005 6:49 pm #20
Metol 2 g.

Sodium sulfite, anhyd. 25 g.

Hydroquinone 5 g.

Borax cryst. 2 g.

Sodium thiosulphate 6 g.

Water to 1 l

Metol and hydroquinone are the developing agents for this brew. They are long organic chains that are looking for silver halides with developable specks and will change the exposed crystals into silver metal.

Sodium Sulfite can be used as a preservative, by putting a cap on the oxygen floating around in the water, going from the SO3 sulfite to the SO4 sulfate form. It also has some silver halide solvent capability, although at this concentration it may not be its main function.

Borax is the alkali, or activator. It provides the energy for the developer to search and develop.

Sodium thiosulphate is a fixing agent. It dissolves silver halides, and normally is used to remove any remaining unexposed and undeveloped grains in photographic emulsions. But in this developer it gets the AgX crystals into solution as Jeff B has remarked and hopefully deposits them onto developing sites.

It looks like the thiosulfate level may be a tad on the high side, as in the side by side comparison of GP3 and OD in the original post the latter seems to have shrunk a bit to the orange side. I can't wait to give it a try!

As far as the other question:

There are two distinct families of bleaches used in holography; rehalogenating and reversal, aka silver solvent.

Rehalogenating bleaches can be used with or without fixing. As their name suggests, they change the developed black silver back into a silver halide. When the holo plate is developed, fixed, then bleached in a rehalogenating formula, the silver halide in the bright fringes are first developed, then the fixing step dissolves out the silver halides in the dim fringe areas, leaving the bright and dark fringes in the hologram represented by black silver in the bright and empty gelatin in the dim. Bleaching in a rehalogenating bleach at this stage will change the developed silver into transparent silver halides, typically Ag Br. Now the representation of the bright fringes is with silver bromide, the dim fringes empty gelatin.

If the fixing step is omitted, the developed hologram has black silver in the bright fringes, and when it is imersed in the rehalogenating bleach the black silver is changed back to AgBr, with the AgBr remaining in the dim fringes "swimming" over to join them. This promotes grain growth, and is why plates developed in this scheme look "snowier" than when they come out of the box. (It may be that the "swimming" is from bright fringe to dim, but I forgot if that question was ever adequately resolved.)

The silver solvent or reversal bleaches take the developed silver grains and disssolve them. In this type of hologram the bright fringes are represented by the gelatin matrix, and the dim fringe areas have the original population of light-sensitive crystals remaining. Holograms processed this way are usually a bit less noisy, albeit at the expense of brightness.

You can tell if a bleach formula is rehalogenating or reversal (that term comes from the role it has in black and white photographic processing; it is the key step that directly turns an exposed film into a positive, not a negative, so it reverses the reversal of the negative - positive process) from its ingredients list. The rehalogenators will have some sort of a salt like potassium bromide in it. Both types will have an oxidizer of some sort, like p-benzoquinone, ferric EDTA, potassium dichromate, etc. The PBU series of bleaches oxidize a developing agent into an oxidizer for bleaching purposes. Both types will have an acid to propel the reaction.

For instance, the typical Pyrochrome bleach is composed of

4 g potassium dichromate

4 ml sulfuric acid

1 liter H20

You can see the black stuff go into solution with this one!

The addition of 30 g potassium bromide to this bath (without the bath ever being used!) will change this into a rehalogenating bleach.

Typically a rehalogenating bleach without fixing (and no TEA-ing!) will yield a color close to the laser wavelength. I have replaced the hologram back into the original plateholder with a single beam reflection and generated real time interferometric fringes thanks to the low shrinkage!

Reversal bleaches will shrink the emulsion so that replay color of reflection holos are shorter wavelegth shifted. With He-Ne's you can go from orangey-red to green by increasing the exposure before the image disappears!
