Kaveh Tue Mar 27, 2007 9:58 pm #3
Hi Joe

Color is a complex subject. The choice of the optimum wavelengths is a contentious issue. You only need to look at different papers to see that there is no one answer. All I can give you are my views, which have not changed over the last 20 years or so!

I don't think that going higher than 610 is going to have a strong effect on color rendition. Obviously there are no practical laser wavelengths around 610, so you can pick 633 or 647. Of course the higher you go, the lower the [luminous efficiency](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image:CIE_1931_Luminosity.png), or apparent brightness of the light, so you will need more of it in proportion to the blue and the green. I think this is the main disadvantage when going higher.

The green and blue, on the other hand, can have bad effects on color rendering index or [CRI](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_rendering_index). 532 nm is pretty good, but 514 is bad. And anything around 488 is very nasty for CRI.

> BobH: I don't think that going higher than 610 is going to have a strong effect on color rendition.You'vegotto be kidding! 633nm looks orange when compared to 647nm. I can't imagine rendering a red delicious apple with 610nm as my deepest red!

Ah... "compared to 647" it certainly looks orange. But were you looking at this in a darkened room where only the two wavelengths were illuminating? In that case, the eye/brain doesn't have a "normal" point of reference and because the very deep red is present, sees the 610 as orange. (The eye/brain is extremely good at comparison of colors, but useless at absolute measurement of them.) This is a very unnatural situation, and if you were to switch the lights on and have some normal objects around, then suddenly you would see 610 as a deep red, and 647 as a deeper red.

I think you will find that the deepest red apple has a dominant color that is under 610, so you do not need a red deeper than that. The key data here is that of [Wintrigham](http://xplqa.ieee.org/Xplore/login.jsp?url=/iel5/10933/4050507/04050515.pdf?isnumber=4050507&prod=JNL&arnumber=4050515&arSt=1135&ared=1172&arAuthor=Wintringham%2C+W.T.) in 1951, which I cited in [1989](http://ieeexplore.ieee.org/Xplore/login.jsp?url=/iel3/1153/1870/00051790.pdf?arnumber=51790). It was cited later by others in color holography, including Hans Bjelkhagen.

Here is the important diagram that I redrew from Wintringham's paper:

![Image](http://homepage.mac.com/kaveh1000/wintringham.jpg)

The shaded area represents the limit of colors known to exist in nature. So the boundary is the most saturated colors known. (Later Pointer extended this to newer dyes made in the lab.)

So you can see that a deep red apple would fall somewhere in the shaded area, and easily covered by 610nm.

The important point about the CIE diagram is that the horseshoe shape represents the colors that the eye can distinguish, not the colors that exist in nature, or even man-made objects. Most of these lie well away from the boundary of the curve, and it is only these we need to represent.

I think I had written a little tract on color holographic exposures as I had practiced them at Lake Forest College early '90's for this forum. (It may have gotten deleted during the forum wrecking, a reminder to please don't let those two back here!)

We used Argons at 476 and 514, and a 125 He-Ne at 633. This is because this was what was available. Prior to that, Hans Bj and V.C. Sainov were using Bulgarian plates, and for some silly reason they were using 488 for the blue, not a wise choice, as that corner of the CIE triangle would not be able to give a decent Navy Blue like 476 could. And of course, 458 would have been a little too scattery.

(Footnote on an experiment I would have liked to try: looking at a line connecting 488 and 633 on the CIE, it passes right through equal energy white! That would be a very cool effect, a white reflection hologram, with cyan and red accents!) (I thing cyan would be a more appropo name for 488 than blue, since it's at that blue/breen border)

Exposures were on the then brand new duPont panchromatic polymer, although it needed like hundreds of milli, not micro, Joules in the red. I did a holo of a Russian Nesting Doll that Yuri D. himself gave to my first born, about the size of a 35 mm slide with that material.

What was more effective was a sandwich of an Agfa plate for the red and blue/green duPont laminated on top. But what also worked (surprisingly!) were back lit objects in a two beam reflection set up, with all three exposures on Agfa 8E75HD! Even better were sanwiches of 8E75HD for red and the blue and green on 8E56HD AHI, or 8E75HD and Ilford blue/green.

There were some left over Bulgarian HP-490 plates, and they gave the best results for the blue green. (Bulgarian HP-650 I never got to work!) These I developed in CWC2, against the then conventional wisdom that this type of processing would never work on Soviet style ultra-fine grain materials.

If I were to do it from scratch, I'd still use He-Ne 633, unless there are newer and cheaper and more powerful reds. I still think of 610 as being orange! The closest I've ever seen to this color was from a tunable He-Ne and who knows if it's still being produced, by PMS (Particle Measurement Systems). And there definitely is a difference between it and 633, except that laser didn't allow simultaneous lasing of all the wavelengths. Curiously, the 594 that could be coaxed out of it looked more orangey than yellow. But 543 was real green!

The 476 blue I like better than deeper ones because scatter is going to be the limiting factor down there. Yeah, 532 or 528 (a low gain argon line) would be better for green, but it always comes down to what you can get your hands on.

Oh boy, the more things change the more they remain the same.

Also I am a 3 lambda man. The recording materials will be taxed enough with three exposures, why burden them with a fourth? Plus how do you juggle exposures to color tune a system like that?

I just can't stop my fingers! I thought the origin of the Wintringham Gamut was a survey of the naturally occurring colors of everyday life to be used by early color TV engineers. So that's the target for that industry. Things outside of it wouldn't be too dearly missed.

There aren't too many things that I have found that emit or reflect at the yellow notch. (Sodium vapor lamps and the above mentioned laser are not so ordinary examples.) Most all the yellows we see are red/green combos.

One topic that I don't see discussed much regarding true color is the replay bandwith. (But I don't have Kaveh's paper infront of me, nor any others.) Whenever you look at a specular highlight in a reflection holo it's not monochromatic, but a discontinuous spectrum; there are some missing lines. Of course there is a peak wavelength that is very nicely constructively interfered with for the brightest peak, then its neighbors sort of fit into the volume grating, so they are present, but not as bright, then there is a black band in the spectrum due to a certain wavelengths geometry fitting in the structure and destructively interfering. You can see a cyclic variation throughout the spectra, especially in deep scenes.

So the lasers can provide the corner angles of the triangle of colors during analysis, but the replay spectrum (synthesis) will grow due to the broadening of the replay colors.

> BobH: ...am I wrong to want to include as much information as possible?

Yes

It all depends on what you need.

Hard-core audiophiles regularly claim that they can hear the difference between CD-quality audio and what most regular people would consider high data-rate mp3 compression. Most normal people with normal audio equipment would be hard-pressed to hear a difference.

The same is roughly true with holography.

Kaveh's point about comparing 633 & 650 next to each other is, imo, well taken. Look at 633 by itself in a dark room pointed at a white surface and it looks (to me anyway) quite red. Put a 650 spot next to it and suddenly 633 looks quite orange.

It's all relative to a very large degree.

A critical question is does it really matter? Does your choice of color affect the end-result to any degree that is remotely important to your target audience? If your target audience is you, (meaning the creator of the work) then only your opinion matters. If you can only see differences in output when comparing two holograms made using difference source frequencies, side by side then my opinion is that it doesn't matter unless the final holograms will also be compared that way. If those same holograms can be displayed in different rooms and nobody notices that there's a difference between them, then I'd have to say that the source lasers don't make a bit of difference.

To me, the more important question is "does the final result look reasonable?" If the viewer has no reason to think that the colors in the hologram weren't the original colors of the objects, then I'd have to say that the there's no good reason to put additional work into getting more "accurate" color rendition. Unless you're doing non-display work it's all subjective anyway.

Colin asked:

> Is resist less sensitive to scatter?

Scatter comes from an inhomogenous medium. Silver halide materials have the roughly 2.5 index of refraction silver halide crystals embedded in the 1.5 refractive index gelatin. Big delta n, lots of scatter. (Of course size, concentration, and wavelength play a part in the Rayleigh scatter equation.)

On the other hand, photo resist is pretty homogenous once the solvent evaporates, so scatter is slight or non-existent.

To say that I'm in no position to argue with Thornton is an understatement. Here are the two papers if anyone wishes to find them:

Luminosity and Color-Rendering Capability of White Light, W.A. Thornton (Westinghouse Fluorescent and Vapor Lamp Divisions, Bloomfield, New Jersey), Journal of the Optical Society of America, Volume 61, Number 9, September 1971, pages 1155-63.

Color-Discrimination Index, W.A. Thornton, Journal of the Optical Society of America, Volume 62, Number 2, February 1972, pages 191-4.

Also, the one by Wintringham is called Color Television and Colorimetry, W.T. Wintringham (Bell Telephone Laboratories, Murray Hill, New Jersey), Proceedings of the I.R.E., Volume 39, 1951, pages 1135-72 (a long paper).

One interesting thing about Thornton's use of 450, 540, and 610 is that he said that just those 3 wavelengths would provide even better color rendition than sunlight. This is because sunlight contains certain wavelengths around 500 and 580 which have a stongly adverse effect on the CRI.

He also made the interesting comment on page 1157 of the first paper: "....Figure 2 indicates that there are three wavelength regions that can only be labeled as disastrous for the CRI, namely, wavelengths near 495, 575, and beyond about 640 nm."

And from the summary:

"....For CRI, the three most effective wavelengths in white light of any color are near 450, 540, and 610 nm. It is established here, perhaps for the first time, that three wavelengths should be predominant in white light from which high CRI and high luminosity are both desired, and it is shown that white light composed of only these three wavelengths is very difficult to excel in those characteristics. Conversely, composition of white light should avoid wavelengths near 500 and 580 nm; not only are those wavelengths ineffective, but they are actually harmful since, for example, removing a slice of light near 500 or 580 nm from the SPD of a cool-white fluorescent lamp causes the CRI to rise sharply."
