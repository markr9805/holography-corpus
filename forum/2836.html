[quote:869af293a0]I think I see 4 holograms being produced this way.[/quote:869af293a0]

Actually, there are more. You have a total of four beams, the two "real" ones and the two reflections arising from them, call these b_1, b_2, b_3 and b_4. Now you can have a hologram by cobining b_1 and b_2 or b_1 and b_3 or b_2 and b_4 etc. Essentially, the problem is one of combinations. In how many ways can you combine 2 objects (beams) out of 4. The answer is then:

4_C_2 = 4!/2!(4-2)! = 6

However each of these 6 holograms has a conjugate (although in one of these cases, given the right situation, the image and it's conjugate are the same). Thus you now have 12 holograms. Excercise: Name the 12 holograms (clearly the 2 obvious ones are one reflection and one transmission).

By the way, I did try this about 5 years ago. You asked at the last PCG whether anyone had ever tried a combined reflection/transmission and I said I did and I'd bring it with me to Texas. I did bring it to Texas, but everyone was too busy for me to give a hologram show. This was basically the way did it, except that I had an H1 instead of a real object. The problem is that the reflection images are dim due to the ratio problems. By using a semi-reflective mirror, I got a much happier hologram. Well, OK, 12 holograms!

Dinesh Tue Oct 26, 2004 7:19 pm #9
The idea of using an "object" as a reference for another "object" is a well known technique for auto-correlation and match filters. For example, you make a hologram with an arrow as one source and a letter, say "a", as the other. Now you have a page of text. You shine a beam through a transparency of the text and where there's an "a", the hologram will take this "reference beam" and generate an "object" beam. You'll see arrows replacing all the "a's" in the text. There have been ideas of using these autocorrelation techniques for encoding data.Mostly the matching is done with Fourier transform holograms.

[quote:36aae84362]I found that specular reflection happens when the hills and valley of a material are smaller then the wavelenght of light).[/quote:36aae84362]

No, you're confusing scattering with reflection. If a material reflects light mainly or completely back along the direction from which it came, then it's called "specular" reflection. A mirror is a good specular reflector. In fact the word "specular" comes from "speculum" an early medieval material for making mitrrors. If the reflection occurs over a wide range of angles and falls off as you view it away from the specular direction it's called "Lambertian" because a guy called Lambert researched it. A flat white paint is lambertian. If you shone a spot on a mirror the spot would only reflect along the specular direction, but if you replaced the mirror with a diffusely white painted surface, it would throw light in every direction. However, the light would fall off as you viewed it away from where you'd expect specular reflection. It actually, more or less, falls off as the cos of the angle away from the specular direction.

Scattering occurs when light going through a material is thrown "off course". A light beam going through a slab of glass goes in one end and out the other. If you viewed the glass through the side you won't see anything, assuming the glass were perfectly clean. However, if the glass had very small cracks, bubbles etc inside, the light would scatter off these and you'd see the light from the side of the glass. In a dielectric substance ( non-metallic) the light is propagated through by the atoms in the substance. Due to various phase relationships, the light mostly goes staright through. However, if the atoms inside are not all the same size some of the light does not go straight through but scatters off to the sides. You can see this if you get a clean glass of water and put a drop of milk in it. Done right, the glass of milk will be colorless but when you put a drop of mil, the water goes slightly blue. This is because the drop of milk spreads throughout the glass of water and forems micro-milk particles that scatter . The blue is because Rayleigh sdattering prefers scattering blue light. A straight reflection is color independant.

Don't take it too much for granted. For instance you mentiuoned you didn't realise that NaCl ionised when dissolved in water. This is due to the charge seperation in the water molecule. Now look at the nearest elements to Oxygen: C, N and F. What do you suppose would happen if you put Nacl into the hydrides of these elements? In fact, why do you suppose the hydrides of Carbon and Nitrogen are gaseous and the hydride of Oxygen is liquid? I've discovered when reading Feynmann that ocassionaly I need to stop and ask, "What is the significance of this statement?". You can always jump right into the optics section. There he makes the statement that the usual statement of The Principle of least Time is not quite what most books state. It should actually read that light takes a path such that any nearby paths only produce a second order change in the time of flight off the light. He just leaves it at that. Try to calculate the change of light's time of flight when reflecting from a mirror where the angle of reflection is not quite the same as the angle of incidence. As a student it took some doing but sure enough, for a small change in the reflection law, you do indeed get a second order change. The point is, I recommend you have a pencil and paper handy and do some simpe calculations when you see an off-hand throw-away remark. Some of Feynmann's throw-away remarks were pretty deep.
