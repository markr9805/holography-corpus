Howdy fellas!

I've just finished creating a particularly ghetto post-swelling solution from 500 mg chewable vitamin C tablets, and I'm posting here about it in the event that anyone might have a reason to not risk dunking my developed film in this vile liquid. I ground up 20 500 mg tablets into a fine powder then dissolved them in warmed distilled water. The result was an orange goo that I stirred until only a few globs of undissolved powder remained. It had a nasty frothy appearance and I have to wonder if there might have been a bit of gelatin in the tablets (is this the Plant Origin Cellulose listed under other ingredients?)...

After dissolving this disgusting muck as best I could, I filtered the solution through a coffee filter and what remained was a yellow-tinted liquid that looks viscus but isn't.

If it doesn't coagulate into jello in my cupboard, I'm hoping to get a decent post-swell from this weird gunk. Please, if anyone knows a reason not to use this stuff, alert me before I ruin a hologram!

Thanks as always, guys.

Dinesh Fri May 06, 2011 10:30 am #4
Personally, I would cut the hologram in half, put one half in lab grade ("pure") ascorbic acid and one half in your goo. This way, you know the effect of the fillers etc. Any effect from the actual hologram would be the same for both samples. When you do the final wash, you may want to wash the goo sample in slightly warmer water. If there gelatin or some other gelatinous matter in the goo, cold water may not dissolve it out. Don't forget the effects from the coffee filter - some of the cellulose may have dissolved in your goo.

Martin Fri May 06, 2011 10:34 am #6
> Justin W: Please, if anyone knows a reason not to use this stuff, alert me before I ruin a hologram!

Using ascorbic acid for post-swelling is probably not a good idea. It may reactivate the bleached layer for light exposure or even reduce the AgX to metal silver, resulting in image darkening. Ascorbic acid actually is a pretty potent developing agent.

Ed Wesly Fri May 06, 2011 10:45 am #9
I have used Vitamin C tablets as a developer! Not a holographic one, but photographic paper, when I was under the gun to do an in-class photo-history demo. I didn't have the Dektol that I needed with me, but I did have some Pyrochrome part B for the alkali. It did make a mess dissolving at first, since there was all kinds of starches in with the ascorbic acid, but it did darken the paper, which is all I needed for the demo. Never really followed up on it as far as seeing if it could give decent results, but there is a developer called AAC that is nothing more than ascorbic acid and sodium carbonate, which is something you can find in the grocery store! I have been using Arm and Hammer Super Washing Soda, and the only thing listed in the contents is sodium carbonate. Don't know if it's mono or anhydrous, but using it in the one-shot Pyrochrome mode it really doesn't make a whole lot of difference! You could probably use the tablets for the post-developer that turns the plates red under bright light to bring up the contrast by bringing down the noise on old Agfa plates.

Ever taste the ascorbic acid from the darkroom? It is perfectly safe, however if you put just a tad on the tip of your tongue it will burn just as bad as the juice from a can of jalapeno peppers! A 500 milligram dose of Vitamin C from the apothecary weighs about 2 grams with all the starches and sugars put into it to make it digestible.

Sodium sulfite is another developer ingredient that you might be eating and unaware of it, it's a preservative for some brands of bacon and sausages. Although triethanolamine might not be good eating, its found in a lot of shampoos and shaving creams! And I forgot where I saw it, but sodium thiosulfate, fixer, or hypo (19th century chemists called it sodium hyposulfite before they got their act together) was in some list of ingredients I saw recently.

Would not the acidity promote printout? As I see it:

Ag(+) + H(+) -> Ag + <other stuff>

and, as I remember:

K = ( [Ag][other stuff])/([Ag][H(+)])

wouldn't increasing the H+ concentration drive the reaction to the right by increasing the collision rate?

Surely it would depend on factors like the local humidity and temperature? If the swelling of the gelatin is caused by a physical separation of the gelatin structure by water or H ions, then I would suspect that an increase of temperature or humidity would promote transport of the H ions out of the gelatin. So, I suspect that places of high humidity would give the swelling a better chance of survival than places of lower humidity.

Sorry, realised an error in the last post. Thinking further about it, the actual reaction is:

Br- + hf -> Br + e-

e- + Ag+ -> Ag

(H+) + e- -> H
