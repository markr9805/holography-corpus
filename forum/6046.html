MarkCavin Wed Feb 07, 2007 10:20 pm #2
The reason for the test strip is so you can determine your exposures. Mask your film so that you will only expose a small strip, then move the mask so as to expose the next small section of film. Vary your exposure times between strips then process as normal. If all goes well, at least one or more of the strips should indicate your optimum exposure period for the processing you used.

I did not find a article in the wiki, I know I have typed this up before perhaps it was before wiki days. Here is a brief description: (It would be better with photos...)

In order to find the optimum exposure and development times it is possible to make exposure tests.

There are many variations on the method but Graham Saxby's method works well if you have your film lying flat in a single beam reflection setup. (Any other setup will only take a slight variation using a moving slit or tape.) Calculate your best estimate at the proper exposure time. Place 4 coins on the film. Remove one coin after 1/4 of you estimate, remove one more coin after 1/2 of your estimate, remove one more coin at your estimate, remove the last coin at double your estimate and then shut off the exposure at 4 times your estimate.

If you use tape, you can remove tape at the proper times. This makes strips of differing exposure, if you cut the plate across the strips and develop each piece for a different time you can see the effects of exposure and developing times.

Tony Sun Feb 11, 2007 11:37 am #7
OK, time for a 'Columbo' question:

There's two ways to make a test strip. In the first, commonly described way, a mask is removed gradually after intervals of exposure, allowing greater and greater exposure on different areas of the film. In the second, which I claim is more appropriate, a mask is used to create differently exposed areas on the film, where each area is the result of a single exposure of differing times.

We're making holograms here, folks! So it is essential that the exposure be stable over time! While the first method of making a test strip might help one to arrive at the proper total exposure based on density, it will not give you any information about the proper exposure for the holographic setup (i.e. the brightest resultant hologram). This is because the act of removing the mask and then resuming the exposure will cause motion at the plate, which could come to rest at a different location. Therefore the fringes from each sub-exposure will not be aligned.

It would seem to me critical that each exposure be over a single time period where the fringes are all stable.
ErichRose Sun Feb 11, 2007 1:15 pm #8
Thanks Tony for bringing up the issue of movement between exposures. I was taught to make each exposure a fully seperate event and NOT make it cumulative. And settling between shots was critical. At least one or two test plates had a section or two that was worthless because I was rushing it.

After screwing around with one inch vertical strips I came up with my own little system: I was shooting 4x5s so I made a black cardboard mask that had one quadrant missing (~2x2.5). It was cut just a bit smaller than the plate so it would nestle in front of the plate between the edges of the plate holder, then it was tacked in place with hot glue. I started with the upper left corner, then upper right, lower right & bottom left. In other words clockwise around the plate. I usually did 1/2 the calculated exposure, 1x, 1.5x and 2x for the first test.

The beauty of the quadrants was the ease of lining up the card. I just flipped it around or rotated it. I had minimal overlap on the exposures. It also gave me a better field of view for assessing the exposure. The "one inch" strips often ended up being 3/4, 1-1/4, overlapped, a thin strip unexposed, etc. I could easily live without the fifth exposure. After a while you get a feel for how much the exposure will need to be even if one of the four isn't quite right.

I also made good use of that fine point Sharpie marker and labeled all test plates immediately. And I kept good notes. Which I am re-reading twenty years later. Just wish I was going to be working again with 8E75 so I wouldn't have to do so much over agian.

 It's a good habit to make a habit of always doing things in the same order: clockwise, left to right, top to bottom, etc. When working in the dim safe light this will make it easier to keep everything straight.

I use this object then lay the plate right on the top thus it is a very stable configuration for single beam reflection holograms. I have 3 sets of cover card. One lays on the top and covers all but one slice of cubes on either end. The second set covers all but the 2nd and 2nd to last slice of cubes and the third set covers all but the center set of cubes. So I do an exposure for the first set of cubes, the resplace the covers wtih the next set and expose, etc. The side blockers insure that each test exposure only had it's own refence and object beams interfering with no cross over light from adjacent test exposure.

This configuration allows me to not only test the best exposure time but allows me to see the differences the exposure times have with different depths. The though is that as the object moves further from the plate it reflect less light.

Ed Wesly Mon Feb 12, 2007 4:30 pm #12
As I mentioned above, check out my article on CWC2 for some info on test strips.

ErichRose is on the right track, by using a quadrant system. This takes into account the natural Gaussian fall-off of the spread laser beam, unlike the strip methods where the peripheries are seeing different flux energies at the edges compared to the centers. The quadrant captures the radially diminishing exposures, so that there is a realistic comparison of same intensities in the center of the plate but with different exposure times.

Another point to make is that the exposure times should be logarithmic, like the response of the eye. In other words, a series like 1", 2", 4", 8", 16" ... as appropriate would be good, or for finer tweaking, 1", 1.4", 2", 2.8", 4", 5.6", 8", 11", 16" ...

It's just frustrating to see some of the newbies not getting results by not using one plate as a test and just using 10" or some other number to shoot the first holo.

And the other thing to remember is to keep the developer time constant and control density with exposure! You can't reliably see density in the dark!

Thanks Ed, that was a good point about my method that I never really had put into words.

I do really like JohnFP's stacked blocks but I often found I needed to make the test specific to the subject. I can see doing a generalized object like the blocks (I'm imagining a stepped pyramid combined with my four quadrants) to get a feel for the laser/power vs emulsion/developer as a starting point or bench mark.

The other extremeley important point you made was keeping the developing time constant. Man I made that mistake a few times in my rush to make good use of my "two days of lab rental."

This all gets around to another good rule of thumb: don't stack up variables. Test one thing at a time: exposure time, developing time, chemistry, beam ratios, etc. Don't vary anymore than one of these at a time. Otherwise you very quickly start to get meaningless data.
