The replay of an object exposed with a particular kind(s) of reference beam(s) and replayed with other kinds of reference beams are very well known. There is a vast array of work going back to the 50's. Gabor himself did not consider image aberrations, since his in-line method practically had none. Leith and Upatniks considered the process as a communications analogy and so did not publish anything specific about image aberrations from an optical standpoint. However, they did determine the efect of the angle of the reference beam. I understand that as a result of this, the first inkling of the importance of film resolution became known. Agfa's 8E75 was originally an X-ray film, I'm told. This was all they had back then for high resolution film. The interesting thing about collimation is that I spent this last weekend reading Benton's notes for his course. The entire mathematical structure is based on diverging beams. The hologram itself is seen as a lens element and given the thin lens, paraxial treatment:

1/f = 1/v + 1/u

I would have argued that the extreme angles used in the notes would have invalidated the thin lens paraxial approximations. It's also interesting that some authors have directly used the "Benton" formulae for collimated references without understanding that this makes no sense! Anyway, the effects due to reference/reconstruction mismatch first were calculated using both geometric ray tracing and the physical optics methods of phase variation mostly got going in the mid 60's.

[quote:de4113b5b4]... shadowing, laser properties, film characteristics, etc. changed from different exposure time to exposure time from close to the plate to far from the plate.[/quote:de4113b5b4]

If by "shadowing" you mean the Fresnel pattern, this also is pretty well known. It;s a little complicated, you have to basically use the Cornu spiral. The variation of "laser properties" is also very well known. I think we've talked many times on the importance of coherence length, mode hopping, effects of current on diode lasers etc, etc. If you intend to change the laser with every slit, the only thing you might see as a result of the change, as opposed to the actual ability of any laser to record a hologram, is the change in fringe density at the edges. As for "film characteristics" if, again, you intend to change the film for every slit, it seems to me that you're again repeating work that's been done many years ago. The effect of recording on standard films using standard holograms has been published and covered here many, many times. Of course, the various films used must match the laser wavelengths and that too has been covered. The sensitivity of PFG-01 vs the sensitivity of VRP for instance. If you intend to change the film and the laser for every slit it seems that you've effectively made a series of different holograms, all with well known properties. As for exposure times, again it's a well published subject and we've covered it pretty extensively. Underexposure generally results in low fringe contrast and, depending on the medium, produces dim holograms. Overexposure causes overmodulation and non-linearity. Higher order Fourier terms start to creep into the reconstruction. Distance of the object to the plate, ie how far or near an object is, will result in well known effects of dispersion and the usual third order aberrations. The most prominent effect is usually the curvature mismatch, especially if the image is in front of the image plane.

Oh, I see what you're trying to do. The words "depth cues" threw me off. Yes, it's a good way of teting for the varying factors you mention. I'd suggest just one shot of all the steps but use a multi-exposure setup. That is, shoot the whole set of steps once, change your ref by about 10 degrees, shoot again, change by 10 degrees, shoot again etc. Using the slits, you have little comparison between steps. Another disadvantage may be that the reference beam hits the object and bounces back onto the plate at a different location to slit, ie it hits the plate where the plate is blocked by a closed slit. In this case, the film will be "fogged" because there is an object beam (the reflected light) and no reference beam. The film is exposed with no image but the light hitting the film will convert some of the grains. When you do expose the particular slit, you'll get a "false positive" because the effect you see is caused by "fogging" as well as by the hologram.

Joy gave a talk on depth cues at PCGGI and when the video sequence is ready, you can see how she went into the exploitation of these cues by classical artists going back to Medieval art.

Briefly, "vergence" is the act of focusing on an object in 3D space by turning your eyes inward. Let's say you're looking at something about 6 inches away from your eyes, which we assume are spread out about 4 inches apart. In this case, each eye is turned about 18 degrees inward in order to focus what you're looking at. The brain knows by how much your eye is turned inward, the 18 degrees, and so "works backwards" to determine where in 3D space the object is.

Inside your eyeball, at the back of it, is the retina - a screen on which your visual world is projected. At the "center" (not really the geometric center) is a bunch of cells from which the nerve bundle sending the visual world goes to the brain. The tramway of your visual world, if you like. This "center" is known as the 'fovea' and is also commonly called 'The blind spot'. Anyway, the point of all this is that the object at 6 inches away throws light into each eyeball and is focused by the lens in your eye into the retina. However, because of the different angles of the light going into each eye, the light is focused on opposite sides of the fovea. This displacement of the focused image on either side of the fovea is known as 'disparity'. The brain knows both the side of the fovea to which the light is focused in each eyeball and it's distance from the fovea and so uses this information, as well as the vergence information (as well as a lot more!) to determine where in 3D space you're looking. In other words, you build up a picture of depth perception from these 'clues'. There is no "depth sensor" in your eye/brain system, only a set of "cues" that the brain uses to calculate.

Dinesh Fri Jul 09, 2004 2:09 pm #14
Fogging in DCG manifests in different ways to Silver. In DCG, the light affects the emulsion as a bulk material as opposed to Silver where the light affects particular crystals. Thus in DCG, fogging due to stray light locally hardens the emulsion. This is because the fogging light causes local cross-linking. If the area of fogging corresponded to an image area the image properties would be different in that area. Thus if an extreme edge of the plate, where there was no image, were fogged you'd never know it, while if a small area in the center of the plate were fogged, the image in this area would appear in a different color than the rest of the image. This is assuming pure fogging - ie only one source of light. If there were low frequency noise superimposed on the hologram due to two sources of light interacting at a low angle, this manifests itself as a surface variation and causes loss in contrast.
