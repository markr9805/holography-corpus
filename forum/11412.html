Joe Farina Thu Jan 23, 2025 11:01 am #2
I couldn't see the rings in the photo. (edit: oops, I didn't scroll down to see the second photo showing the rings.) Probably, this effect is due to water vapor that has managed to penetrate the edge seal and contact the gelatin. The rings seem to be in a non-image area, so it's a bit difficult to be absolutely certain. When water contacts the gelatin, there's nothing that can be done to reverse the damage (when the hologram is sealed between two pieces of glass). Nice hologram, though. It looks like the image hasn't been noticeably damaged yet. Probably the sealing was less than perfect, and it was exposed to high humidity (say 75 to 80%) for some length of time. The important thing is to store it at a reasonably low humidity, say a "typical" room humidity of ~55% or less. Going higher than this will increase the risk of the damage spreading. The rings might be arrested indefinitely, if the RH is kept low.

Din Fri Sep 19, 2025 12:21 pm #4
Very probably. It looks like a water leak.

The gelatin in a DCG hologram is quite soft, even after processing. I read somewhere that it's 200 times softer than silver halide; I can't confirm that, but it is several orders of magnitude softer than silver. Because it's so soft, humidity leaking into the hologram will cause the DCG emulsion to swell, initially causing rings because the swelling is of the order of the wavelength of light (an interference effect) and finally swelling so much that image is destroyed. Therefore, DCG holograms have to be sealed by placing, usually, another sheet of glass over it, and gluing the second piece of glass to the hologram. But, the environment, the method of sealing and the glue are very important for long term stability. If the glue is not strong enough, water vapour will leak through the sides, cause the DCG emulsion to swell, and destroy the image. Some holographers use 2-part epoxy which works well in environments that are not too extreme, some use uv cured polymers, which are better sealants, but expensive. Some holographers scratch off about a quarter inch off the sides of the hologram, hoping that water penetration does not go beyond about a quarter inch. Some holographers apply sealant to the edges of the hologram to stop water penetration. In our (Triple Take Holographics) case, our DCG holograms were quite stable under conditions in San Diego, but when we took the holograms to India - temperatures of ~ 90F and humidity of 100%, water leak damaged the holograms in a matter of days. We took to scraping the edges and sealing the edges with uv polymer. In the end, our final "proof" of the hologram was to put it in the dishwater!

In the case of the necklace, since you were wearing it, I suspect that humoidity from your skin penetrated the seal. If the hologram is reasonably well sealed, a little rain shouldn't damage the hologram, but protracted wearing may cause slow penetration.

There are ways to mitigate the edge leaks. This is a hologram made in 1986, and the picture was taken about 2011 - thirty years later. My wife hid the leak, as you see
