Joe Farina Sun Oct 30, 2005 11:28 am #2
I like the part about using a steel framework to support the table. That's what I use (square, perforated steel pipe, with threaded tie-rods going through some of them, tightened firmly on the ends with bolts).

Personally I wouldn't use a segmented table top. I use a single piece of granite. Although it's the thin variety (about 1 inch thick), it's well-supported underneath by the steel framework. It's the kind of granite used for kitchen counters, and since it's reasonably thin, the weight is much more manageable (compared to thicker granite). Personally, I don't like steel because of its greater expansion/contraction with temperature changes (for long exposures, that is). I use gravity mounts for the optics. The mounts don't need to be heavy, just flat on the bottom so they don't wobble (three-point supports could be used also). The top surface of the granite should already be flat and smoothly polished.

For legs, I bolt 2-inch galvanized pipe (for plumbing) to the steel framework (the legs are 3 feet long in my case). For vibration isolation, I put a racquetball under each leg. (I needed to put a fixture on each end of the legs, so that the racquetballs wouldn't creep up into the pipe over time.) Racquetballs are pressureless, and don't need to be re-filled with air. I would use as many legs as necessary to provide plenty of support for the steel framework.
BobH Sun Oct 30, 2005 1:44 pm #3
I would not recommend a segmented table top. Although it will work (if constructed by someone experienced with interferometric stability issues), the uncertainty about its integrity when built as a first table would (I think) outweigh its portability. Instead, I'd recommend a layer of interlocking cinder blocks (tied together by threaded rods through their holes), supported by a sheet of 3/4" plywood, and topped with a sheet of 1/8" steel. While not portable, this construction comes apart in pieces easily managed by two people.

Hans, Danny came up with a very good idea about a table some time back.

Find an steel door that has a little weight to it (100lbs US or so). Then simply place this door on inner tubes.

Colin Kaminski Tue Nov 01, 2005 1:24 pm #8
Lon Moore was one of the most prolific holographers ever. He ran his lab in different incarnations from 1971 to 2003. He prefer ed to master on a sand table even though he had a very nice newport table.

His take on sand was there was not a long settle time once you learned how to place the optics. He said that you acquire a feel for how to insert the mount in the sand.

It is a shame that this generation of amateur holographers has not adopted sand tables. They work very well.
JohnFP Tue Nov 01, 2005 1:32 pm #9
I tried a new approach to sand tables just before I moved into the lab that I am in now. About 9 months ago. I had a sand table and my mounts are made out of a weighlifting set. Big round bases. You can see them on my web page under new lab. I took the dark green contractors trash bags (the thick ones - I think 6 mil thick) and put them on the top of the sand and duct taped them together. When I would set a optic, I would place it where I wanted and tap the top, a little hard at first and gradually easier. I could feel it set in the sand through the plastic liner. This worked very well and kept the sand dust down when moving optics.
