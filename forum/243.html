BobH Tue Jun 07, 2011 8:37 am #2
I seldom use goggles when working in holography. With green light, I generally use a beam attenuator right in front of the laser to turn the power down to a comfortable level for working with it. I'll wear goggles to look at and center the expanded beam if necessary, when the beam is at full power.

It's more important to keep track of where the beam is going. Every time you turn a laser on, it should be blocked with something. Every time you put something into the beam, you should be aware of where the beam will go, including the back reflections. Avoid ever having the beam point upward! That's incredibly dangerous.
Dinesh Tue Jun 07, 2011 11:46 am #3
I don't use goggles. The problem is that the goggles can be burnt by a direct, unexpanded beam. While you're adjusting optics, you do have to bend over and reach across the table. In doing this, your face is sometimes inches above the table and it's quite possible to get in the way of a direct unexpanded beam which can be seen, generally, without goggles but cannot with goggles. So, it's possible that with goggles you may get in the way of an unexpanded beam which would burn through the goggles and damage your eyes. You've got to be aware where all the raw, unexpanded beams are all the time. I use a laser with a current control, so I turn down the current while I'm setting up.
Gall Thu Jun 09, 2011 4:55 am #4
Goggles are used to protect you from random reflections in case something falls out its place, you accidentally cross the beam with a reflecting screwdriver etc. They really help in such cases even if they aren't high quality; they may be burnt by the beam but they save you from accidental reflections.

Goggles are not really needed if you're working with fully assembled robust equipment and aren't moving lenses and mirrors directly by hand. If you're able to attenuate the beam to something like 1 mW and your attenuator is safe enough, you do not need goggles as well.

Always wear goggles if you're working with anything more than 10-20 mW "by hand", without FULLY controlled beam (i.e. while adding one more lens to your table - the dangerous moment when the optical component already crosses the beam but is not fully in place, the beam may be reflected or refracted in an unpredictable way by edges, holders, screws etc.)

Cheap eBay laser goggles are mostly Ok. Just check them: first, you should be able to see the laser spot looking like a $1 laser pointer's one, and second, be sure that they aren't burnt within 1-2 seconds if placed directly under the beam. (Better use non-optical parts of the goggles to do the smoke test, they're made entirely from the same plastic). You'll close your eyes within 1-2 seconds anyway, so this is enough to protect you.

You can view IR with your camera (cellphone cameras, cheap webcams etc. are Ok). They are all sensitive to IR and represent them as blue. (Try to look at TV remote control through such a camera while switching channels). You may use a cheapest camera to check if your goggles are safe enough - unlike a eye, the camera can be replaced.

In general, choose the OD of the goggles so that your laser is attenuated to laser pointer's (1 mW) level. OD 2 glasses attenuate 10^2=100 times so they turn 100 mW into 1 mW. OD 3 should be usable as well. OD 4 is too much for 100 mW.

Gall Fri Jun 10, 2011 3:46 am #8
One has to mention that using "wrong" goggles is worse than not wearing goggles at all. It is obvious that there are goggles for wrong wavelength or with low OD. Less obvious is that there are cheap goggles with TOO MUCH OD. They are dangerous if you're doing alignment because you cannot see the beam at all and may notice it only when it has burnt a hole in your goggles.

For visible light, always wear goggles of such OD that you can see the laser of non-dangerous level. I suggest to choose the OD equal to the number of zeroes in laser power in mW or a bit more. I.e., for an 100 mW laser OD 2 is ideal, OD 2.5 or OD 3 is usable.

Proper goggles make the alignment easier.

For invisible light (IR and UV), there cannot be too much OD. Just choose high-quality goggles.
BobH Fri Jun 10, 2011 8:25 am #9
Kyodal makes good points above, but I don't agree. The wearing of safety goggles all the time when working with laser light, just so they will be there if a beam ever hits you in the eye is neither practical nor a good idea. The first step in preparing a room to work with laser light is to provide for variable lighting. Light level is always changing, depending on what you need to see. Especially with holography.

One moment you're looking at a bright raw beam on a white wall as you move it toward an aperture, then you're seeing it on a black iris, then you're looking at just the dim edges to center the beam in the aperture, then you're looking for back reflections off (for example) a microscope objective, then you're looking for faint light coming through a pinhole, then you're fiddling with an expanded spot to make it uniform. Goggles would necessarily be on and off several times in that 60 second exercise, each time needing to be set down on a crowded table, picked up and put on again and again. Constant fiddling with the damn things. And if you wear reading glasses, you have to constantly take the goggles on and off to see something close or far. Nothing but frustration, and THAT is waaaaaay more dangerous in a laser lab than the absence of goggles.

And what about using more than one color of laser light simultaneously? I think it's more important to focus on safe handling of laser light than protection from it, when the power is less than a few hundred milliWatts. Over that, there should be goggles on hand (to put on face) with much higher situational awareness and access control. As mentioned above, keep the beams in a plane and as far away from eye level as possible. Treat laser light like high voltage.

Dinesh Fri Jun 10, 2011 10:39 am #11
Perhaps what's missing here is the appreciation of the different environments. Kyodai works in a chemical environment. In such an environment, the chances of a chemical splash are not insignificant and sometimes quite random. Also, there is no "blink response" for a chemical splash. While working with chemicals, it's quite easy to observe reactions through the glasses and the situation of a raw beam hitting the glasses is not valid in a chemical lab. Therefore in a chemistry environment, I think wearing glasses all the time is an important lab procedure.

In holography, the points mentioned by Bob and Gall make the difference. If a raw beam did hit the goggles, the fumes given off by the burning plastic are probably just as dangerous as the actual beam. Also, for a raw beam, there is no real blink response, a raw beam of 100mW will damage your eye faster than you can blink and will continue to burn through your eyelids. Also, as Bob mentions, there is a need to constantly put on and remove the glasses. This increases the chance of a reflection from a ring or similar artifact. So, I'd agree with Bob that proper care and control of the beams are the more important factors.

> Jeffrey Weil: Does anybody know any holographers that have actually done real damage?

Not 'real' damage. But someone (forget who) told me he was working with a pulse laser and it reflected off his metal watchband. He said his world was seen through a green disk for a week. Luckily, the green disk disappeared after a week.

I think it's a matter of personal preference with a little common sense thrown in. You cannot protect people from themselves; if you're going to play with highly coherent focused beams, you need to be careful, up to a point. Similarly, if you're going to play with high voltage supplies, you need to be a little careful. Rules will neither protect you nor ensure that something will not go wrong. However, a laser beam in and of itself is simply a very bright light. Modern cars have headlights that, reflecting off the rear view mirror, are more powerful than a lot of lasers, more dangerous in some cases since the light is hitting you directly in the eye in dark surroundings. It's not the laser that's dangerous per se, it's the beam intensity at your eye. As an example, sunlight hitting a white wall directly gives off (very roughly) 1 W/sq m. or 0.1 mW/sq cm. A 50 mW laser expanded to 500 sq cm (about 12 cm or 30 in. in radius) will give the same intensity, actually less since the eye is not so sensitive at narrow band frequencies. At the plate, the beam has been pretty seriously diminished by the usual lenses, mirrors etc, to (usually) somewhere around a milliwatt or so. In this case, expanding the beam to 10 sq cm ( about 2cm or an inch in radius) will give the same intensity as staring at a bright, sunlit white wall.

Steve got a beam in the eye when working on his large frame argon ion laser. He said he was cleaning it and looking for the source of some off-axis scatter in the tube, and just went in too far. That's what I remember, and his embarrassment for having done such a dangerous thing.

One thing I use instead of goggles is a set of large diameter polarizers. You can get them from Edmund Optics (4" diameter) or just use a couple of sheets held together. It's very easy to twist them near extinction to have a variable attenuator right in front of your face to see very bright areas, and tell if (for example) a bright beam is centered accurately in an iris.

The attenuator I build in front of the laser to turn it down is a half wave plate, a polarizing beam splitter cube, and a shielded beam dump.
