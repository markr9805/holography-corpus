Joe Farina Thu Jul 31, 2008 3:07 pm #2
Good to see you back, Dutchelm05

My interest in holography has been sustained for many years (now more than ever). The reason I'm so interested is because of its potential to become art. That's the whole key. You have to look at art first, then holography second. People continuously fail in holography, and lose interest, because they see holography first, and then try to "capture the real world in a three-dimensional nutshell," to borrow a really inane phrase from Hans Bjelkhagen. You can make a science demonstration, even with a hologram so good that it's difficult to tell it from reality. But so what? This may be interesting for a while, but it soon becomes isipid. Well, I certainly can't speak for everyone, but's that's my view.

An interest in holography can only be sustained if you have enough objectivity to throw Holography into the trash can, if that's where it belongs. This is what I mean by putting art first, and holography second. The problem is that people in general don't give a damn about art. They want gimmicks to make a few bucks, so they can buy consumer products (drugs).

But to answer your question Tony, I would say take up sculpture, but sculpture based on the parameters established by DCG. This is exciting and challenging, and I can't imagine losing interest.

The rub is when the hologram has a reflection. Danny's Penguin is a great example (sorry for picking this as an example ). While much of the light was reflected, a large percentage of the light was being reflected in a gold tone specular reflection. It left a very nice gold square on the floor in front of the hologram. This reflection detracted from the light forming the image. Bob and I pondered the source of the reflection and speculated that it was a proximity copy with no index matching fluid. However if I remember right it was an H2 from a transmission master.

Joe Farina Mon Aug 04, 2008 3:40 pm #30
With DCG it's really assumed that the hologram is going to be very bright. I would just buy some commercially-made DCG images, if you don't have them already, then you will have an idea about the typical brightness. I wouldn't get stuck on the brightness issue. Do you have some of A. Muth's holograms? If not, I would buy a few of the small ones, or a single bigger one. Then you will have a benchmark from a leader in DCG technique. I have one by him that's an image-plane holo, it's broadband and extremely bright. I use this for comparison. Another thing you can do is make a hologram of a mirror, which will show what a 1:1 ratio can accomplish (likely it will be dazzling bright). DCGs can really diffract a tremendous amount of light. I don't see any reason why a properly-made split beam master reflection hologram can't be just as bright as a H2. The only thing with the H2 is that you can cause the image to straddle the plane, or pop out in front, which, to be honest, I never thought too highly of. In my opinion, the very highest quality holograms are split beam master holograms, not H2's or copies. But by "highest quality" I'm taking all factors into consideration; cleanliness, brightness, noise, parallax, etc.

JohnFP Wed Aug 06, 2008 6:32 pm #54
Well, I believe there are two theories and I actually believe both theories are true depending on how you process your DCG.

The first is, a simple difference of index of refraction between the unexposed (less dense and a lower index of refraction) and the exposed (more dense and a higher index of refraction). It is this differential that refract or bends the light into the image. Here is it gel/gel.

The second is, during processing the unexposed regions are actually emptied of some gelatin and water and processed in such a way that an air void (or crack if you will) forms there. Again it is this differential that refracts or bends the light into the image. Here is it gel/air.

This really should be studied to see which method works best for the outcome one is trying to produce (broadband, narrow band, reflection, transmission).

I would have to say for transmission it would cause the replay angle to change more for the latter then the former due to a greater bending. But probably not enough to effect display work.

JohnFP Wed Aug 06, 2008 7:14 pm #59
R. K. Curran and T. A. Shankoff

Applied Optics, Vol. 9, Issue 7, pp. 1651-1657

Abstract

Experiments have been performed which lead to the conclusion that dichromated gelatin holograms arise as a result of the formation of an air-gelatin interface, with the observed high diffraction efficiencies resulting from the air-gelatin refractive index differential of 0.52 found at the interface. It is postulated that rapid removal of water by isopropanol in the development process creates strains in the gelatin film which are relieved by splitting. The resultant cracks in the hologram manifest themselves in an obvious location, between the highly exposed planes of cross-linked gelatin. Photomicrographic evidence on Bragg-Lippmann and on plane gratings, liquid gate data, lack of thickness reciprocity for the gratings, and observations of grating thickness employing bandpass measurements comprise the bulk of the experimental work. From these data, a model is proposed that is consistent with all the experimental results for both Bragg-Lippmann and plane gratings.
