Sorry, I hit "submit" too soon.

Regarding your question about the algorithms used to generate the imagery, I think there is a simple way of understanding it which I might ironically be unable to express clearly here this evening.

Basically, their algorithms compute what the 3-D scene looks like from a variety of viewpoints, and then modulate a bunch of "basis fringes" (varying frequency carriers) with the amplitudes that are intended to be seen from those viewpoints. These are summed together, launched down the AOM, and then de-scanned to counteract the fact that the time-varying holographic data are flying down the AOM.

If you're familiar with electronic 3-D displays, it's conceptually similar to multi-view displays. Dr. Lucente refers to this as "diffraction-specific" computation. In my opinion, it's a good method because you can quickly compute what a scene looks like from many points of view, and then "weight" the basis vectors with those data. This is faster than using brute-force to compute the contributions of each point in the image, etc. etc. It ends up in basically the same information (although I suppose the phase might be ignored.)

If this is unclear, it's my fault. You can probably find a better explanation in Chapter 4 of his thesis [(PDF)](http://www.lucente.us/pubs/PhDthesis/4.pdf).

favalora Sun May 06, 2007 1:52 pm #10
Hi -

Regarding your first set of questions, I unfortunately don't know the answer to that. I am not familiar enough with the physics of AOMs. I like to think about it "one level up," which is as an agile beam-steering device with non-zero length and the ability to launch beams in multiple directions at once from each small region of the AOM.

You suggest its inability to elicit accommodation responses. I don't think that the MIT group ever measured that. Howver, I believe that it is theoretically quite capable of generating good reconstructions of light fields, even if they are horizontal-parallax only, and even if Mark I and Mark II had some scan-line clarity issues. For example, some researchers in Japan believe that you can cause accomodation if you have a sufficient number of "views" - the researcher called it the "super multiview condition." Perhaps the MIT system is capable of that too.

I don't know how the eye focuses in response to highly astigmatic imagery, such as HPO holograms.

Actually, I would like to know more about what cues cause the eye to focus. I have spoken with several vision experts but am not satisfied with the answers I hear yet; perhaps that is still an area of advanced research. I wonder if one problem is that researchers can't create good experimental stimulii yet! That is, I wonder if they need a good full-parallax display in order to do the experiments they need.

You asked about my opinions on the viability of electro-holographic versus multi-view displays that, for example, use multiple projectors or time-multiplexed view scanning. I think that both are promising. In my opinion, non-holographic displays have the advantage today, because in 2007 it is easier to reconstruct 3-D light fields with multiple fast projectors than to create arbitrary diffraction patterns. However, I would guess that this will change. I have a feeling that holography could win, but I don't know how many years it will take for this to occur. 2 years? 10?

For example, [we](http://www.actuality-systems.com) built a display in 2003 or 2004 that generated an image from a flat panel with - believe it or not - 198 views across about 30 degrees. It was 4" big and 4" deep. It was very believable; it was the only flat-panel multiview display whose imagery I involuntarily reached out to grab. (How did it work? Here's [one description.](http://appft1.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1&Sect2=HITOFF&d=PG01&p=1&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.html&r=1&f=G&l=50&s1=%2220050270645%22.PGNR.&OS=DN/20050270645&RS=DN/20050270645))
