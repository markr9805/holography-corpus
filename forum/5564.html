Tony Wed May 24, 2006 10:54 am #2
The equation for gaussian distribution is:

Amplitude = exp(-1* [x/k] ^2)

(sorry, no equation editor here).

k is a constant, which is the radius of the circle where the amplitude is 1/e times the peak.

As for your example, there's some data missing. What is your beam diameter before expansion? Assume it's 1mm. What does that mean? Assume it means k is 1mm. After expansion to a diameter of 8 inches, the beam amplitude at the edges will be 1/e times the center amplitude . The exact definition of beam diameter should be buried in the specs for your laser. For example, the beam diameter and divergence for a high power Melles-Griot HeNe are specified at 1/e^2.

So you first have to account for the 1/r^2 reduction in intensity due to expansion to find the amplitude at the center. You'll need to integrate the above equation over the beam area if you wish to relate it to a total output power number.

You can use the equation above to get the relative amplitude profile across the expanded beam.

Hope this helps.

Tony Fri Jun 09, 2006 11:59 am #4
> Can it be said simply that some number like 20% larger then the plate is best because after that there is not much advantage of a more even intensity distribution until the beam is expaned to 1000% the size of the plate.Or can it be said that if the beam is expanded to just the corners of the plate that that is the best as larger only wastes light with no better intensity distribution.Or can it be said that the more you expand the beam the more evenly illuminated the plate becomes.

All of the above is true!

Unless you expand the beam infinitely, there will always be some variation across it. And if you expand it infinitely, you get an infinitely long exposure!

The real answer is as follows:

Your goal is to get the maximum use out of the limited dynamic range of the medium you're using (DCG or silver, etc). Dynamic range is the range of the highest to the lowest light intensites that fall within the quasi-linear region, and therefore index modulation, attainable. You need the reference + the brightest object reflection to be at the top of the quasi-linear region. You also want to make sure that the object:reference ratio never exceeds 1:1 at any point. (Using 1:2 or 1:4 as guidelines accounts somewhat for the diffeerence between peak and spatially averaged readings).

Therefore, in the center of the hologram, you have a limit set by the top of the dynamic range of the medium. At the edges, where the reference beam is lower due to the gaussian profile, this limits the peak object light you can accept.

This means that you are basicly losing an amount of dynamic range that is proportional to the difference between the reference beam in the center and at the edges. So there's no definitive answer; it is a subjective kind of thing.

A large variation (reference beam expanded less) will result in some vignetting due to the loss of dynamic range at the edges. A small variation will be more uniform, but will add technical difficulties if the exposures get too long. If you have adequate sensitivity and/or stability, then you want to expand the beam as much as you can get away with.

It's interesting that the eye and human perception are actually quite forgiving to the vignetting effects - it seems to be much less noticeable than one would think from making absolute measurements.
