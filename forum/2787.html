edelbrp Fri Oct 15, 2004 7:10 pm #7
I got fustrated with getting my gloves torn handling the glass plates. After turning on the lights, I would realized my glove had some developer leaked into it dissolving my fingers away..! Yuck!

Now, I've got a little plastic holder which I snap the plate into. I never touch the chemicals, and it's very convenient to use this handle to move the plate from one bath to the next. It also has the added benefits that it keeps the plate a couple mm from the bottom of the tray (in case the emulsion is face down), and keeps finger marks from showing up where the bath wasn't consistent across the plate.

I've also use plastic drum tanks for developing 4x5 or larger film (not plates). Those are really easy to deal with and can be used in regular light. The drawback is that you can't check the development. But, if you have the process down and know your timings, it's a breeze. And, no trays to spill or clean.

BTW- Are there significant advantages of using TEA over sugar? I've been using sugar (only a few times), and it seems to work pretty well. Maybe safer, too. ;') At first I thought TEA increases the sensitivity (it might), but I read that doing a pre-soak in pure water (with a little photo-flo) also increases sensitivity. (At least for film that's been around a while.)

-Phil

Tom B. Mon Oct 18, 2004 3:04 am #13
> edelbrp: This probably isn't a new idea, but what about using sugar (OK, or TEA) for multiple exposures to generate (psuedo) full color with regular film?

The technique is known as "pseudocolor reflection holography" and is often used in art holography. Saxby credits Phil Kaufman and Lon Moore as pioneers of this technique. Along with getting the exposures right, the trickiest part is maintaining correct color registration - since the replay angle and magnification change with wavelength, the reference beam must be adjusted for each exposure to compensate. I don't recall where I saw the geometry worksheet online, but it's also in Saxby's book.

No, I haven't tried it yet.
Colin Kaminski Mon Oct 18, 2004 3:15 pm #14
Jonathan, Michael and I went to Lon's studio and saw some Achromatic holograms made this way. It was very remarkable. I don't remember any full color holograms, but lots of psuedo color stuff.

The problem with making a full color hologram with a single laser is there is no easy way to make the object reflect different amounts of light for the different exposures. This would be required to make different parts of the object different colors. With Lon's psuedo color holograms he simply changed to a different object in a different space for each color.
Ed Wesly Mon Oct 18, 2004 5:16 pm #15
Have a scene which can be carefully repainted (like three models of the same thing with different reflectivities for each wavelength) and a way to put things nearly exactly they way they were for the previous exposure.

Larry Lieberman and his studio, which evolved into the "C Project" did exactly what is described above. Either the object was repainted between each shot to give the RGB sep's, (areas of the object blackened or whitened) or three identical objects but painted for color separation were kinematically re-positioned in front of the plate holder.

They used a big Krypton laser at 647 for the H1's, and Tea'ed the H2's.

Some of the famous artists that had holograms recorded at the C project studio were Ed Paschke, Larry Rivers, Roy Liechtenstien, James Turrell, etc.

Unfortunately there is no web site devoted to the rise and fall of this great idea. Once in a while one of these holograms pops up in a retrospective or show of the artist's work, so be on the lookout.
