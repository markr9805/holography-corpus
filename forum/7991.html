Yep, another table design question, as I am planning to build one. "Problem" is twofold, mainly space and the time it will be standing. The house I live in has 1 rather small room left for me to make holograms, but that will limit the table to approx. 100 by 80 cm's (3 by 2,5 feet). In the vast archive of old posts I have found numerous posts stating size does not matter and I believe it was Colin who said he could and had done almost all types of holograms on a 1 foot by 1 foot table. So size might not be a problem, correct?

I have taken a look in the wiki about tables, and there are some types mentioned. Mainly concrete, granite and sand. Steel is also mentioned on the forum, apart from sandwiches of steel and hexcel.

The thing is I will not be living in this house for long. Any table I build will be removed (disassembled) in a relative short amount of time. For that reason, I am not very enthousiastic about using concrete, since getting rid of that stuff is, to say the least, rather cumbersome. More so when (and that looks to me as I really need to) I use metal strengtening.

I have looked around and it is rather (to say really) expensive to use really thick steel, as a door or a seperate sheet op thick steel. And since I might build a "proper" table after I have moved, I was not planning on spending a huge amount of money on this one.

Sandwiches of steel and some exotic material are also not really wanted due to cost.

I know JohnFP used to have a sand table, covered with some plastic and I think he was quite happy with it. So that might be a possibility. It is both cheap and easy to build/remove.

However, granite also seems to be possible. Due to the limited size of the table, I can purchase tiles of granite with the 80 by 80, or maybe even the 100 by 80 size. The thickness would be 3cm (slighty more than 1 inch). Would that work?

If I use granite, the tabel would be four legs made of cinder blocks, probably two on top of each other, with a layer of particleboard in between (to act as a shelf for items), on top of the second block a piece of carpet. On top of that another sheet of particleboard on which rest a number of inner tubes. Again a sheet of particleboard layered with some paving stones (for the weight only, not stiffness, since they are too small to cover the entire space alone, I need to use more then 1 per layer). Another particleboard, topped with the granite.

Does that sound like a possible table (of course the proof in is in the Michelson)?

I also read something about tennisballs? And if that slab of granite isn't completely smooth, would another layer of carpet and a plate of glass as a top be useful, or would that destroy the stability? (sanding the granite doesn't seem a possibility).

At first, I will do some Denisyuks, so the need for stability is minimal, but I would also like to expand into multibeams and such, so a small, but useful table is wanted....

i would recommend the largest piece of stone you can carry/lift (two man) , if its a 1" marble or ,you may find a 36"x36", then on this build a small pipe frame to hold your collimating mirror, (as in overhead reference beam),now place your laser and spatial filter off on a separate table. i agree about standing concrete blocks on end for legs,(i glued mine together and to the floor with liquid nails glue) ,then place your 3/4" thick plywood on top (to tie all the legs together) , then place dampeners and finally your slab , these marble/stone tops can be found at antique stores missing a corner or chipped and be very cheap! this should be a good go between until you out grow the table , as you will already have a nice 12“-14”

rzeheb Thu Oct 01, 2009 7:54 pm #7
Hi Stranger,

My table puts about 100 lbs on each of 4 tennis balls. The tennis balls sit on 3/4 inch plywood which sits on about 2 inches of compact foam. I have been using this set-up for about 8 months and I have sufficient stability for split beam transmission and reflection holos and also for image plane H2 copies. However, as JohnFP points out the tennis balls are slowly deflating. Eventually, I will have to replace them and that will be much more of a pain (I think) than trying to get a shot of extra air into an inner tube valve.

The_Stranger, I think you have misread some post since I have never used tennis or raquet balls, though, I have also asked about them. It seems most use innertubes. I put a piece of carpeting below (shag up) and a piece on top (shag down). This not only insures no splinter or edges rub into the tube causing a leak, but allows it to breath. I never worried about having the valves accessable but it would be a good idea. My table used 6 tubes and they were all acccessable from one of the sides. If you do the hole in the center of the tube for the valve, that should be good enough to release the pressure on the inside of the ring.
