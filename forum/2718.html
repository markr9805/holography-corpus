[quote:0ec0f4f33f]1. When doing the reflection holograms, the exposure time was 10 seconds, should I have increased this for the transmission? [/quote:0ec0f4f33f]

The exposure depends on how much light you're putting into it. The 10 seconds for the reflection (single beam, I assume) would also depend on how much light the object was throwing back at the plate. A lot depends on the ratio of object to reference. For a reflection, you need about 1:1, for a laser transmission, you need about 4:1 (as you've found). If your object is dim in the reflected laser light, either paint it a flat white or change it's orientation so it throws more light onto the plate. One way of telling is to put a white card in the plateholder, shut off the ref and see how much light is scattered onto the card. Then turn on the ref and wiggle your fingers in the reference beam, making sure that your fingers don't interrupt the object beam. The shadow of your fingers on the card should be a sort of light grey. If the shadow is very dark, get rid of some of the ref. If you don't have a variable beamsplitter, put a piece of glass in the path of the ref and twist it so some light is reflected off. The more you twist, the less the ref until the shadow is grey.

I don't know off-hand what the sensitivity of PFG-03M is, but I suspect it's about 100microJ/sq cm or so. If you have a meter, get the ratio (I like the "wiggling finger" method, others have different ways) and then measure the reference beam in microW/sq cm. Divide 100 by this number and that'll be the exposure. So, if you measure 0.5 microw/sq cm, exposure is 100/0.5 = 200 seconds. If you don't have a power meter, several people on this forum have made one from Radio Shack parts and may be able to help you make one. You can also try exposure tests. Start by assuming you've cut the power at the plate by 1/20 of the laser power, so a 3mW laser now gives you 0.3mW or 150 microW/sq cm . This is a (100/150) 0.6 sec exposure. So make a black mask with removable sections. Remove one section, expose for 0.3 seconds. Replace the section, remove the next one and expose for 0.6 seconds . Go on to the next section and expose 0.9 seconds etc. The number of tests you can get on one plate depends on the number of sections you can cut in your mask.

[quote:0ec0f4f33f]2. Also not sure about the 60 Degree angle, how exact does this need to be? I think I altered this a little to try and get more laser beam on the plate and less on the objects. (trying for 1:4 ratio to make result brighter – if this is correct?)[/quote:0ec0f4f33f]

Not very. The 60 degree is due to an angle known as Brewster's angle and has to do with polarisation considerations and woodgrain. We covered the Brewster's angle "myth" in a previous thread: [http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1576](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1576)

[quote:0ec0f4f33f]3. When viewing transmission I have heard that I need to use the original laser from the exposure. Does this work with JD-4? Apparently there is a colour shift with this developer, so I’d be trying to view a green hologram with red laser? – would this be a problem? [/quote:0ec0f4f33f]

Maybe. When there's a "color" shift for a laser transmission, the reconstruction angle changes. In order to see the image at its brightest, you need to twist the hologram a little. There's also a distortion of the object. If you're doing a hologram where you wanted to exactly image plane some feature, it'd be difficult. But on the whole, so long as the change was not too drastic, there's little problem. We shoot H1's (laser transmission) at 514 and H2 (the reflection copy) at 488 with some distortion, but not really noticeable.
Tony Fri Sep 10, 2004 12:46 pm #3
Rick-

1. Exposure. Typically transmission holos need less density than reflection holograms. If the film is as dark before bleaching for transmission as for reflection, you have adequate exposure (and could use less). Overall, exposure is a fairly weak factor in resulting hologram brightness.

2. Brewster angle. This is not a requirement to get a bright hologram. The suggestion to maintain Brewster angle is only to mitigate woodgrain artifact. You can get perfectly bright holograms at other angles (brighter in fact). Also, Brewster angle is only important if you are maintaining p-polarization.

3. Shrinkage / reconstruction wavelength. In transmission holograms, the grating spacing is much larger than in reflection holograms, so the shrinkage effect is less. Shrinkage might cause geometric distortion of the image, but typically would not be a strong effect on brightness. The zeroth order effect of shrinkage on a transmission hologram would be to change the angle required of the reconstruction beam for max brightness.

So what does this leave? By far the strongest effect on brightness is stability. It's odd, as more stability is required for reflection than transmission, but apparently you've had success with reflection. However, it's typically easier to get good film-subject stability, by resting/attaching the film to the object in a reflection setup. Without seeing your setup, I would think that doing what you can to make it more stable would help. Have you built an interferometer to test stability? Is you setup single beam or split beam?

Another factor is beam ratio, but you are on the right track at 1:4.

Dinesh et al, please feel free to jump in and correct my generalities and gross overstatements!

> rja4096: 3. When viewing transmission I have heard that I need to use the original laser from the exposure.

I have used the same materials you are using. It was a single beam transmission holo. I pulled the lens off the laser-diode to get a clean divergent beam. When I looked squarely through the plateholder in the direction of the objects, they appeared centered and brightly lit, with the beam hitting them from the left side (in my setup,) but mostly from the front, not directly from the left side. Also, I had to add a piece of cardboard to keep the light from hitting the backside of the holo plate.

A mirror was placed to the right side of the objects to reflect the extra light that was missing the objects back to the plate as the reference beam (approx. 40-60 deg angle).

I used the same general dev. procedure you described. I made a little balsa-wood box and used the holo as one of the faces of the box. I put a really cheap $1.99 laser pointer, again with it's lens ripped off, in the corner of the box and when you looked inside you could see a very bright psuedoscopic image floating in the center of the box. You don't need the <i>exact</i> laser, just the same or similar wavelength. In this example both were 650nm.

As a side note: since the transmission holo was shot with a diverging reference beam, and I was reconstructing with another diverging beam, I could not get an undistorted image to reconstruct as orthoscopic, with the image floating between my eyes and the plate. This also means you could not make an H2 imageplaned copy of this hologram without a conjugate ref beam (a converging ref beam needed in this case.)

I was having some development issues using PFG-03M and JD-4 and have since switched to using PFG-01 and JD-2 (still purchasing from Integraf) and now get very consistant results.

Good Luck and Keep trying!

Hi. I've used PFG-03M and JD-4 and had good results.

With a light source at 654nm, I found that 1.5 mJ/cm2 works pretty good, although as others have noted, less energy works for transmission holograms too.

My experience has been that it's very important to let things settle before doing an exposure. One thing that I do is leave the box of plates on the table for a day before I do the shot, that way they'll be fairly close to the same temperature. I'd guess you have the holography kit from Intergraf. Using the materials in the kit, my first holograms didn't work very well until I made sure things were thermally and physically stable. Generally, I have things set up a day in advance. When I'm going to expose a plate, I fire up the laser a half hour before. At a distance of 37cm, with the output from the laser striking the plate at around 45 degrees, I was getting good results with 10-15 seconds of exposure with the Intergraf laser. When you develop PFG-03M plates look at the plate at the end of the developer bath; It should be visibly dark, but you should still be easily able to see the safelight through it. I typically use about 15 seconds in the developer. Try and mix the developer only a short time before you use it; if you're lazy like me, it'll work for around a day after, but it tends to wuss out even before it turns dark.

I built a plateholder, and I try handle the plate as little as possible while transferring it from the box to the plateholder. I let it settle for about 60 seconds, then I lift the blocking card just off the table for about 10 seconds, then do the exposure.

PFG-03M developed with JD4 replays at a shorter wavelength, but for transmission holograms it doesn't seem to make much difference. I use this material to make gratings, and it works swell.

--Ted.

I made my first transmission hologram last night, and it came out! PFG-03M, JD-4, exposure similar to a reflection hologram setup (very dark after development). The depth is amazing. The reference beam was at about 45 degrees to the plate, and the diode was at the far end of the table (about 5 ft) with the lens screwed in quite a ways to uncollomate the beam wide enough for the shot. If I look through the plate down towards the diode, I can see the wood grain of the table a few feet! Flipping around the plate and shooting the laser beam through it, it projects the scene from the perspective of that spot on the plate. Very cool.

I'd suggest trying again, if you haven't already. I've had quite a few plates simply not show anything after development. And, who knows? Something could have slipped, maybe the batteries for the diode needed charging, maybe the chemicals are spent, the neighbor kid with his booming subwoofer drove by at just the wrong time, etc.
