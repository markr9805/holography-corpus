Given the assumption that an object can only travel 1/4th to 1/2th the wavelength of light being used, during the total exposure time it takes to make a hologram, I did a few simple calculations.

I thought it would be really neat if a Q-swwitched YAG laser, frequency doubled to 532 nm - could catch a bullet flying out of a gun - in mid-air.

So I wanted to know what the maximum velocity of the bullet would be.

The velocity I came up with, for a distance traveled during this time -

between 1/4th - 1/2th the wavelength of light being used - was a velocity between 59.5 mph - 119 mph. That would be 95.76 kph - 191.52 kph for those of you on the metric system.

While that may sound fairly fast, the muzzle velocities of guns are usually much higher. But the muzzle velocity could be lowered with a low grain bullet, making such a hologram possible.

Since I was now thinking in terms of maximum velocity, instead of just the amount of distance traveled during exposure time, I decided to do this same calculation for non Q-switched dye lasers.

These have a pulse time of 500 ns.

For a green dye laser; with an output of 540 nm, the maximum distance an object can travel - given the same assumption that this distance must be limited to 1/4th to 1/2th the wavelength of light being used - is ALSO equivalent to a velocity between 0.27 m/sec - 0.54 m/sec.

This translates to a velocity of 270 millimeters - 540 millimeters per second.

Or between 10.63 inches/sec - 21.26 inches/sec.



CONCLUSION: While it may not be possible to capture a bullet in mid-air with a xenon pumped dye laser, it should be possible to make holograms of PEOPLE, PETS, and in general, LIVING ANIMALS.

You wouldn't be able to capture "action scenes" . . . .

But people are already accustomed to "posing" for conventional photographs. The fact that they would ALSO have to pose for a hologram, should not affect them in the slightest.



The only thing that is lacking in a dye laser is coherence length.

But if I'm not mistaken, Q-switched YAG lasers have a very short coherence length also.

In a frequency doubled YAG laser, this problem is solved by filtering the beam through prisms, while discarding the unwanted frequencies of light. The beam is THEN run through an interferometer, which narrows the bandwidth. The final output is then passed through a green filter, eliminating the last few remaining traces of unwanted light.

At least that is my understanding; while attempting to understand the schematics described in a Russian holography studio, that uses a Q-switched YAG laser at 20 ns.

Of course I could be mistaken in my understanding, and also could be off in my calculations.

Well, pulling out the famous back-of-an-envelope ...

The average heartbeat of an average person (whoever that is) is about 60 a minute. This is about 1 Hz. I don't know the amplitude but a human hair is about 50 microns, so if we assume 1/10th of a human hair, that gives an amplitude of 5 microns. Thus skin moves at about 20 microns/sec or 40 lambda/sec (remember that peak occurs at 1/4 period). In order to limit motion to less than 1/4 lambda, you'd have exposure times of 6.25 ms. Now assume that you're exposing a 4x5 (10cm x 12.5cm) film with a sensitivity of 150 microjoules/sq cm (typical figures for todays emulsions), you need to zap the plate with 18.75 mJ in 6.25 ms or 3125 W. That's an awful lot of power (but check the calculation, I'm prone to arithmetical errors. Well known fact: mathematicians can't add!) With a pulse, this is pretty easy, but with cw I'm not sure it's that easy unless you do it with a fairly small area, as Ed Wesley and Hans did. So, not impossible, but not easy either.

Joe Farina Mon Dec 06, 2004 3:47 pm #13
Over the years, I've had an interest in dye lasers for holography. As Sergio pointed out, they have certainly been used for this purpose.

But a person has to be practical, and the first question should be this: Which dye laser do I intend to use? And just as important, how will I get it?

I'm of the opinion that dye lasers are the ultimate form of laser, because they can do many things the others simply cannot. And since the lasing medium is a fluid, it can be rapidly pumped out of the excitation zone (as rapidly as necessary). So, they can be scaled up in power to an almost arbitrary limit, since they can dissipate heat like no other type of laser can. And, if I'm not mistaken, dye lasers hold the record for the narrowest linewidth for any type of laser. Add to this the fact that they can produce any color in the visible spectrum.

What is the cost for this kind of performance? I would define it as incredible, in terms of complexity. I would say that buying a dye laser suitable for holography is pretty much out of the question. So we're left with the prospect of building one. Another tremendous task. Of course, you could build the one in Scientific American. Even that is dangerous and quite difficult. But the power is they key isssue. It simply doesn't have enough for a pulsed laser for holography. If the ouput had a decent coherence length, perhaps then it could be used. But it doesn't. And if you want sufficient coherence, you will need to narrow the linewidth by using a resonator with a grating (probably one in the grazing incidence configuration, or one in which the beam is expanded first before it reaches the grating). The simple grating setup shown in Scientific American isn't sufficient. This grating will reduce power drastically, and increase the complexity of the laser.

Honestly, I've given the matter a lot of thought, and I did intend to build a repetitively pulsed dye laser which I estimated to have a time averaged coherence length of approximately 5 centimeters. Even this modest amount of coherence involves an incredible amount of work and substantial expense. I've decided it's not worth it, and have given up.

Years ago I was quite determined to make a dye laser for holography. There was one paper in particular that caught my attention: "Narrow linewidth operation of a flashlamp-pumped dye laser" by J.H. Zhang, B.R. Hilty, and H.A. Schuessler from Texas A&M University. This was published in Volume 21, Number 17 (September 1982) of Applied Optics, pages 3065-7.

I've read many, many papers (and books) on dye lasers, including some where dye lasers were used for holography. But this is the one I wanted to use as a starting point, because I think the laser these authors describe could be used for holography, not as a "single pulse" laser like ruby, but essentially as a CW laser, except that the beam would consist of a series of mutually-coherent pulses at say 50 pulses per second (more or less). I realize this sounds odd (and improbable), but, according to the paper, they achieve a "multipulse" or time-averaged linewidth of 1.3 GHz. By comparison, a HeNe laser has a typical linewidth of 1.4GHz. They also state that the average power of their laser is 140mW, at 50 pulses per second, each pulse lasting 2 microseconds. (The peak power was 1.4 kW.)

I will quote the opening paragraph of the paper:

"Currently a pulsed due laser design using a grazing incidence mounted grating and a tuning mirror (references 1 & 2) or a second Littrow mounted grating (reference 3) is being used extensively for nitrogen laser and Nd:YAG laser pumped dye lasers. The pulse duration and the peak power of these tunable dye lasers are typically in the range of several nanoseconds and kilowatts. Since in some nonlinear spectroscopy experiments there is an important need to obtain more photons per laser pulse than in these lasers, a long pulse duration flashlamp-pumped dye laser at similar power levels is a good candidate to achieve this goal. So far a grazing incidence grating and mirror (reference 4) arrangement was used to tune a coaxial flashlamp-pumped dye laser thereby achieving a time-averaged linewidth of 2.4 GHz. This Letter describes a different laser geometry using both a grazing incidence grating and a Littrow mounted grating as well as an elliptical cavity for efficient flashlamp pumping. The two-grating combination, together with the small beam divergence produced by the dye capillary and several apertures inside the cavity, improves the time-averaged linewidth to 1.3 GHz. For single laser pulses a linewidth of <600 MHz is observed. The laser described operates with a repetition rate of more than 50 Hz without a loss of laser power."

A muffler? Good try but for a single lamp cavity the focal points of both sides of the ellipse needs to be at the center of the rod on one end the then center of the lamp on the other. Another thing I have not seen mentions is the power (capacitor) needed to flash these Xenon lamps. Pretty heavy duty and dangerous. Another consideration is how to tune and tweak the oscillator if you are only getting very short pulses at a single pulse per second or less (1Hz). You could speed up the repetition rate for tuning but then you would have to water cool the lamp and rod. Also, you would have to build a stable rail to hold the oscillator, the two mirrors, the KDP if used, the q-switch and any other components needed. Furthermore, anything that is varied like cavity temperature, pulse rate, mirror percentage reflectivity, capacitor parameter results in a different mode. Simply aligning the optics with an external laser may get it lasing but fine tweaking for holography has to be done with the real time beam. I used to build Pulsed Yag Lasers for Lenox Laser and design software to vary the repetition rate from 50pps to 1 pulse per 5 seconds with the option to fire one pulse only. They were water cooled and the power supply, including capacitors, was as big as a refrigerator. They were putting out about 2 to 5 joules each. Single cavity, dual cavity, osc/amp, frequency doubled, intra cavity attenuation (helps a lot to clean the beam) depending on your crystal, different mirror reflectivity percentages, length of oscillator, where cavity is in oscillator, etc... Many, many parameters to deal with.

I am not saying building your own pulsed system is impossible but if you are going to build your own system start with a known designed and matched cavity with crystal and lamp. And make sure your power supply matches your lamp. Make sure the crytal is suitable for holography in itself. Many are not. Then build your laser.
