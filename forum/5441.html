Tony Tue Mar 21, 2006 9:25 am #2
As you get above 5mw, it becomes very difficult to find a diode laser that is stable enough for holography. When you do figure out which ones are stable, they turn out to be expensive. For example, the Compass 315M is good, but they are around $1600 now from the surplus houses. ATI purportedly makes some stable high power diodes, but they too are expensive.

This is one reason why I have gone with HeNe's for red holography. They are power limited at around 35mw, but those are available in the $200-$300 price range. (See my post in 'For Sale'!).

My 100mw Wicked Laser came in promptly without problems with Chinese markings on the packet with it marked in English “pen light” ,-----very sensible too I thought, --rather than actually having it marked with the dreaded L word let alone “Wicked”. The fact that this “pen” was capable of visibly writing on clouds and mountain tops needed not to be gone into as far as officialdom was concerned!

Anyway it all worked and I decided to just put in 2 new AAA Duracells and run it in a clamp with a clothes peg holding down the ‘on’ button and then to see what sort of results I got from a simple Denisyuk reflection set up.

The wavelength I measured on a spectrometer as being approx 660nm

I arranged for some coins to be at different depths down to 2.5inches (6 cm).

After leaving it on for a half hour I made the first holo and saw no trouble with depth . However strong horizontal stripes ran across the image. These moved as you moved your viewing position. Symptomatic of “mode hopping” I understand. I ran another test 20 minutes later (ie. 50 mins after switch on.) This time the stripes were less but thicker--- only 2 stripes across the face of a quarter dollar instead of the 6 stripes I could count previously. After running for an hour my batteries were still in the OK green zone on my meter . Now an important point about the batteries is that unlike all the other laser pointers I have seen including my 20 mW 532nm, this one has the casing as NEGATIVE so you pop the batteries in with the positive down toward the head just as you would with a pen torch . There are no markings or instructions warning you of this.

So my first reaction is that you CAN make reflection holograms in principle with this laser, there is no particular problem with coherence length . The way to go about it I suppose is to try to run it from a well stabilized power supply at a lower voltage than specified so that it can still give a very impressive output of say 40mw instead of the nominal 100 with a chance of mode hopping not occurring but I cannot promise that at all. But you can always stick to using appropriate objects as in John’s post !

Tom B. Sun Apr 09, 2006 5:27 am #10
I think the stripes (aka "sliced bread" or "contouring") in holograms from high power diodes that I have seen are more likely due to simultaneous multimode operation than to mode hopping, which involves chaotically flipping back and forth between different single modes. As you note, it's possible (and I have found) that reducing the output power might reduce this, despite expert opinion which asserts that increased power reduces mode competition. Is it obvious whether the laser contains an automatic level control driver or is it just a simple resistor to limit current as found on many inexpensive pointers? The latter would be easier to deal with if an external adjustable power supply was to be used.

Tom, now this is an interesting point I have noticed too. Here is what I think, if you lower the power it is less likely to mode hop, but has a shorter coherence length. I don't really know why I think this but with my test that is what seemed to make the most sense.

Sliced bread can be the result of two modes competing or two modes during the exposure. If it jumps half way through the exposure the hologram will be indistinguishable from a hologram that had both lines running for the entire time.

So the only way I can think of to distinguish between the two is with a scanning Fabry-Perot interferometer or with a amplifier hooked up to a photo cell. You use the latter method I think perhaps you can enlighten me.
