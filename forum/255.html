First question: Probably, no. Second question: it shrunk because the diverging lens was probably placed too far away.

The collimating mirror is simply a lens "folded out". Consider a point source of light diverging and hitting a lens. Ignore, for this analysis whether or not the light expands around the lens (you can always iris it off). If the light source were placed at infinity, effectively about 10 focal lengths away, then the beam coming out of the lens would focus at the focal length of the lens - f away. As the point source approached the lens, the point where the light focuses on the other side of the lens moves away from the lens. When the point source is 2f away (twice the focal length), then the light focuses at 2f on the other side of the lens. Bring the source even closer and the focused spot moves even further away from the lens. Finally, when the light source is at the focal length at the back end of the lens, the light on the other side is collimated, ie it "focuses" at infinity.

Same with a mirror. Let's say the collimating mirror had a focal length of 25 in (if your mirror is 8 in and it's probably an f#3, it's focal length would be about 25 in). If you place the diverging lens (DL?) over 250 in away from the mirror, the light out of the mirror will focus at 25in. As you bring the DL (I assume that's what a DL is) towards the mirror, the focused spot moves away, but still focuses. Putting a card at some distance from the mirror will give a smaller disk of light than 8in because you're "catching" it in its way to focusing. When the DL is at 50 in from the mirror, the focused spot will also be at 50 in from the mirror (the "2f - 2f" relation). As you bring the DL closer to the mirror, the focused spot moves further out. Finally, when the DL is at 25 in, the beam is collimated.

As for the angle, circular mirrors are designed for on-axis illumination. If your DL was not on-axis (and it really can't be!), then the beam is distorted by an amount depending on the angle between the mirror and the DL. The greater the angle, the greater the distortion. However, the distortion makes the beam thinner (because that's the plane of the mismatch - the plane of the beams), so the height of the beam remains constant and it just gets thinner. If the height also changes, the mirror is not just reflecting side-to-side, it's pointing upwards by some small amount. This might throw off your polarisation. Of course, if your mirror is meant upwards, then the converse is true - the height changes and the width remains. By the way, a note of caution. The greater is this distortion, the greater is the distortion of the image. You should really not have an angle greater than a few degrees between DL and mirror axis.
