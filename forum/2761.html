I think it depends a lot on the fringe formation mechanism. DCG and polymers are continuous materials, while Silver is a "quantised" material. In silver the individual grains are affected.

[quote:caa6f2cbb9]Why is it that DCG has so little response to pulsed light xposure?[/quote:caa6f2cbb9]

This is one thing that led me to the question. I felt that there had to be some analogue to reciprocity in DCG. But DCG is continuous. As it hardens under expsoure, several continuous things happen. First, the increased overall hardness will increase the refractive index. This rate of change might affect the "future" development mode. If fringe formation is dependant on the rate at which the emulsion hardens, then the incredible short light pulse may exceed the rate at which hardnening of the emulsion can occur. Next, as fringes form, incoming light is diffracted away. This must have some affect , again, on fringe formation. since the ratio is dropping. Another factor, for a polymer chemist, is: Is the rate of cross-linking dependant on the already-crosslinked molecules. As I remember from my Chemical Kinetics, the rate of reaction is exponential, it's ratio of the reaction products to theconcentration of the initial reactants, thus as the product concentration rises, the rate slows. Is this true of polymerisation? Because, if so, the the rate at which the index changes is now also dependant on the rate at which polymerisation happens. I thought then that the initial curve would be exponential, but as the emulsion hardens, a secondary affect would "pull" the curve up so that it would be a hyperbole. All of this will depepnd on the dichromate/gelatin concentration. In Silver, there's a slight increase in the gelatin concentration since gelatin cross-links under energy input anyway, but the major affect is the release of electrons and the reduction of the halide on the crystal. As these released electrons started flying around inside the matrix, would they release further, non-actinic, electrons, thus causing "exposure"?

Obviously, you need to "tune" your stability to whatever curve the exposure is taking place. Under John's scheme, any movement at the beginning and end of the exposure is not as important as movement during the middle of it. If 80% of the fringes occur in the middle 20%, any movement there will be far more destructive than the first 10% of exposure.
