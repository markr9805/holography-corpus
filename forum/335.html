Dinesh Thu Sep 22, 2011 9:33 pm #2
> Johnfp: I was with a holographer that swore a miiscule of fringe movement helped.So if a fringe moves 1/10 of a micron at 532, in what geometry would that help?In other words, do the finges need to be 50/50 albeit sinusoidul, but what happens if they sway a bit one way or another?

I'm not sure what you mean by "what geometry would that help?", but generally speaking, it's a rule of thumb that in transmission holograms you can "get away" with 1/2 lambda and with reflection you should aim for 1/4 lambda or less. Is this what you meant? Ideally, of course, you should have no overlap at all.

Generally, think of the hologram as a set of lines with a sinusoidal variation. You're trying to take a photograph of those lines. Like any photograph, if the subject moves, you get blurring. The diffraction from the lines, if perfect, will exactly reproduce one of the two waves that created the lines in the first place. However, if the lines have moved as you photographed them, then the blurred lines are not an exact replica. The fuzzier the replica, ie the more the motion, the worse is the diffraction. But, like any photograph, a slight motion doesn't matter. If a friend took you picture and you moved a little, your friend would still recognise you, if you moved so much that the whole,picture is blurred, your friend won't recognise you.

How much is "too much"? It depends on the spatial frequency. The important point is that the actual motion is independent of the spatial frequency, and depends on the degree of motion. So, the lower the spatial frequency, then for a given motion, the less the deleterious effect.

So, let's say that the motion of a mirror or something causes a fringe shift of 1/2 a micron. In a transmission geometry with a HeNe at a reference angle of 30 degrees, the fringe separation is lambda/sin(theta) = 0.633/0.5 = 1.26 microns. A shift of 1/2 a micron is about 1/3 of a fringe separation (0.5/1.26 ~ 0.39) , leaving 2/3 of a fringe unaffected. You still have enough integrity as to not lose too much information. Now, shoot the same hologram in reflection with a ref at Brewsters. Now, the fringes are separated by 0.4 microns. Now a shift of 1 micron wipes out the entire fringe structure.

BobH Fri Sep 23, 2011 9:36 am #7
I'd say it depends on the recording material more than the geometry or spatial frequency of the system. Silver halide is very sensitive to fringe visibility. It takes very little light into the dark fringes to create latent image sites on the AgX crystals. Photopolymer is much less sensitive to fringe visibility, because it requires much more light to get molecules to cross link. Same with DCG.

If there's 1/4 wave of motion during the exposure, fringe visibility decreases just the same as if the beam ratio was increased. If one has a system set up that gives optimum results at beam ratio of 10 with perfectly stable fringes, it may give optimum results at beam ratio of 2 in the presence of fringe motion that degrades fringe visibility to a point equal to that in the previous example (assuming there's no banding or other artifacts from what caused the fringes to move). Regardless, I agree with Dinesh that there's never any time when any fringe motion is better for a recording than no fringe motion. The entire technical goal of an optical holographer is to create a fringe pattern in space, and hold it still for an exposure.

Dinesh, I forgot that you asked about the 1/20th of a fringe thing. I think the idea is as follows. When a fringe-locker is used, if the gain on the op-amp is set fairly low (with the potentiometer), then it produces what's called a weak lock or a loose lock. The fringes drift very slightly (almost imperceptibly) but then the photodiodes sense the movement, and the system kind of "nudges" the fringes back into position. > BobH: If there's 1/4 wave of motion during the exposure, fringe visibility decreases just the same as if the beam ratio was increased.

I never actually thought about it like that, ie that fringe visibility is connected to ratio. Motion tends to smear out or flatten out the peak of the sinusoid because the peak shifts. Ratio determines the height of the sinusoid. So I suppose if there was a small flattening out of the sinusoid would have the same effect as lowering the peak of the sinusoid.

Anyway, just out of curiosity, I did a quick back-of-an-envelope calculation. Here it is for anyone interested:

Motion causes a shift of the sinusoidal pattern. This is equivalent to adding a phase-shifted sinusoidal pattern to the original pattern. The sinusoidal pattern is actually a cos squared function. So, adding the original cos squared function to a phase shifted version of it results in:

Original pattern: (cos^2)(A) =(1/2)(cos(2A) + 1)

Phase shifted pattern: (cos^2)(A + phi) = (1/2)(cos(2(A+phi)) + 1)

Adding to give the final pattern on the plate: (1/2)(cos(2A) + 1) + (1/2)(cos(2(A+phi)) + 1) = (1/2)*(cos(2A) + cos(2A)cos(2phi) - sin(2A)sin(2phi)

If the motion is very small, phi is very small. In this case:

cos(2phi)~1 and sin(2phi) ~ 2phi

This gives: 2 cos(2A)/2 - 2(phi)sin(2A)

If you subtract the two, you get (1/2)cos(2A) _ (1/2) - 2phisin(2A) = (cos^2)(A) - 2(phi)sin(2A)

Giving you back the original pattern, with a small modulated sin wave sitting on top of the main sinusoidal fringe pattern.
