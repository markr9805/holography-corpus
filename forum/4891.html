jean Thu Jun 09, 2005 5:29 pm #5
I've tried the double-jet method and frankly, imho, the diffusion method is as far as I know, better (at least for hobbyiests). With the doubje-jet method, you must deal with grain grown problem, you need a grain grown restrainer but quality and source of the gelatin play a role too. You need a solution of low concentration gelatin (1 or 2%) to get suffisantly small grains but coating is less easy (of course, you can add gelatin after rippening to raise concentration and then use curtain veil or meyer bar coating but this operation can produce grain growing. And last but not least, hardening of gelatin can be a challenge.

As you can guess, I didn't enjoy results of my several tests of double-jet method. Maybe someone else should have better results than I, I hope.

But for me, it's definitively the diffusion method who get my preference.

Joe Farina Thu Jun 09, 2005 6:52 pm #6
The diffusion method has always been of interest to me, but I decided to pursue the double-jet method because the diffusion method involves three applications of liquid material, and three drying cycles for the emulsion:

1. Coat with gelatin, dry

2. Apply nitrate, dry

3. Apply bromide/dye and sensitizer, dry

Hopefully others who have tried the diffusion method can clarify this (I haven't tried it yet) but it seems to me (and for my purposes) that there are too many opportunities for irregularities in the emulsion to develop. I think Dr. Blyth once stated that his process was designed for people who wanted to do it for the fun of making their own silver-halide holographic emulsion. I don't think it was ever claimed that it could routinely produce flawless emulsion layers, which is essentially what we would expect with a commercial emulsion.

I would hazard a guess that all commercial holographic emulsions are produced (and have been produced) by the double-jet method.
