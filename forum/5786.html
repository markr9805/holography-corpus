OK, here's the background:

I have had consistent good reults with my table setup using exposures up to 90 seconds long. However, I'm not certain that the long term stability is good enough for exposures longer than 90 seconds.

I'm working on the PDG (Porcelain Dog Group) contest hologram, and I have an H1 I want to copy but it is pretty dim. So dim that I will need a 3 minute exposure when I copy it onto BB. Will it work? I can make 60 second test exposures on 8E75 (faster).

Here's the hypothesis: long term table instability (drift) will cause a loss in brightness, due to loss of fringe contrast.

Experiment:

Control hologram - 60 second H2 copy exposure.

Experimental hologram - two separate 30 second exposures made 3 minutes apart. The idea is that if the table drifts in three minutes, the net exposed fringe contrast will be degraded.

Control & experiment holograms otherwise processed identically, etc.

Result: Both holograms are of equal brightness.

Conclusion: Long term table stability is good enough for 3 minute exposures.

Any comments, disagreements, errors?

MichaelH Sun Sep 17, 2006 3:59 am #4
Good for you!

For those of us who can't afford labs of equipment like wler but do have a video camera I highly recommend doing time-lapse videos of your table with an interferometer running.

By projecting the fringes onto some graph paper it become much easier to see what your table does over time when you're out of the room.

Then take the next step and get two cheap walkie-talkies and walk around your environment opening and closing doors of all kinds, turning on appliances etc and seeing what, if any, affect that has on the fringes while you describe what you're doing. This will be invaluable six months from now when you can't quite remember all the little details of the last time you tested your table. I've found that one critical part to my being able to do exposures of minutes is to make sure the AC is turned off upstairs as the pressure changes in the house cause my tubes to change shape.

Joe Farina Thu Sep 21, 2006 10:53 am #7
For the electronics-minded people among us, there is a circuit diagram of a fringe counter in The Laser Cookbook by Gordon McComb (on page 140 of the first edition). He gives detailed instructions for building one, and it looks very inexpensive. The detector (a phototransistor) is placed in the fringe pattern, and it automatically counts the fringes which pass over the detector in a given time period, with a digital readout. I think this would be an extremely useful tool for anyone who wants to improve table stability.
JohnFP Thu Sep 21, 2006 11:01 am #8
It would be good to baseline drift but would not be beneficial for vibrations, ie. the fringes would vibrate then go back to their original location.
Joe Farina Thu Sep 21, 2006 11:22 am #9
> JohnFP: It....would not be beneficial for vibrations, ie. the fringes would vibrate then go back to their original location.

It would be good for vibrations, too. It doesn't matter how fast the fringes go past, it would still count them, up to 9,999 according to the circuit diagram.

To quote from the book:

"....A counter circuit can be used to count the number of light-to-dark or dark-to-light transitions of the shifting fringe pattern, even if the fringes move several thousand times per second."

Gross vibrations would drastically reduce the accuracy of the counter, according to McComb. But I don't think this would matter much for our purposes. The only problem would be that it's only good for one distubance over a given time period. Video monitoring would be better in the sense that any number of disturbances could be seen.
JohnFP Thu Sep 21, 2006 1:05 pm #10
Oh, I see. I guess the only thing it would not register is a movement of 1/4 wavelength or so, or where ever the light intensity would not drop enough to signal a count. It sounds like a nice tool.

I was thinking some time ago about in addition to fringe locking add an additional single photo cell with separate electronics. Place this cell in the Fringe Locking Michelson fringes along with the fringe locker photo cells. The electronics on this single cell would be easy and would be placed in the lightest part of the fringe. It works in such a way that when the fringes move the shutter is closed. When the fringes return to their bright spot, threshold being calibrated with a pot in the electronics, the shutter would open back up. A built in delay after closing would insure the shutter does not go crazy opening and closing with a vibration movement.

The reason for this, and it comes from years and countless hours of aligning fringe lockers is, even if the fringe locker is dead on, there are cases where the fringes may jump or move. If the locker is tweaked well, the fringes should come back to where they were. And I have also seen many times where the fringe shift a half a fringe for a few moments and then shift back to where they were.

I feel this additional device may increase exposure time but would help in that ultimate goal of preserving perfect fringes during long exposures.

It would also be beneficial to those set ups where something mechanical needs to move on the table over the duration of an exposure or multiple exposures.
