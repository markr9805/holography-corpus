Ed Wesly Wed Jun 25, 2008 8:35 pm #2
Mag wrote:

> But the fringes on the back side of the plate -after developing them- became quite different.

I am guessing that what you are calling fringes are the bullseyes and other coherent artifacts caused by dust and dirt on your optics, and over the time between shooting sessions stuff moved around. These patterns just get in the way of viewing the image, so try to keep things clean or even splurge and get a spatial filter.

The business of keeping the reference beam at 45 degrees is so that when you view the image you don't get hit with the replay beam. This was the big stumbling block of the original holograms of Dennis Gabor. The light that doesn't get changed into image when you light up the hologram but passes through the plate is another example of the zero order beam.

Yes, the BB plates could be used for making the open aperture copy, and they could be used for pinhole photography if you want to make exposures on the order of hours! Those plates are probably about 1000 times more light thirsty than photographic paper!

It does not look like wood grain which is caused from internal reflections in the glass when the plate is not at 56 degrees from the normal and it does not look the internal reflections you get from the light hitting the edge of the plate. But it may be that either your spatial filter for your reference beam is not tuned properly or some light from your raw object beam is hitting the plate. or you object reflects light funny, or something else on the table is reflecting light to the plate.

What is the object you are holographing?

Put a white card in where the film usually goes. Block the Object beam so just light from the reference beam is hitting the card. Do you see fringes? You should not. If you do, you need to tweak in your spatial filter a little better. You should see a nice, even, smooth distibution of light.

Now, block the light of your reference beam and unblock the light for the object beam. Keep the white card in the holder. Make sure the raw object beam does not hit the card. You should see a faint fairly even light hitting the card which is the light being reflected off the object. Do you see those fringes now? You should not.

Now unblock both the object beam and the reference beam. Do you see the fringes now? You should not

You should not see fringes at all on the white card. If you do, something is out of tune with spatial filter or there is light hitting the plate from somewhere other then just the object and reference beam.

> Mag: i really appreciate your help guys.i have another question, its a bit off topic, but maybe you can give me a hand. Try to find a large black piece of foam board or card board, maybe about 45cm x 60cm. You can paint one side of the cardboard black. Then make a hole in the center just large enough for the lense of your camera to fit through. Now mount the camera and black card board on a tripod or other rigid stand.

Not take your hologram and lean it against a black surface. Again black painted card board will work. Place the back cardboard on the back wall and the floor where the hologram is to be photographed. Lean the hologram sitting on the black cardboard and leaning against the black cardboard. Adjust light for best reconstruction. Now take a white piece of paper about the size of your hologram and place it a couple cm away from the hologram, leaning against the wall like the hologram.

Now, what you do is to focus the camera on the white piece of paper by holding down the shutter button half way, then when it locks in light level and focus, slightly move the camera to view the hologam while still holding the button down half way. Then finish depressing the shutter button and you will capture the imgae very cleanly.

The idea is the black card board around the camera keeps down your reflection on the hologram. The white piece of paper next to the hologram is used for adjusting light level and focus. If the picture comes out to bright then focus on center of paper. If picture comes out too dark, then focus half way on the white paper and half way on the black background. By adjusting the white paper closer or further from wall, you can lock in which ever focus depth you want.

Hope this helps.

Hello again Mag,

Difficulty photographing holograms?

You're not the first. It's a chore.

You didn't mention what type of 'gram you are attempting to capture to film/CCD - but - in 99% of the cases of difficulties, it's focal issues related to autofocus.

My folks have a Sony Cybershot as well, and I really thought that camera was just about the pinnacle of digital photograph capture devices, but truth be told, that infernal contraption needs to be totally coaxed into capturing a quality image of a reflection hologram.

From what I glean from your posts to this point, though, I get the impression you are burning transmission holograms - which adds an additional twist to the act of capturing images of your glory. Digital cameras get WAY CONFUSED by laser light. That fuzzy quality to the light....? That strange coherent self-interference fuzz that brings every surface to life leaves a digital camera scratching its lil' digital head....

Your eyes may note (and probably do) that movement amplifies the effect.

Your only hope for capturing a photo of a transmission hologram (diffracted laser light) is with a perfectly stationary camera.

Try this: Replay your transmission holo with a clean beam (spatial filter) Not too bright. If necessary, attenuate your replay beam somewhat so the image doesn't glare. A dimly-lit or darkened room helps your camera not get confused as far as light levels. Make sure the flash is disabled. Set your camera to Auto. Set your camera to Macro. Set the timer. Set your camera down about 8 inches away from your plate. Taking the shot with the timer shutter delay eliminates any motion.

There are more tricks you will have to learn when you start trying to capture reflection holos, such as fooling the autofocus with a white card... Cross that bridge when you come to it.

Justin W Wed Jul 02, 2008 1:16 pm #41
The real image is only in focus within a finite little region.

Start with a white card very close to the film, move it away from the hologram - at some point the smudge of light that is your diffracted real image will suddenly sharpen into the detail that is the very front of the image. moving the card farther will resolve details of the image from front to back until it dissolves into a blur again.

If you absolutely cannot find this image, it is possible that you do not have the film oriented properly in the beam. This term "flipped" can be dodgy. For a hologram with an overhead reference beam, you will pitchthe film 180 degrees. For a hologram with a side reference beam, you will need to yawthe film 180 degrees in order to reconstruct the real image.

Incidentally, this real image - in it's little swath of space it occupies - is the image that is used when doing an H1/H2 transfer. An "image-planed" hologram could be made by inserting fresh film right there in the space where the image is sharp on the white card. That "object", made of pure light, hanging out there in free space, allows a holographer to transfer their original image to a second piece of film without having to work around troublesome object beams and solid matter.
Colin Kaminski Wed Jul 02, 2008 3:55 pm #42

Oh!

I see...

You're replaying your hologram with a raw beam?

Careful, careful! If you haven't already, then you are likely to: Burn a little brown dot in your hologram

I, myself am a beginning holographer, so the most technical my uneducated @ss can get as far as identifying those scattered images is to say that you are seeing (I believe) the different orders of diffraction. Plain old diffraction grating will do this to a laser beam as well - you will have your zero-order beam either transmitted or reflected (depending on whether the grating is reflective or transmissive), plus the ascending orders of diffraction arranged from brightest to dimmest beside the zero-order.

I don't know the math behind it, but can say that the spacing of these different orders of diffraction is based upon the wavelength of the light and the spacing of the grating. If you research diffraction gratings some, I'm certain you can satisfy any curiosity you have regarding this.

Anyway... As dangerous as it is to eyes and emulsions, you can have some fun playing a raw beam around on a transmission hologram

You will note that as the beam hits different parts of the film, the image will replay as viewed from that one dot of film - very cool to watch - you can make your projected image appear to tumble as the beam moves. Fun stuff, but not all that useful.

Ideally, your transmission hologram would have been made with a collimated reference beam (spatial filter shining on parabolic mirror so that it is turned into a parabolic-mirror sized tube of light), then replayed with that same collimated beam - Colin spoke of the conjugate - the conjugate of a collimated beam is a collimated beam ... When replayed under these conditions, a holographer sees a perfect real image, neither shrunken nor enlarged, and in focus in space and ready to copy.

If you shot your holograms with a diverging beam (spreading from the pinhole), you can still see a real image by "flipping" the holo in the original reference beam. It should be visible and easy-enough to find. It just won't be the perfect image for transferring - it will shrink/expand because the hologram you have created is something of a fresnel lens with its focal properties decided by the divergence of the original reference beam. You will most likely note these focal difficulties in the virtual image as well - moving the pinhole closer/farther will change the size of the image.

Anyhow, my friend, those last two paragraphs are just to say, raw beam play is fun, but plans involving quality transfers will be based upon spread beams.

You're doing great! I was months-deep into holography before I even had decent holograms to play with. Keep up the good work.
