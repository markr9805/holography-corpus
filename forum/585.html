Dinesh Fri Jul 20, 2012 1:15 pm #2
These kind of questions depend on a large number of factors, so it's impossible to give any precise set of figures.

When you record the hologram, you're recording the Bragg planes and/or the fringe structure on the surface. So, the first level of dependance is: is this a surface hologram (eg resist) or a volume hologram? If volume, is this a reflection hologram or a transmission hologram? Then you need to determine/know: what is the spatial frequency of your recording as a fraction of the spatial frequency response of the medium?

For example, if you're shooting a reflection hologram, on a silver halide plate of dimension 4x5, of an object that's 3 inches in diameter, placed about 6 inches away with a reference angle at Brewster's, with a HeNe (a common geometry), then the spatial frequency recorded by the edges of your object will be 2322 l/mm, while the centre of your image records 2369 l/mm. Most modern silver halide emulsions can record 3000 l/mm to about 5000 l/mm, so you're pretty close to the edge of the frequency response. So, let's say that you're recording on a material that can take up to 5000 l/mm. This means that the efficiency starts to fall off at about 4000 l/mm (this depends on the grain density and a whole bunch of other factors, but, as a rule of thumb, the response is pretty flat for about 80% of the frequency range and then falls rapidly). So, let's say that you record this 3" object placed 6" away with a HeNe referenced at 40, 50, 60 and 70 degrees. The spatial frequencies for these angles are:

angle (degrees) spatial frequency (l/mm) fringe separation (nm)

40 ........................ 2789 ........................ 300

50 ........................ 2595 ........................ 385

60 ........................ 2369 ........................ 422

70 ........................ 2120 ........................ 471

You can see that the fringes are getting "fatter", but the spatial frequency is dropping. This means that in this range you'd need to expose longer as you decrease the angle from 70 to 40, since you're getting close to the edge of the frequency response. The drop needs to follow the drop of the frequency response curve which is usually close to a falling sinusoidal. If it matters, I'd drop the exposures as a square function, but to each his own! Other holographers take it down linearly and the experienced holographers simply pick a number that "feels right".

If you're doing a transmission hologram on silver halide, then typically your frequencies are in the range of about 1000 to 1500 (shooting a transmission at at Brewsters with a HeNe gives a spatial frequency of 1368 l/mm). This is well below the extremes of the spatial frequency range, so there's no need to drop the exposure times, you can keep them all the same. This is assuming, of course, that you're not thinking of shooting anything above about 5 or so holograms on the same emulsion. As the number goes up to the 10s and beyond, you start getting reciprocity failure problems and so above 5 or so you have to then decrease the exposures.

The variation in differences between reference angles again depends on the spatial frequency range and on the development procedure. When you shoot a hologram, you have a "latent image" recorded. This means that the complete set of fringes are already "in the plate", but at very low modulations. There's less than 1% difference between Bragg plane and not-Bragg plane. This small difference is amplified by about 10,000 by the development process. But, the development process also swells the plate and causes the Bragg planes to swell. If the plane structures are too close together, you won't see much difference between the two angles you exposed. More than likely, you'll get one image surrounded by a halo of noise. A good "rule of thumb" is to set the next angle so that the difference in frequency is about 10%. In the above example, going from a ref of 50 to a ref of 60 gives a percentage difference in frequencies of

(2595 - 2359)/2595 = 0.091, or a 9% difference in frequencies.

If you're intending to do pseudocolour, ie swell the plate between exposures, then there's a whole different regime for successive exposures. You now need to calculate the new spatial frequency of the swelled plate (the plane separation has increased on swelling, so the frequency goes down).

If you're doing dcg, you have to remember that the material is continuous.

Note comment on reciprocity, because this too has a bearing on exposure times, especially for particular long exposures or pulse shots.

Dinesh Mon Jul 23, 2012 12:36 pm #5
> holomark: If I am shooting on silver halide plate of dimension 4x5 and my exposure requirement is for example 250uW (for a typical single beam reflection) at 50 degrees- what would I be my target exposure be if I wanted to make two exposures - 1 at 50 degrees and 1 at 130 degrees? In other words should each exposure be 250 uW or should the total of the two equal 250uW?

My first reaction is that 250 uW sounds a little high! But, assuming this is right, I'm not sure what you mean by a "single beam reflection" at 50 and then another exposure at 130. If this is a Denisyuk at 50, then is the 130 exposure mean you're bringing the beam over to the other side of the plate while keeping the object in the same place? A single beam Denisyuk and a transmission H1 on the same plate?

In any case, in this geometry, I'd shoot the single beam at 50 at about 300 and the 130 exposure at about 200 (assuming it's an H1 now).

The thing to understand is that when you make an exposure, you're creating a set of fringes which have, at their peak, a higher density than the surrounding material; somewhat like a scene with rolling hills. The difference in height between the top of the hill and the "ground level" is the index modulation. So, let's assume that you're looking at a flat field. Now you "expose". Hills begin to appear on the field, but, at the same time, the average level of the whole field also rises. If the hills are appearing and rising faster than the entire field, you've got good modulation and a good bright hologram (noise aside). But, if the average level of the field is rising almost as fast as the hills, the the effective height of the hills above the ground level is not that large. The modulation is not that good and the hologram is weak. The trick is to expose it in such a manner that you minimise the increase of index in the ground level, while maximising the index of the peak. This is the reason for the ratio, if you get your ratio right, you'll achieve this juggling act of minimising one while maximising the other. However, when you expose twice, and I'm assuming that the ratio for each shot is different if indeed you're shooting a reflection and a transmission, then you have to balance the increase with the ground level index with the increase of the hilltop index. Also, the plate needs about 250 uW of energy to react chemically with the light to begin with. If the exposure is too low, there'll be no actinic reaction - the hills won't rise at all! So, you need somewhere around the film sensitivity of light to have an actinic reaction, but you can go a little below and a little above. I'd go slightly under with the transmission and slightly over with the reflection.

Ed Wesly Tue Jul 24, 2012 9:42 am #8
One of the best papers written on the subject was by Stephen Hart, of the Voxel holographic technique. But I don't have a copy handy to relate its highlights.

But I cannot stress enough that exposure testing will yield plenty of practical results. As usual I must plug my web site, nlutie.com/ewesly to review my exposure testing series set up.

What is needed, and can find by testing, is the threshold and saturation points of your materials and processing scheme, i.e., the minimal exposure when the image first appears, and when the plate gets overloaded with information. A quick and dirty solution would be to simply divide the maximal exposure by the number of desired exposures to give the exposure for each.

As an example, my old experiences with Agfa 8E75HD showed that the material gets no brighter but plenty noisy at about 400 microJoules per square centimeter, and just an inkling of an image at 25 - 50 of those units. (Single Beam Reflections, CWC2 developer for 2', CWPBQ2 bleach) So you could conceivably put 16 or 8 of the lesser expos on the plate. But usually 2 or 3 is all that is required.

Sometimes the two expos might not be equal, like if you are blending colors or varying relative intensities. As another example, I ran an expo test on Sphere-S GEO-3 plates of my test object at 633 nm. Then I used half of the max exposure as the basic expo on a plate, then ran an exposure test at 532 nm to see if I could get a decent yellow.

The bottom line is, keep your processing constant, and run organized tests to sneak up on the peak exposures for all those images you put on the plate.
