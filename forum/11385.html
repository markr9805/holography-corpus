Joe Farina Sun Nov 03, 2024 2:03 pm #1
So far in my experience with holography, I've been able to make DCG coatings with minimal amounts of dust, but only dust-free (or reasonably so) only rarely.

The dust-free ones are apparently due to luck. I'm using a homemade hepa/laminar flow enclosure with Mayer bars. Part of my problem may be that the enclosure is getting old (~20 years) and it's not possible to clean it thoroughly without re-building it. I seem to recall getting better results when it was new. 

I was wondering, especially with regards to professional HOE makers, what the preferred method is for producing dust-free coatings. I assume that in the field of HOE's, there would be a low tolerance level for mistakes to allow dust to settle on the surface. I assume that such professionals use spin coating in some type of clean environment.

I may be re-building the coating area based on spin-coating, with a new hepa/laminar flow enclosure. Any tips would be greatly appreciated.

Din Mon Nov 04, 2024 1:35 pm #3
Our predecessor, Don Broadbent - a dcg holographer going back to the 70's, and from whom we got most of our practical knowledge - set up an enclosure about 5' wide, about 3' tall and about 2' deep with a floor about 5' above the ground (I think on a bench but I don't remember exactly due to advanced senility and it being 8 years ago!) in a small room with a HEPA which became our coating room. Don immersed the gel-filled beaker in a slow cooker set at his temperature, to which he added the dichromate. We took over his lab and tried this, but were not too happy with it; we used a hot plate with a magnetic stirrer which melted the gel and dissolved the dichromate uniformly. Joy drilled a hole at the centre of a glass disc about the size of the top of the beaker just large enough to take a thermometer. This way, we could cover the beaker as the gel melted and the dichromate dissolved, but, at any time, we could check the temperature.

At any rate, Don's enclosure was open in the front, with a wooden floor that Don simply placed the coated plates on. Joy put a thick glass plate about 6" above and parallel to the floor, making sure it was horizontal with a bubble level, set on three adjustable screws. She then placed a large sheet of plastic over the opening of the enclosure, thus protecting the plates from dust. In addition, she put a standard cooker hood on the roof of the enclosure. She then put a series of pennies in a 2x2 grid (one coin per corner of plate), one set for each plate (NIST approved spacers, she called them). The point of the pennies would be to prevent any gloop from overspilling and sticking the plate to the glass. We usually coated on Friday afternoon, then put the "wet" plates on the pennies and turned on the hood. Any dust getting around the plastic sheet would get sucked up by the cooker hood. We'd leave this over the weekend, and shoot on Monday.
