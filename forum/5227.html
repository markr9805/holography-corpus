Joe Farina Wed Nov 30, 2005 8:22 pm #4
Now that's what I call a table!

Granite, 20cm thick (~8 inches) X 130cm wide (~4 feet) X 310cm long (~10 feet).

I wouldn't dare criticize this table in any respect, except for weight!

My view on the subject is that, while 8-inch thick granite doesn't leave anything to chance in terms of stiffness, the incredible weight makes it rather impractical, to say nothing of the cost.

I decided to use a small granite table, which is only 18 inches wide, and 4 feet long. The thickness is only about 1 inch. However, the most important part of my table is the steel structure supporting the granite, which is very substantial. This consists of rectangular steel pipe which is held together (under a lot of tension) by threaded tie rods going through their centers. The shape of this structure resembles a ladder, so the granite has plenty of support. This frame is firmly bolted to six two-inch steel pipe fixtures (with female threads), and the six two-inch pipes that I use for legs (with male threads) are screwed into them. (Sorry for my choice of words, but nothing else fit!). A racquetball rests under each leg for vibration isolation. As long as the steel frame maintains its rigidity, I figure that the granite will remain reasonably straight and stiff, considering the fact that I don't place any heavy weights on top of it.

Tables are a rather "personal" issue, and there are many factors to consider, based on the requirements of the holographer, available materials, cost, etc. These are just my personal views.

Colin Kaminski Thu Dec 01, 2005 1:59 pm #12
I agree with Joe, tables are very personal, I have worked on tables ranging in size from 2x4' to 5x12' made from everything you can imagine.

If you are only going to do single beam work a small table is quite simple. The ones Bill designed at CCAC are perfect for a small space. It is a 2x4' cabinet with casters and lock down feet. The top is a thin sheet of steel. The laser rests in v-blocks and aims at a 6mm concave mirror. The beam is aimed up at a small stand made from 3/4" x 3/4" wood. Top top of the stand is 8"x10" and has a glass plate. The reference beam shines up through the glass plate.

The holo-plate is placed on top of the glass plate and the object to be recorded is placed ontop of that. since the object rests on the plate it is very stable and requires no dampning or stiffness. The tables did rest on the ground floor concrete. However I think this would adapt to an upstairs envrionment.
