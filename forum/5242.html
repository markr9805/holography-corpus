JohnFP Wed Dec 07, 2005 8:24 pm #2
I used a manual shutter for years before I made a remote shutter.

After you load your plate or film let it settle for 15 minutes. The two main things that will destroy your hologram are vibrations and air movement. Both of which are very minimal in a Denisyk hologram especially if the plate is leaning or lying right on the object. When you move the shutter (or blocker used for a shutter) out of the way do it very slowly as to not disturb the air too much and have everyone hold very still and hold their breath (assuming your exposure is within breath holding capacity).

JohnFP Thu Dec 08, 2005 5:54 pm #6
With the set up in the drawing, the laser and light DOES have to be leaned. This will give you the best replay when looking at is so that the light comes in from above the hologram when you are looking at it.

Does the school have a camera tripod that tilts. That way you can mount the laser and lens on the tripod and adjust height, tilt, etc... Put the laser and lens close enough such that the beam expands just enough to cover the plate.

The black tape covers the edge of the glass. The edge of the glass will act like a fiber optic and if laser light gets into the edge it will bounce back and forth inside the glass and make a lots of shiney hologram lines. You do not want this, I would not think.

Definitely open the box in complete darkness or a very dim safe light specifically for those plates. They can be ruined very easy like black and white unexposed negative film.

Others on this forum can help you more and are more familliar with Silver Halide.

Gary Deal Fri Dec 09, 2005 4:33 am #8
Ok, so almost no equipment, a restroom next door, and a physics instructor that doesn't know holography, doesn't think it will work, and wants a Yoda of all things. Next he'll want you to do it with both feet tied behind your back.

With an understanding of what is required one can often figure out what can be ignored, and making a simple hologram can be little more complicated than shining a light on something for a few seconds - under the right circumstances. It's those must-have and can-ignore details that make all the difference.

It's time to use some non-physics related skills in order to get your physics project to work.

It's generally better to do your exposure at night when darkness is easier and there's nobody walking the halls, but that's not always possible.

Do you know the janitor/custodian that deals with that restroom? Ask him if he can put a "closed for cleaning" sign on the restroom door during the time you make your exposures, even stand in front of the door if necessary. Tell him you'll try to make a hologram of some keys for him if he'll help, and be sure to try to make one for him. Even if your images aren't very good, show them to him. Being a custodian doesn't mean he doesn't know anything. He may also be able to get you things that aren't immediately obvious - like a large flat paving stone if you can't get a granite slab, and useful small metal pieces.

You're going to need darkness and preferably a proper safelight. Do you know anyone that does photo darkroom work? Borrowing a safelight with a Wratten #3 filter (very dark green) might be helpful to keep from tripping over things in the dark. They'll probably have a tripod as well.

Examine the laser. Is it in a long black tube, a rectangular box, or an unprotected glass tube? If it's a black tube, does it have a mount that it fits into? If it's a box, check for a threaded hole in the bottom, for tripod mounting. If it's a glass tube, it will need an enclosure to keep stray light inside. If the laser belongs to some other department, go talk to the ones that normally use it about mounting options.

If there's no mounts and nobody knows anything, you'll have to pull out some engineering skills. And a cardboard box, a knife, scissors, and some adhesive tape. You'll probably need these anyway. I can explain a quick and dirty setup that should work, though it offers no vibration isolation. Cardboard construction should provide additional surprise for your physics instructor, but the corrugated cardboard box material is particularly "dead" and doesn't move around much as long as it's dry.

If the laser is an unprotected glass tube wired to a power supply, do not forget that there is enough electricty there to kill you. Quickly.

I'm still not really clear on what you have to work with or can get hold of, but I have a long history of performing the unlikely with things I pulled from the trash heap so I might be able to help fill in some of the gaps.

Have faith not only in holography, but in yourself and your ability to coordinate the requirements of the project. If you have a laser, holographic plates & chemistry, and a lens to spread the beam, chances are that everything else you actually need are at hand if you only take the time to see them.

Covering the edge of the glass plate is good to eliminate internal reflections, but you may learn a couple of things by letting them get in on a plate or two.
