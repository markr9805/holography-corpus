To be honest, I've never seen one, or heard of one. Has anyone made a split-beam reflection hologram with three wavelengths? The holograms made by Bjelkhagen and Gentet are the simple Denisyuk type.

Maybe the reason is because of serious practical problems. For example, if the lasers are off the table, then putting all three beams through a spatial filter would be extremely difficult, in my opinion.

If the lasers are on the table, then vibrations are very much a concern when it comes to water-cooled ion lasers. As John pointed out, a high level of table/component stability is required for split-beam holograms, as compared to the single-beam holograms as done by Bjelkhagen and Gentet.

This is slightly off-topic, but this is a picture of the set-up for a three color transmission hologram, using three lasers. I am passing all three beams through a microscope objective on the left. For reference, I am bringing the three beams from different angles onto the recording plates. The different angles mean that the volume effect of the emulsion works against getting false images, and in any case they would be away from the single 3-color image.

The exposure was simultaneous, and on two plates, Agfa 8E56 and 8E75.

Of course the same reference beams would be required to see the image, so it is hardly a practical method, but it was just an exercise to see what quality of color I could get.

Ed Wesly Tue Apr 12, 2005 1:17 am #5
I have done it. Photons supplied by an S-P 125 for red, a Coherent CR-2 for argon 515 green, and an S-P 166 for 476 blue.

The geometry was set up for backlit objects; a groundglass was placed parallel to the plateholder about 20 mm behind it; the reference came in at 45 degrees from the top, and another beam impinged upon the groundglass from its behind. A schematic of the set up would look like a parallelogram, with the plateholder at one of the obtuse corners

A broadband polarizing beamsplitting cube was used to divide the beams, and each laser had its own 1/2 wave plate to control the beam ratio. A Fresnel Rhomb, which is an achromatic polarization rotator was used after the cube to rectify the object beam to the same polarization vector as the reference.

I made a test target using ROYGBIV + magenta Roscolux filters and the words LAKE FOREST COLLEGE HOLOGRAPHY WORKSHOPS, (since I wasworking there) which were mounted upside down for the recording exposure and then flipped pseudoscopic for real image replay fun.

Exposures were on Agfa 8E75HD, 8E56HD plain and with AHI, Ilford Green plates and some Bulgarian Academy of Sciences HP490. (Anti-Halation Intra-emulsion, not a backing but an anti-scatter dye. It was always thought that this would prevent the material from being used in a reflection scheme, but it helped by increasing fringe contrast by reducing scatter!)

None of the Agfa products could make a single beam reflection hologram using 515, much less 476. The Ilford was designed to do just that, but it still had a problem with 476. The Bulgarian plates worked really well. I used CWC2 developer and bleach on these plates, the first "Soviet-style" emulsion I got to play with, and it worked surprisingly well!

But this back lit two beam scheme was magic! Every one of these emulsions could make green and blue images! The Bulgarian plates were the best, and the next best was the Agfa 8E56HD AHI, even better than Ilford!

I would make a series of exposures on all the materials at all the wavelengths to see how they reacted, like what was their threshold etc. The best results were sandwiches of Bulgarian with 8E75HD, but surprisingly this Agfa material could record a true color image!

Inspecting its spectral sensitivity curve a dip at 490 nm is found, but it has sensitivity at 515, about 1/4 of 633 nm. (Is green a really safe light for it?) And of course it has to have some sensitivity in blue.

These images were noisy as to be expected but decently bright. They were not anywhere near as good as what could be expected with today's materials, but the addition of the object light with a real 1:1 beam ratio really helped!

I don't know if this answers the question exactly, as the object was not a front-lit three-dimensional one. But yes, it has been done, I am not so sure of the fate of the holograms, I might have one or two in my basement in boxes that have remained unopened for years, or there might be some in the lab at LFC. But that extra light really made all the difference in the world!

I believe part of the art of "true color" holography is not so much as using the correct wavelengths (most of the time that cannot be controlled) but in matching the paints to your wavelengths. A lot of work and tests but worth it. I tried to contact a couple paint and pigment manufacturers and see if they had spectral charts on their paints and pigments, and none did. Some could tell me a very vague range but I don't think any spectral studies on paints have been performed to any detail.
