So, I have been ponder, as I often do, what exactly is the nature of a holographic pixel? That is, what is in a cubic 1/2 micron or so physically that enables that small area to recreate every point it sees on an object.

For a reflection hologram I guess the answer is simple. That cubic pixel is a 3-d model of the object but coated with a mirror. Obviously it's not really a mirror in DCG or a bleached silver hologram but actually a shell of the surface of the object with anther shell of the surface of the object behind it of a different index of refraction. So when light is shone on that cube with the shell, the light is reflected out as that shell.

Example:

We have a ball we make a hologram of. The front surface (the visable part by the film) of the ball actually exists as a shell in the cubic 1/2 micron area of that film. Now for simplicity (I mentioned before it is more likely a difference of index of refraction of two shells (positive and negative interfernce)) lets say that shell is coated as a mirror. Then when light hits on that ball, head on and our eye is head on, we only get reflection of the very leading perfectly perpedicular point on that ball shell. All the other spots on the shell are refelcted at some angle not perfectly perpedicular. This is important for the cubic area to the far right of the ball because it is reflecting not the center of the ball as is our first cubic area, but the edge of the ball. Now we move the light or our eyes and the first cubic area is now refelcting the edge of the ball while the cubic area that was reflecting the edge is now reflecting the center of the ball.

Now take this analogy and apply it to every point on an object and you see we have a full object shell in a 1/2 micron spot.

That is my understanding of holographic pixel or as we so commonly refer to it....a fringe.

Let's say I wanted to build a hologram molecule by molecule.

We will assume in using the word dense below, it mean a different index of refraction.

I would take some dense gelatin and form it in the shape of a shell of the part of the object that will be the front on the scale of the wavelength of light I want it to reconstruct. Then I mix up a some gelatin that is ever so slightly a little less dense then the first shell, form it in the shell of the part of the object again that will be the front . Now I take this second shell an fit it into the first shell. I do this again with a little less dense gelatin and again and so on until I have reached the limit of how less dense I want the material to get. These layers add up to 1/2 wavelenght. Then I repeat the proceedure but start makeing the gelatin a little more dense until I get back to the density of the gelain I started with.

We have now sucessfully created a hixel. Lay multiple of these on top of one another and we have the depth of the hologram. Now to get a hixel that lies adjacent to the first one, we have to start all over because that hixel contains the information of the object from a different view point, one slightly to the side. Not to critical from hixel to hixel (as we all well know from the strips in a stereogram) but non the less important as the view point changes significantly.

So there is how to manually construct a hologram. (Darn, I'd do it but I lost my nano tweezers again).

I like the idea of looking for the basic building block of holographic images, but I think that the problem is in trying to look at a hologram like a digital image, which dices up an image into little squares or pixels. A different approach might need to be looked at.

The basic holographic picture element might be better thought of as a zone plate. Each "point" on the object generates its own zone plate interference pattern, and instead of an orderly side by side array like digital pixels, the holographic image is a collection of overlapping zone plates.

Some might argue that a zone plate is more of an on-axis optic, but when you stray to the peripheries of the plate, especially if it is really big, the in-line reference beam is off to one side, so that should take care of that objection. And for the reflection case, the building block would be some sort of concave/convex mirror.

walschuler Sun Jan 25, 2009 4:15 pm #20
OK---

I agree that the best way to think about this is to use the idea of the zone plate for a single point and mutiple overlapping zone plates for any larger item.

For a single point, the wave fronts are diverging from the point and spherical, and if the reference beam is parallel its wavefronts are flat. If the reference is off-axis, the zone plate is also off-axis and tilted. The recorded pattern will also have curvature, such that from one side of the emulsion the zone plate interference pattern will be convex and from the other side concave. If you look from the side that is convex, you will see the virtual image, and from the concave side the real image. If the hologram is a reflection type then the curved interference wavefronts stack in depth and the hologram selectively reflects the color whose wavelength is twice the layer spacing.

For more than one object point, as Ed said above, the zone plates interfere and sum together. The curvature properties still hold, though a point in the interference pattern will no longer have the curvature of any one object point, and the "elementary shell" shape idea in the first post has to be given up. For any ordinary object the interference sum at a point in the emulsion has components that are strongest from object points that are most reflective and closest together. But for any ordinary object each point in the emulsion will sum an enormous number of points in zone plate patterns, and the curvature will be a complicated sum of the individual curvatures. For me the magic comes from the fact that this result can be Fourier analyzed, and further that in the viewing of the image the reconstruction light acts like it "understands" and reconstructs the appropriate wavefront shape to reproduce the original object wavefront, and if a reflection hologram, also the original color(s).

The inner zone plate zones are strongest, but give the lowest resolution. If you create a zone plate and clip off the outer rings, the object point becomes fuzzier and the resolution in the image falls. A post above reminds us that this relates to the modulation transfer function (MTF), which also falls. If you block only the inner zones (the center of the bullseye) then the image is sharper, in an ordinary object edges are emphasized, but the brightness suffers. In a proper setup for Fourier image processing, these are both normal possible functions (the former made by an aperture stop, the latter by a central block - an apodizer I believe its called).

If you keep restricting the size of your viewing zone in the emulsion, you are in effect clipping off the outer rings of zone plates from nearby points. You can also say you lose resolution due to diffraction, which increases as the aperture size shrinks at a given wavelength.Larger aperture holograms, like lenses and mirrors, have better resolution. But then grain size will impose a limit, as loss of resolution also grows as grain size increases, because then the emulsion will fail to resolve more of the finer outer zone plate zones.

I have wanted to distinguish between smallest element in an image and smallest element in the recording that generates it, more or less as I believe John does, and if possible link to prior terminology so that the (general?) public "gets it" on first hearing it.

I think pixel came into use after the advent of color TV (someone will correct me?), when it became necessary to make the image out of 3 discrete color cells per image element. Or perhaps with the rise of solid state TV sensors, which have discrete sensor cells and are read out by scanning voltages. It could have arisen earlier either for screen printing processes or even for pointilist paintings going back 100 plus years (see Seurat). For vacuum tube black and white TV or ordinary photos the smallest element would be the (random) grain, and "picture element" did not arise.

I don't see much support out there for my ideas for terms ending in "xel" so I suppose that Bob's idea of zonel is my next favorite for smallest element in the recording. Hogel, already out there, would be reserved for the smallest element in a holo image. I don't think there is an absolute smallest zonel, but certainly the resolution of the emulsion, set by grain size, must have something to do with the limit.

The smaller the zonal in area the worse the image resolution, the more restricted the point of view, the smaller the parallax effect if you are looking through only one location, and as the depth of the zonel shrinks (in a reflection holo), often the fainter the image, the wider band the reconstruction and the worse the chromatic dispersion in white illumination.

An aside: In a standard Lippmann image the lateral extent of the image has no effect on parallax because there is none to start with, but again the shallower the interference the broader band the reconstruction. If the source is broad band the interference is the fourier transform of all the frequencies, it looks like a single "fat" (deep) hump in exposure, and the peak is 1/4 wavelength into the emulsion and the interference null is at the surface, if the emulsion is in perfect contact with a mirror. If no mirror, then the hump peak is at the emulsion surface.
MfA Wed Mar 11, 2009 7:15 am #32
There is no smallest element in the recording that generates it ... right down to electrons and below everything to a more or less degree determines how light is affected by the hologram. As an abstraction the zone plate is not terribly useful, because it fails to allow you to capture all the potential effects in a hologram. Yes it can represent a point in space in a reflection or transmission hologram ... but it can't do the same for a rainbow hologram.

The zone plate is a good example to explain diffraction and how it allows a hologram to work, but it's in no way fundamental.

As for the hogel, I think you are misrepresenting it ... it's not so much the smallest element in a hologram. It's simply a small hologram, of which Zebra puts a whole lot together to form a bigger one.

Really ... a simple pixel is not so bad an abstraction of the smallest element inside a diffraction based hologram.
