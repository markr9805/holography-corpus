Here is a picture of my first multiplexed H1's reconstructed image using a HeNe laser.

![Image](http://4c-s.org/v-web/gallery/albums/album20/muxface1.jpg)

Details:

The entire hologram was comprised of 27 - 800x600 images projected to a diffuse glass plate with a modified DLP projector. I tried an LCD panel early on, but could not easily deal with the diffractive effects of the panels pixel structure, and polarization (I only had a random polarized laser then.)

I used a fixed plate holder and a stepper motor driven slit that was 0.1" wide.

Film/Dev.:

Slavich PFG-01 4x5 glass plate/JD-2 developer kit.

Laser:

20mW HeNe

Exposure time:

20 seconds per frame. 3 minutes settle time between frames.

Beam Ratio:

~4:1 ratio at the plate.

Size:

The projected real image shown is approx. 2"x2"

Notes:

This H1 is far to dim to get a good H2, and you know how hard it is to photo one of these things . To take this picture I projected the real image from the H1 onto a piece of diffuse glass located at the image plane, (where you would place a new plate to make an H2.)

Due to the limited space I have here(my 2'x3' table is stuffed into my wash-room) I had to use a curved mirror to reflect the image to my camera. You might have to sit back and look at it for a while to see it. The inset grey image is representative of one of the frames to help you visualize it in the red laser image. The portion of the image you see here in red is at least 15-20 slits wide.

Lot's of work still needed to be done to get the brightness up and make a whitelight H2, but the basics are in place for me to move ahead!

LarryD Tue Oct 25, 2005 7:56 pm #4
Thanks guys for the pat on the back! It is much appreciated.

> JohnFP: Why do you think the image is too dim? Couldn't you just beam split at the extreem 99% to object and 1% to reference to get the ratio needed, then compensate with exposure time. Or would your exposure time be too long?

yes, the exposure would be something way long, and I don't think I have a stable enough environment for that. The CCD camera was very sensitive and I had to really reduce the expsoure to grab the image (you are looking at the light transmitted through the diffuse screen.) When that image is projected to a white card, it is obviously very dim.

> HoloM: how did you calculate the pictures?

I have a 800Mhz celeron based PC running XP. It actually took 30+ hours of continuous rendering time for 27 frames at 640x480 pixels each. Each of those frames was generated 5 times(with sub-pixel offsets) and summed to perform antialiasing. That allowed me to scale up to 800x600 (even higher) without getting noticable "jaggies" in the image.

The model of the head itself was a free mesh model from one of the 3D sites offering free models. I created the scene, made textures for the skin and background and played a lot with multiple light sources (4 used here) to get the shadows right.

> HoloM: which software did you use ?

I have a friend who has access to LightWave3D, but pretty much any 3D rendering software (including POVRay, etc...) could be used.

I found a freeware DLL program to let me talk directly to the parallel port while under XP, and downloaded some example C code on how to drive a stepper motor. It was very exciting to know I could now create the image series, then load a plate in the holder, describe the exposure and other parameters, then click "go" and have it make the holo for me! I actually went and took a nap during this shot. When I got up there it was with the slit in the final position, and all I had to do was develop it! That was great!

> HoloM: I often thought about this. i think there must be a relation between slit wide, distance of panel to film plate, camera distance to object, camera movement between pictures viewing distance and so on. in the literature there is nearly no info about that.

I definately would not have been able to get this far without The Internet, and the many professionals and academic sites out there willing to share information.

I don't want to name names since I don't have permission, but I can say that I have contacted a couple of the originators, and current practioners of this technology, and some have been pretty generous helping with the information I required. Plus I found a paper on the MIT web site describing the issues involved in utilizing and interfacing to the DLP projector to make multiplexed stereograms. And the US patent office was a good resource since there are drawings presented to show the different configuration of optics, image generation, etc.

It is tough sometimes to convert the academic papers into my laymens perspective, though. I had to re-read some of these papers over-and-over again, and cross refernece many against each other to get the a mental picture. And Dinesh was helpful with getting me to understand what some of the math meant in the real word.

I will say that if you attempt to do this you need PATIENCE! I actually had built three different versions of the setup over the last 8 months before I got anything visible in a hologram at all! And it really helps to know a bit about software development, electronics design, optics, mechanical systems, and not to mention 3D visualization, and some graphics design.

This is one area of holography that you really have to do things hands on, the whole way through. It has been extremely rewarding, though!

Colin-

I bought the DLP projector, a very used Proxima-DS1, for only $50 on eBay, as it had no bulb and was pretty cosmetically beat up. There is quite a bit of dirt on the internal optics as well. Infocus has a very tight rein on all schematics and design info, and some of them are made in japan so info is very hard to come by. No local shops would give me schematics for ANY projector, saying their hands were tied contractually.

You may have to do a lot of mods to get to where you can shoot a laser through one. These things get really hot when used normally, due to the bulb. I think this one used a 400W one. So there are several sensors, and fans with feedback, etc. that you have to deal with to fake out the software so it keeps the DLP running.

The DS1 is small...about 12"x10"x3" and is stuffed with surface mounted components and different types of small pitch flex circuits and other hard to work with stuff. Those things are so delicate and easy to break that I decided to leave the projector intact as much as possible. The MIT paper(I'll try to find a link and post later) showed a picture where they had completely dissassembled an off the shelf projector (not a DS1) and laid it out on their table, giving direct access to the DMD chip. I used basically all of the projectors optics as designed, except for the final projection lens, which I moved so I could focus the image in about 5 inches instead of the 3-12 feet it was designed for.

I've recently read that the newer projectors have much better DMD chips in them, with internal coatings, etc. to minimize scattering. Plus they are higher resolution. The contrast of this one is definately iffy compared to using a film transparency.

I'll take some pics of the table later today or tomorrow, but don't laugh, it's "crude and glued"...literally

DJ-

I found I did spend a lot of my time cross referencing the few papers I did find. My configuration is roughly based on DeBiteto's panaramic stereogram techniques. And the image generation and alignment techiques I borrowed from Franks web site about stereographic portriats using an LCD. You might try contacting the authors of those research papers if you can find them.

If you want to do this technique, just take one tiny step at a time! Let the small bits of information stew in your mind. It might be helpful, if you have not tried already, to shoot a single frame holo from a film transparency. Try out DeFrietas' described technique where he uses film instead of the LCD, or something similar. Then move on to the LCD, DLP or whatever. MichaelH had posted something earlier about some open-source software he wrote that interfaced to hardware to control stepper motors and a shutter. And you can buy complete moving stages with motors if you can't build it.

There are so many little details, if you are working alone you can expect to spend at least 6 mos or more before you shoot. As I mentioned above I had gone through three iterations of my system. After each iteration I actually quit, gave up completley, tore down the table and settled for shooting single beam Denisyuks for a couple of weeks. But the "bug had bit me", so I would try again, again until I got to the meger shot above. I still have LOTs of work to do to where I can get a clean, bright image in white light...

Good luck and have fun!!

> Colin Kaminski: Can you share photos of the table?

Sorry for the grainy look. Cheap camera

The slit looks wide here in the first pic because I was in the middle of making the slit larger to see what happens when using fewer frames.

Not visible in the lower picture is a 1" spherical mirror directly behind the projector, which is diverging the beam from the beam splitter into the DLP. The beam path actually goes through the bottom of the body of the projector where the door for the bulb would normally be, to hit the spherical mirror.

Also due the the tight space the ref beam is not completley collimated, but it's good enough.

Lots of home made stuff there, but hey it works!
