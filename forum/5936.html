Same picture but with lasers on and traced with note card. Notice how the green beam diverges more than the blue, which diverges more than the red, giving through three distinct cones. Only the center is used to expose 4x5 PFG-03c plates.

The three beams are merged with appropriate dichroic filters and variable neutral density filters control the RGB ratios with the shutter controlling the overall exposure. [/img]

> John Klayer: Would it work to put a different shutter on the green and red lasers then leave the red shutter open longer to make up for the red laser being weaker?

Yes. But in the case of MB/R6G DCG, the 633 sensitivity is rather poor compared to 532, so it still pays to conserve the red light as much as possible.

It's a very interesting question as to how long the red exposures can go (using passive means, not active., ie., fringe locking). Cristiano did a fantastic job with a 15 minute exposure. This is a very long exposure using just passive stabilization, but it worked very well in his case. Each lab will have a different level of stability, because each is different. Joe Farina Tue Apr 24, 2007 9:37 pm #22
Brown is an interesting case. It seems to reflect pretty much all wavelengths, with emphasis in the yellow, orange, and red.

In the Mayer book there is a graph showing how burnt sienna (for example) reflects white light. It reflects evenly between 400 and 525nm (for a 10% tint, i.e., 10% burnt sienna and 90% white) with about 40% reflectance, then jumps up to about 60% reflectance between about 600 and 700nm. So in other words, it reflects pretty much the full spectrum, with a bit more emphasis in the yellow, orange, and red.

So, if you used just 532 green and 633 red, and there was absolutely no desaturation or bandwidth broadening, then I don't think brown would result in the final hologram. Wouldn't the colors obtainable simply be a straight line across the CIE diagram between the two wavelengths? The line from 532 to 633 does not cross through white, which is needed to reproduce brown (I think).

Assuming no broadening may be quite unrealistic, though. If there is a fair amount of broadening (which is probably typical), then the shorter wavelengths would reflect to some extent as well, possibly producing a shade of brown (I'm just guessing here).

So, if you minimize broadening, then I don't think those two wavelengths will be enough to do a good job of reproducing brown. But if you intentionally make it a bit more broadband (thereby desaturating the colors) you may get a pretty good shade of brown, albeit you would be doing it in a rather uncontrolled way. A more controlled way would be to use 3 wavelengths and minimize broadening and shrinkage. But that's another can of worms.

Maybe I'm all wet here, these are just guesses on my part. Kaveh, could you help to clarify this?
Kaveh Wed Apr 25, 2007 1:44 am #23
Slide digression:

Brown is a good example of the complexity of color science. We only perceive brown when there are other colors around it for comparison. In experiments in color perception, it is common to get rid of all extraneous light, and have an observer look at a mixture of several colors (say RGB) and say what color they see. In these experiments, the observer never nominates brown. But if you put dark yellow next to bright yellow, the darker one is perceived as brown. That's why it's been called the [Bastard child](http://jheer.org/blog/archives/000235.html) of color perception!

My guess would be that if you can produce yellow, then you can do brown too.
