No. The reason that stereo pictures work is that you're using something called "vergence" which is one of the 3D cues you use to see 3D. There is nothing in the brain that processes 3D, the brain interprets cues and various geometries of eye muscles to interpret 3D. The fact that youre eyes twist in opposite directions to focus on an object, the fatc the youre eyes focus at diferent depths etc.

In stereo, you have two independant pictures from two perspectives. If you twist and focus your eyes in such a manner that each 2D image is "projected" along the line of sight of each eye and into the same point, then your brain is "fooled" into creating a 3D effect. In holography, the actual waveform of light, the light emanating from the actual object congeals at some point in space and then diverges, exactly as if the real object were still there, from a particular perspective. The eyes see this waveform and the brain processes it. It's exactly like looking at the real world. The depth to which you can "see" 3D in a hologram is exactly the same as the depth you can "see" in the real world - about 5 feet or so, depending on lighting conditions,.

A couple of points:

When I tried this, several years ago, one problem was that the hologram was too bright compared to the surrounding room and could have swamped the delicate electronics of the camera. The point is that a consumer camera may not have been so delicate and also, this was in 1986 and this may not be true anymore.

The hologram needs to be reconstructed by a strongish light, usually from overhead. The camera cannot be too near or it'll obstruct the overhead reconstructed light. Having said that, if the holographer is making a hologram for this specific purpose, he/she should be able to compensate.

A hologram is, as I said, an exact reconstruction of the real bject.Therefore a hologram may not be translucent, ie you may not be able to "focus through the image", because it'll appear solid. The translucency of a holoram depends on how strong it is, the so-called "efficiency" of a hologram, and the strength of the reconstruction source. The brighter the recon source, the more solid the hologram. In the above case when I tried it in 86, the hologram was what's called an achromat", which makes it pretty translucent to begin with ("reflection" holograms are not too translucent). I reconstructed the hologram against the background of a white wall but could not dim the reconstruction source. You could see right through the hologram.

Actually the reason stereo pictures work are because the two pictures are slightly different. As when you look at the real world you get two different pictures on your retinas. You either can have a cross eyed or a parallell eyed stereo pair. If you focus on a point, ie get your eyes 'diverged' or 'converged' (depending on which type of stereo pair you use) so both eyes look at the same object in the two pictures, you see this as one picture. Objects that do not form a single picture as the one in focus, and instead are seen double, are processed as being in front of or behind the focused object. Your brain process this as a whole 3-dimensional image. If the two stereo pictures are the same then you see a flat image.

If the two pictures differ too much then the coherence is lost and the image just becomes a "blur" and the brain preferably presents only one picture at a time in your consciousness.

The orientation of the eyes, the focus of the eye lens as well as colors and other cues are of course also used to determine our 3-dimensional world. Because an eye is constantly moving you get different pictures on a single retina so you even see in 3d with a single eye, though much less than with two eyes. About 10 percent of the population have a problem to see in 3d despite them using both their eyes.

[quote:a2c7be7719="D J Mathson"]Dinesh, in what way do you mean that there is nothing in the brain that processes 3d?[/quote:a2c7be7719]

Well, the eye/brain system has sensors for detecting a variety of optical inputs and differentiating them. For instance, twist in the ocular field has a sensor which detects the amount of twist and feeds that information to the brain, although some processing does occur before the visual cortex. Thus if you looked at lines and they twisted to some angle, then the eye/brain processing system gets this information and processes it as "twist". This is also true for example, for motion parallax as well as angular parallax. Of course it's a lot more compliacted than that.

Basically, there are "dedicated processors" in the language of Information, or Data Processing, which take in data from the visual field and interpret them. It's one reason for seasickness; the "data processors" are getting conflicting data and so are processing "out of synch". However, in terms of depth of vision, or 3D vision, there are no processors. 3D vision occurs by insinuation of certain musculatures in the eye. Thus, if you take a picture with an ordinary camera of an object, move over a couple of inches and take a picture of tha same object, you can generate "3D'. The way you do this apes the configuration of the the twist of the eye muscles in focusing in real space. Let's say you're looking at a pebble you hold in your hand. You hold it about a foot (18cm) from your eyes. The standard eye seperation is about 2.5 inches (5.25cm), thus each eye twists by an angle of about 17 degrees from the "straight ahead" position (90 - (inv tan)((18*2)/5.25)). The brain says, "Aha, each eye has twisted by 17 degrees. I know this because I know the stress on the lateral eye muscles. Let me calculate tan(90-17). Since I know his eye seperation, by knowing tan(90-17) I have the vertical distance to the object he's looking at and so I have a z coordinate" Now, in this example, the distance from each eye to the pebble is about 19cm. Suppose you took a stereo picture of the stone and placed each picture at, say, 9 cm from each eye, but close enough so that you could focus your eyes to a point 18 cm away and the lines of the eyes passed through each image. The right eye image would focus at 18cm and so would the left eye image. The two images would "merge" or "converge" at a point 18cm away at exactly the position where the real pebble was. Your brain, not having a dedicated processor, but simply taking it's cues from eye muscles, would be fooled into thinking there really was a real pebble 18 cm in front of your eye. This is a technique known as "vergence" from the fact that you're "merging" two seprate images by focusing your eyes along a line of sight that intersects the two images. As you say, there are several versions of vergence.

Dinesh,

Yes, angles are important for depth perception or stereopsis, but no, it is not the angular vergence of the eyes or the tension of the muscles that are responsible for the experience. It is mainly the size of the images, i.e. different angles on the retinas compared to the fixation point that gives the perception of depth. There is definitely 3D-processing going on in the brain.

The experience of visual 3D is not something 'out there' but is a construct created by the brain.

![Image](http://www.eyedream.se/bilder/diverse/stereopsis.gif)

Stereopsis

Beyond ca 30 meters (100 feet) we can not use stereopsis and instead use cues like previous familiarity, interposition, linear and size perspectives, distribution of shadows and illumination or motion parallax. For objects closer than this we use stereoscopic vision.

Since there are others that are better in english than me I will make a long quote from Principles Of Neural Science (3d ed) by Kandel, Schwartz and Jessel (page 455-458) that may make it more clear. I know it is long, but it is a good basic description.

" Although monocular cues are important for depth perception at a distance, the perception of depth for near objects less than 100 feet away is also mediated by stereoscopic vision. This involves comparing the retinal images in the two eyes. When we fixate on a point, the image of this point falls upon the center of the retina in each eye. The convergence of the two eyes causes that point to fall on corresponding points on each central retina. The point of focus is called the fixation point; the parallel (vertical) plane of points on which it lies is called the fixation plane. The distance of an image from the center of the two eyes allows the visual system to calculate the distance of object relative to the fixation point (Figure 30-16). Any point on the object that is nearer or farther than the fixaton point will project an image at some distance from the center of the retina. Parts of the object that are closer to us will be farther apart on the retina in a horizontal direction. Parts of the object that are farther from us will project closer on the retina.

Because the two eyes are about 6 cm apart, each eye views the world from a slighly different position. Thus, three-dimensional objects produce slightly different images on the two retinas. This can be clearly demonstrated by closing each eye in turn. As vision is switched from one to the other eye, any near object will appear to skip sideways. We adapt to this disparity by sensory fusion, by fixating both eyes on one point. By positioning the eyes so that left and right images of the object fall on corresponding positions on the two retinas, we see one object. Fusion is not perfect, however, when we fix our gaze on a three-dimesional object. The two retinal images of the object do not fall on exactly corresponding positions. The difference in position, called binocular disparity, depends on the distance of the object from the fixation plane. Thus, points on the tree-dimensional object just outside the fixation plane stimulate different points on each eye, and the multiple disparities provide cues for stereopsis, the perception of solid objects.

Surprisingly, not one of the great early students of optics - Euclid, Archimedes, Leonardo da Vinci, Newton, or Goethe - took notice of stereopsis, although each could readily have discovered it with the methods available to them. Stereoscopic vision was not discovered until 1838, when the physicist Charles Wheatstone invented the stereoscope. With the stereoscope, two photographs of the scene 60-65 mm apart, one from the position of each eye, are mounted into a binocular-like device such that the right eye sees only the picture taken from one position and the left eye sees only the other picture. Remarkebly, this presentation produces a three-dimensional image.

How is stereopsis accomplished? Clearly the brain must somehow calculate the disparity beween the images seen by the two eyes and then estimate distance based on simple geometric relations. But must the object first be recognized before the brain can match the corresponding points of the object in the two eyes? Until 1960 it was generally thought that this was so, and stereopsis therefore was thought to be a late stage in visual processing.

In 1960 Bela Jusesz proved that this view was wrong when he found that stereoscopic fusion and depth perception do not require monocular identification of form. The only clue necessary for stereopsis is retinal disparity. To demonstrate this remarkable fact, Jusesz created a pattern composed entirely of random dots, in the middle of which are som dots arranged in a square. The square form is visible only when two identical copies of the pattern are viewed in a stereoscope. When the square in one of the copies is displaced slightly to one side, it appears in binocular view to lie in front of the rest of the pattern, and to be floating free of its background. If the displacement is in the opposite direction, the square appears to lie behind the rest of the pattern (Figure 30-17). By itself, each random dot pattern will not produce any clues. Only with stereoscopic vision can one see the square within the pattern.

With this method, Julesz demonstrated that humans can detect form and movement in depth from stereograms consisting only of randomly placed elements. Since all the dots are identical, it is remarkagle that the visual system can discover which are the corresponding dots in the two pictures. Initially it was thought that this correspondence problem is solved by the cooperation of many sensors, each corresponding to a single point in the stereogram. It now seems more likely that the dots contain arrays of micropatterns or clusters so that the problem of correspondence is solved by matching a few of these with the input from the two eyes.

Julesz´s experiment also showed that stereopsis does not have its origin in the retina or in the lateral geniculate nucleus, but occurs at the level of the striate cortex, or at an even higher level where the signals for the two eyes are combined. As a result, Julesz has called this kind of perception Cyclopean perception, after the mythical Cyclops, who had only one eye in the middle of his forehead. Neither eye alone can make sense of the shapes or contours of the figures: Each receives only a meaningless assembly of random dots. Where then does fusion occur?

Information from the Two Eyes Is First Combined in the Primary Visual Cortex

Hubel and Wiesel first demonstrated that the initial opportunity for fusion occurs in V1. It is here that single cells in the visual system first receive input from the two eyes (Chapter 29). Stereopsis, however, requires that the inputs from the two eyes be slightly different - there must be a horizontal disparity in the two retinal images. The important finding that certain neurons in V1 are actually selective for horizontal disparity between the input from the two eyes was made in 1968 by Horace Barlow, Colin Blakemore, Peter Bishop, and Jack Pettigrew (Figure 30-18 ). Disparity-selective neurons have now been detected all along the magnocellular pathway: in V1, in the thick stripes of V2, and in MT. [Middle Temporal Area or V5]

Are these disparity-sensitive neurons important for stereopsis? This question has been addressed by Gian Poggio, who found that about 70% of both simple and complex cells in V1, V2, and V3 of alert monkeys respond to binocular disparity. Certain neurons are sensitive to stimuli nearer than the fixation plane, whereas others are sensitive to stimuli that are further away. As shown in Figure 30-16, stimuli nearer than the fixation plane are interpreted as close, and those further from the fixation plane are interpreted as far.

The existance of neurons sensitive to disparity raises the question: Do these neurons also respond to a stereogram that contains no depth clues except retinal disparity? To answer this, Poggio first located responsive cells using a solid (three-dimensional) bar as a stimulus. He then replaced the solid bar with a stereogram of a random-dot pattern. Many of the complex neurons that responded to the solid figure also responded to the random-dot stereogram (Figure 30-19). These analyses suggest that when a cell responds to a random-dot pattern it does so because the clusters of elements form a micropattern to which the cell responds effectively. When two such patterns are presented at the optimal disparity for the cell, the cell responds optimally. Thus the cell responds to the line pattern presented to each eye and the input to the two eyes yield summation."

You're right, I was being sloppy. Disparity determines the size of the object and hence is the cause of stereopsis , and also the seperation between two objects when they are approx 5 arcseconds apart. Vergence determines the location of the object, so that it gives the z coordinate, while the extent of the object is given by disparity. There is also a kind of disparity that occurs when one eye can see an object but the other eye cannot. This is referred to as "monocular occlusion", but stereopsis s determined by disparity.

When I say there is no processing, I mean that the visual impulse contains no depth information. The visual field receives a "pattern" of light, a spatio-temporal 2 dimensional light field, which is impressed upon the retina. Of course, there are two light fields, one for each retina. This information encodes stimuli which are filtered by a group of cells "behind" the retina, such as the ganglia, first, then passed on to specific centers in the visual cortex for further filtering and analysis. Together specific parts of the cortex filter the light field and extract information on colour, movement and size among other things directly from the light field . However, the spatio-temporal signal impressed upon the retina do not by themselves contain depth information. Depth information is determined by querying the various depth cues from the scene and adding them to the filtered data set. There is nothing in the cortex that extracts depth information - it's calculated by other cues. However, having said that, the brain can do a fourier transform and determine the phase difference between each retinal data set. I feel that this ability to do fourier analysis is still a calculation from the visual field as opposed to an encoding of the visual field. In then end, I suppose, it all depends on what you mean by "processing"

In reading this thread I would have to state the if the phase of the light and phase differences are not detected and only 2D images and cues are input into the brain then our description of what 3D is has to be generated in the brain. That is, I believe that all that has been described is used by the brain to create a 3D image. Then any processing, like an object moving across a background is processed as subsequent 3D frames. I think the brain would not be so inefficient as to continually try to compare each individual 2D part with next frame.

This is something some in the field of computer processing are trying to mimic.

I remember quite a while ago I went to a "science" convention. There were a couple of college students doing research on robotics and navigation using holography. What they were doing was to take subsequent 2D images of the navigation down a hall with 3 or 4 openings to the left and right and record them holographically (somewhat like a stereogram) into a Lithium Niobate crystal. But each image was stored using a slightly different reference angle. The crystal was put into a "chamber". The camber had an opening at the front of the robot, the eye. This eye took the impute image and projected it on a transmission LCD screen. This screen was illuminate with a laser. The image from the screen was then focused to the crystal. Then on the output side of the crystal, there was an array of photocells each corresponding to the reference angle used in creating the hologram of that particular scene. This eyes image (actually the object beam) was focused onto the Lithium Niobate crystal and depending on the angle of the output reference beam the robot could navigate looking at the "whole picture" of where it was instead of having to do the magnitude of calculations and then search a database to determine where it was. Basically, if the robot was supposed to turn left at the second opening on the left it only has to check a single photocell, the one the corresponded to that particular scene's output reference beam angle to make the decision to turn left.

While this is somewhat different it does express the efficiency of processing an entire "picture" at once instead of the many individual components separately. I believe the brain does this also but not just the flat whole scene but its entire components (all the 3D cues) as well, simultaneously. It was a fascinating presentation
