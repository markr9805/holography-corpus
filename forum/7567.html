Ed Wesly Thu Oct 16, 2008 12:34 am #2
My usual reply is always give it a shot an see what happens. What makes it Anti-Newton Ring glass is a light etch or sand blasting to make the surface slightly rough, eliminating the internal ringing between flat and parallel surfaces. At least that's the secret to A-N slide mounts. To see if the glass you are going to use is etched, look at the reflection of a light source and see if it is a nice specular reflection or if there is a little halo or diffusion around the light. Since light is intended to go through the glass sandwich in the slide mount or photographic negative carrier case, the roughness is negligible.

But for holographic use, will it be stable? Instead of a complete layer of intimate contact, it's like a lot of little fingers holding the glass down. Maybe it will move, maybe it won't, I dunno. And will it destroy the spatial coherence of spatially filtered light enough to blur fringes, maybe yes, maybe no. But what the heck, give it a wack!

The slide mounts that I have used for slide projectors was from a company called Emde. Besides making (if they are still in business, who knows with the film photo world going down the tubes) 35 mm slide mounts, they were making 2 1/4" square mounts. And they snapped together with a loud click when film was trapped in them, so they might be a solution to the problem that has been plaguing a similar thread in this section!

Duh, why didn't I think of this earlier? Maybe because like BobH I am a firm believer in the simplicity of plates.
Colin Kaminski Thu Oct 16, 2008 12:41 am #3
There are two kinds I have seen. The cheap kind is just AR coated glass. It proves to be too fragile and the coatings get scratched. In addition the coating near the film is the wrong design to be index matching and reflects more light.

The real anti-Newton-ring glass is etched with a fine pattern. This pattern is too fine to resolve in normal film applications but it does resolve just fine in holographic films. The idea is to break the rings up into a smaller size so the film can not resolve them. I have never tried it myself but I would suspect the pattern would leave a pattern in the reference beam. If you get some try spreading the laser beam out and shining it through the glass and project the pattern on a white card a few feet away. My guess is you will see a strong fringe structure. Of course I could be completely wrong...
