> i think that the complete kit from holokits.com is affordable.. But are you sure that it has all the stuff that i need for making a hologram?

Here's a few of the un-foreseen costs I found when getting started:

1) A method for vibration isolation.(Depending on your environment could be as simple as a board on an inner-tube, etc. Search this site.)

2) Gluing things down with glue-gun can save time.

3) Batteries to power the laser. I have used 2-D-cells for hours of fun making holograms.

4) Some sort of mask and eye-protection are recommended when mixing the dev. solutions.

5) Rubber Gloves.

6) Distilled water

7) Paper towels and trash bags for clean-up

8) A green safe-light (for the red-sensitive plates in the holokit from integraph) to help see what you are doing and not prematurely expose the plates (fogging)

There may be a few more, you should still be just within budget, though.

Good Luck and Have Fun!

Gary Deal Wed Dec 08, 2004 6:52 am #12
Ah, so the physics instructor wants you to make use of what's available rather than just buying everything you need? Good man, you'll probably learn more that way.

A 1mw hene should work, it'll just limit you a bit. Michael's right, make a small hologram to keep your exposure time short.

You'll need a variety of things, but there's ways to get them...

A vibration isolation table is best, but a closed school on a Sunday or at night might not have much vibration. Look up and set up a Michaelson (sp?) Interferometer (requires a couple of first surface mirrors, a beamsplitter and a lens) and you'll be able to see just how much vibration there is. You need to have about none.

You can make a reflection hologram with only a laser, lens, holographic film, and a subject. It's not difficult.

A transmission holograms is less sensitive to vibration during exposure, but must be viewed with the laser.

You'll need photographic chemicals - is there a photo department at the school? Watch for someone taking pictures with a large bellows camera and talk to them about developing. "Hot", lithographic, or high resolution plate developers have worked best for me. Is anyone there making micrographs with an electron microscope? Talk to them. You might learn stuff you hadn't planned on.

Holographic film is the one thing that's hard to fake. It has extremely high resolution, and unless there's some around the lab or a photo-emulsion professor there, you'll probably have to buy it. That is, unless you're not actually being required to produce a 3D holographic image and a diffraction grating will do. You can probably use a very slow speed conventional (35mm) film for that - basically overlapping the spread beam onto the film. I haven't done this (not on purpose, anyway) and others could direct you better than I. Doing so regardless would probably be a good exercise in dealing with the beam and geometry.

Don't be afraid to adapt things to your purpose, my interferometer lens was scavenged from a Minolta point-and-shoot camera and the beamsplitter came from a Kodak. I made my first hologram with a "lens" that wasn't even a real lens. Look in "junk" stores, old plastic polaroid cameras that have the film at the bottom have first-surface mirrors inside, though sometimes they're garbage. There's parts everywhere, you just have to figure out how to find and use them.

Have fun,

-Gary

I reinforce the set up of the Michaelson Interferometer. This can be accomplished before you even buy plates or processing chemicals. This will tell you if the laser is suitable. This can be done on a lab bench. If you see nice contrasty fringes, even if they move, giggle or bounce, your laser should work fine.

Then if you determine the laser is addequate to make a hologram, make you first hologram a Single Beam Denisyk in which the plate is resting on or against the object directly. This will give you the highest probablity in obtaining a hologram. In this type of hologram the plate and the object are the only two components that have to stay perfectly still with respect to one another. Thus having them in contact helps insure this.

Sorry, more information after reading the other replies again.

You do not need a vibration isolation table at all for a Denisyk as I described in my last post. Again I recommend laying the object on the table on it's back. Lay the plate right on the object. Let it settle. Expose it from above coming in from the top of the plate/object at around 55 - 60 degrees.

TIP: when you place the plate on the object take your finger nail and tap the plate at different points on the plate. You should be able to hear a dull "thunk" or a ringing "twing". The "thunk" is solid and wanted. The "twing" means the plate is ringing at that point and could move at that point-not wanted. You may be able to shift the plate to get the entire plate to "thunk", which is wanted.

[i:2cb50e4851]Isolation Table[/i:2cb50e4851]:

Necesary in some cases and unnecesary in others. In your case, I'd use one. This way, if you don't get a hologram, you can at least eliminate motion as a problem. If especially you've never done this before, it's best to take the "can't hurt" approach and be safe rather than sorry. For an actual table, anything firm and solid will work, depending on your circumstances. As Gary says, the school in the weekend may be pretty quiet (It wasn't in my school. Mine was a boarding sxchool and every nutcase in the school was in the lab on Sunday afternoon doing "stuff". I was trying to measure cosmic ray showers with a home-made cloud chamber while the kid next to me was building a Whimshurst machine - the gadget that gives off all those sparks in old Frankenstein movies. Those ##@** sparks kept disrupting all my nice, well-behaved protons! ). Anyway, the quieter the surroundings, the less vibration isolation you'll need. You can use an old door, a stone slab, sheets of a hard wood over breize blocks (breeze blocks?) Anything good and solid and firm.

[i:2cb50e4851]Michelson[/i:2cb50e4851]:

Place the laser so that the beam crosses the largest line across the table, usually this means along a diagonal. Place a beamsplitter as far as reasonably possible (not the edge of the table, but not too near the laser). The beamsplitter can be a piece of "Saran" wrap tightly stretched across a frame of some sort, or a 1/4 in glass. The beamsplitter needs to be at 45 degrees to tne beam. Now one beam goes right through the glass and one reflects fromn it (maybe two if you're using glass. If you are using glass, block off the second beam reflecting off the back of the glass with a small piece of black tape on the front surface of the glass). Let both the transmitted and reflected beams fall on front surface mirrors. Be sure they're front surface. Let the mirrors reflect back onto the beamsplitter. Out of the unused face of the beamsplitter will come a patch of light with stripes across it. Magnify this with a lens and project the stripes on a wall or a screen. These stripes ideally must not move at all and must be very contrasty. In the real world, they will move and drift a little and the contrast won't be that great. Movement is what you're trying to avoid. If one black stripe goes a quarter way into the neighboring white stripe in about 10 seconds or less, there's too much motion. Get a better table or fix your optics more firmly (Hot glue: The Holographer's Friend!) . If the stripes jitter so that all you see is a blur, get a better table. If the stripes gradually drift across one direction, fix your optics (usually just one optic) more firmly.

You can place the plate right on the object, depending on the object. In some objects this works, but not in all objects. If the object is a small, slippery plastic chess piece, the plate or object will slide, depending on how slippery your table is. Best to place the plate on a platform with an extension, like an "L", with the plate on the long arm of the "L" and the object glued on the horizontal platform of the "L". This way, any motion of one translates to the motion of the other. You're trying to avoid relative motion between plate and object.

JohnFP Wed Dec 15, 2004 1:51 pm #17
Use the laser and lens to expand the beam to hit the plate evenly with the object behind the plate. The best set up for a first hologram is to have the laser and lens mounted above the plate and off to one side to hit the plate at about a 56 degree angle. Try to use an object that has flat spots such that the plate can rest directly on the object to insure no movement. Remember to take a piece of black tape and tape the leading edge of the plate so that the laser light will not hit the edge of the plate. It will reflect multiple times inside the glass and cause problems for the hologram. If you are using a hand held shutter, try not to breath in the direction of the plate/object. The warm air from you breath might effect the hologram negatively. After exposrure take the holgoram to your processing area. Be sure to always use a safelight. Develop the holgogram, rinse, the bleach then rinse again. The last rinse may contain a few drops of photoflo. Squeegy both sides in an even, fluid motion trying to get all in one pass. You can practice this on plain glass in full light. Lean the holgoram against a wall and let it dry completely. You will not be able to see it until it is completely dry. If you want to speed up drying you can use a hair dryer but use it on low setting about 18 inches away and keep it moving around the plate. You want to dry it very slow with a hair dryer.

Other things I can think of you may need;

An object that is a diffuse or flat white - not shiney metal

Holographic plate or film - I would go with 2inch by 2inch to start with

Developer

Bleach for reflection hologram

Safelight for processing.

Squeegy - Car windshield wiper blade is good

Trays for developer, water rinse, bleach and final rinse (4).

Black electric tape for masking off leading plate edge

If film is used instead of plate, you need two pieced of glass cut the same thickness as your film and four black paper clamps. Use two clamps to two opposite sides

Additional things not necessary;

Photoflo

Light tight for transporting plate

Plates instead of film

Camera tripod for mounting the diode and lens.

Running water for the final rinse

Not needed;

A mirror for this set-up

If you're just getting started, I would highly recommend plates rather than film. I found it took a LOT of experimentation and practice (and wasted film) to get even moderately acceptable results with film and results were never as good as with plates which are SO much easier to support and stabilize and to show off when they are worth displaying. There is so much to learn starting out that dealing with the added frustration of film is just not worth it. Save film for later.
