HoloM Sun Jan 15, 2006 2:26 pm #5
Michael, I try to explain:

I asume you will use a slit which is horizontal and moves up and down. This is normally easier to use, because you need no overhead reference. The slit is moving up and down.

if you use an assembly like in A) you have two problems:

- the slit will not be accurate horizontal because of a force F which is generated through the slits own weight.

-the slit will move back and forward, if it is not led with a second axle.

In picture B) there is a second axle which prevents moving the slit back and forth, but you still have the force F which is bigger in this case because weight of the slit increases because you must mount it somehow on the second axle. So the slit will bend down slightly on the right. But this is a problem in this case, because then it's difficult to avoid that the slit wedges.

My first setup was typ B and I didn't get it going. Nor up, neither down. The only solution to avoid wedging is to drive the second axle, too.

You can do this either (picture C) with a second stepper motor which is electrically synchronized with motor 1 or you can do this by mechanically synchronizing the second axle like in picture D.

I use a system which drives both axles with mechanical synchronisation, like in picture D.

HoloM Sun Jan 15, 2006 6:52 pm #9
Because of geometry reasons I think moving the slit is better than moving the plate. I think it's easier to calculate the pictures with a moving slit. I am not sure but I think you must correct the pictures in some way to get a correct 3D image when you move the plate. Also the reference beam could be a problem I think, especially if you don't use a collimating mirror. if you move the plate the source of the reference beam will no longer be a point source, more a line source, because every slit has his own reference source. perhaps also with collimated light this could be an issue.

If you move the slit, you must have a bigger reference beam which is nearly equally bright. but this normaly shouldn't be a problem, for normal master holograms you also need such a big reference beam. But there's an other problem i had to deal with, by moving the slit. not the reference light is the problem, but the object light is more critical, especially if you use an lcd panel and not a DLP projector. Because LCD have the property that the passing light is only visible in a narrow angle in front of the display . So the more you get with the slit out of the middle of the lcd, the lower is the object light intensity. so on the outer zones you get a worse ratio than direct in front of the panel, and this is a problem. I think by using a dlp this would be no problem.
BobH Sun Jan 15, 2006 9:45 pm #10
> . Because LCD have the property that the passing light is only visible in a narrow angle in front of the display . So the more you get with the slit out of the middle of the lcd, the lower is the object light intensity. so on the outer zones you get a worse ratio than direct in front of the panel, and this is a problem.

I solved this by using a lens to focus the image from the LCD onto a diffuser. I put a large diameter weak positive meniscus lens I happened to have (from Surplus Shed!) just dowhstream from, and in contact with the LCD (which was illuminated with collimated light). This converged the light into the projection lens (a 9" f/4.5 Plummer process lens). The hologram on the cover of The Holography Marketplace Fifth Edition was made with this system.
Ed Wesly Mon Jan 16, 2006 4:23 am #11
Both stereogram systems that I have experience with, one at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago, the other at CFC, (which was a direct descendent of the Newswanger system that did the approximate meter square Dizzy Gillespie), both moved the slit with the plate stationary. Both worked fine. The falloff in the reference beam is just like any other H1. The edge to edge brightness of the projected image wasn't much of an issue either.

The SAIC system used 16mm film, the CFC used a DLP.

As usual, the best thing to do is try it. But what you will inevitably find are lots of nice diffraction artifacts all over the pixels! Basically what you are doing is making a Gabor-type hologram with this scheme!

When the image from the DLP or LCD is in focus, the diffracted edges disappear! I guess that this is a case of the Fourier (or Fresnel or Fraunhofer) transform of a Fourier (or Fresnel or Fraunhofer) transform going back to the original object, although in this case it is enlarged!
