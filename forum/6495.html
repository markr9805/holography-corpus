Tony, room temperature, or especially warm temperatures, will accelerate the dark reaction. So for storage of DCG emulsion, or for coated plates, anything other than refrigeration is a no-no. I would simply use a small dedicated refrigerator, such as can be found quite cheaply these days.

Joe Farina Fri Oct 19, 2007 7:59 pm #7
Colin and John,

As soon as my DCG plates come out of the refrigerator, water begins to condense on the surface. Immediately after removing the plate from the refrigerator, I give the emulsion side a hard, long blast with the hairdrier. My hairdrier has various settings (for temperature and air speed) and I usually do this with no heat, or with a little heat. So basically my objective is to not let condensation form in the first place, or if this is unavoidable, then get rid of the surface moisture as quickly as possible. I have not seen any problems or irregularities in my holograms due to surface condensation when using this method.
JohnFP Mon Oct 22, 2007 1:57 pm #8
Joe that's great. I never had much sucess with fridging the plates but I hear other do it all the time. I got bad spots on mine so I abandoned the technique pretty quickly.

Tony, You want to keep the holograms cool and dry for storage. They will keep the logest that way. Then prior to using let them suck in some water from the air and warm up a little. So if your box can keep them at around 60F and 40 humidiy, they should keep nicely for up to a week (maybe longer). Then allow them to come up to room temp 70 - 75F and ambient humidy 50-60%. Also they are more sensitive the warmer and more moist they are. (to a practicle point of course).

So to recap. For long storage Cool and Dry.

For exposing warmed and more moisture.

JohnFP Mon Oct 22, 2007 4:37 pm #10
Well, think of it this way. If you make a DCG hologram with 532nm and the image comes out lime green, then at the moment the plate is on the table and getting ready to be exposed and when the hologram is completely processed the relative thickness must be the same right? But that is a completely oversimplified thought.

So some scenarios:

Plate made and dried for 24 hours to a final thickness of 7 microns and stored in a cool dry place. The plate is then brought out into a more humid environment and swells to 8 microns. A recording is made with 532 nm. The plate is then placed in warm water and swells to a thickness of 10 microns but is also being hardened. This hardening is happening as a sine curve centered on the UNEXPOSED regions as getting the maximum hardening with the EXPOSED getting the least hardening. The exposed regions are mostly hardened at the 8 micron thickness during the exposure. But the chemical hardening (Fixer) is happening in a liquid and we are saying the thickness is at 10 microns. Now how fast are you hardening in that liquid (how much hardener is present)? And what is the temperature? And how much AmDi are we removing? We then place the hologram in the alcohol. The alcohol makes the gel freeze (hardened to a brittle state). Now if you use high temp alcohols and run through alcohol very fast water is pushed down to the deeper layers where is swells those fringes and when they finally get dehydrated by the alcohol they are thicker then the fringes near the surface. This yields a broadband hologram and while the final thickness of the fringes may vary +15% to -15% the final thickness of the emulsion may still be 8 microns. But if the emulsion is dried very gradually with a lower temperature the emulsion thickness may go from the 10 microns swollen in the water to 9 microns and yields a greenish-yellow hologram. So elevate water temp and you get a more swollen state ready for alcohol processing. Lower water temp and you get a less swollen state ready to be alcohol processed. Now process the hologram in too high a temperature water and this breaks the bonds and when we dry out in alcohol the fringes collapse and we get a deep green or blue hologram. This one may measure at 6 microns or so.

Also, one byproduct of the nature of gelatin is you're going to lose some of the gelatin itself in the processing steps. So it is important to "freeze" those fringes to the desired wavelength thickness after the removal of AmDi, gelatin and water.

So I guess what I am saying is no matter how humid the ambient air is when you shoot the hologram, you can compensate for it with processing to yield a blue shift, red shift, same wavelength color or broadband final hologram. But remember, if the DCG sets too long it naturally cross links and this inhibits the whole process. We want to get the water out of the heavily waterlogged areas (unexposed) and replace that water with air for a good Delta index of refraction.

A very simple thought is if the hologram replays at a shorter wavelength it shrunk with relation to its size when exposed. If it replays a longer wavelength is expanded and you can adjust processing to move in the direction you want.

Hope I didn’t' go over board on this....

Joe Farina Wed Oct 24, 2007 12:14 pm #15
It's too bad Richard Rallison doesn't participate in the discussions (although I think he's a memeber). We could really learn a lot about DCG from him.

Anyway, the only reference concerning wet "drying" of DCG is the following comment by Rallison:

"Extremely thick (25 microns or more) emulsions....sometimes pull off the substrate if not annealed in a wet oven."

As I recall, Ed mentioned that there was another reason for gelling or drying (or storage) under wet conditions, according to Rallison. What was it, Ed?

Regarding storage after coating, my Lake Forest tutorial manual has some interesting comments (again by Rallison):

"DCG stores well at low humidity in a refrigerator or freezer but containers must prevent contamination, condensation, freezer burn and frost which can all destroy surface quality. Film of 10 or 20 microns or more store best and are good for at least a year. At room temperature and 50% RH, thin films are good for a few hours, thick films typically last a week or more. The addition of a small quantity of TMG to the mixture will greatly increase storage time at room temperature by increasing the pH."

In my opinion, there are great advantages to storing film in the refrigerator. Note above that Rallison says that coated plates (in the 10 nto 20 micron range) are good for a year or more. DCG holographers know how much of a trial-and-error process it is. So having a substantial number of plates, made from the same batch, is extremely helpful when adjusting parameters to get good results. You may have to go through half a dozen plates or more before getting your feet on the ground. If you have to mix and coat another batch of plates during the course of the trial-and-error work, you might be introducing variables that could complicate (considerably) the fine-tuning process.

I currently coat 16 plates at a time, and store them in a refrigerator. So it's a very simple matter to make a test exposure when needed, I just take a plate from the fridge, zap it with a blow drier set on warm (so that the condensation just doesn't lay there to potentially form large damaging droplets), let it reach the same temperature as the room (maybe 5 minutes for 2.5 X 2.5 inch plates), then expose.
