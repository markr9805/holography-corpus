Ed Wesly Wed Jun 21, 2006 7:10 pm #3
Sometimes you find these types of images included as accessories for laser pointers, where you put a cap on the laser and an image of some sort appears near to the laser spot.

These things are usually interference fringes written by an e-beam machine, instead of a conventional expose and develop process. Unbelievably, something they said could never be done at the dawn of holography, computing and writing fringes, can be done now.

If you expand these projected images or in the case of John's glasses, put an undiverged beam through the film, by letting them grow over the distance of a couple rooms, you will see that they are a collection of points. The fringes for each one of those points can be calculated; it is a variation of the zone plate formula, since the image is usually fairly close to the zero order.

What writes these fringes is an e-beam machine, which is essentially an electron microscope. But instead of the electron beam sweeping through the sample space in a regular raster pattern like a TV screen, the beam is directed kind of like a laser light show pattern to expose a photo-resist plate in the chamber where the sample for the electron microscope should go.

The resist is developed like resists normally are, and a tool for embossing is produced. Sometimes interesting non-linear effects can be generated, for instance in John's example the image is in the 2nd order but not the first. This is because the e-beam exposure is more or less on or off, and it doesn't create the sinusoidal wave recording that interference fringes are capable of. It is like a binary interference pattern.

I had approached a group at University of Wisconsin at Madison about using their e-beam writer to make some patterns when I used to work for a place that shall remain nameless. The device was located in an Electrical Engineering Department, and they used it to write nano-masks for making chips.

However this machine was a '60's era Zeiss Electron Microscope being driven by an '80's era Digital PDP-11 computer, and our holo files were too much for them. But if anyone is interested in such a project, let's get a used electron microscope and...

These "holograms" are pretty much in the domain of secure printing, that is why we don't see too many of them. Plus they are time-consuming to make, since there is a lot of information per mm^2.

The Kinegrams of Kurz are one such example. The best source for these images that I am aware of is:

[http://www.computer-holography.com](http://www.computer-holography.com)
