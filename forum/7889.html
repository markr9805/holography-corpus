Ed Wesly Mon Jul 06, 2009 11:02 pm #4
Let’s see what went wrong or right, without getting too Dinesh-like. (Don’t forget, I am the Math-Science department of in an art school by day, holographer by night!)

“First I get the radius of the circular beam, which is 10.16 Cm (Radius)” OK

“I then get the square (10.16 x 10.16) = 103.2256 Cm^2

I then Work out the area using Pi r^

3.142 x 103.2256 = 324.3348 (area of the beam)” Cool!

“I then work out the plate area... 12.7 Cm x 10.16 Cm = 129.032 Cm^2” Irrelevent!

“I also need to know the laser energy which is 200mW x 1000 = 200000.00 uJ” (per second is what you forget, but not necessary, you got that below)

“I then divide the laser energy by the beam area 200000.00 x 324.3348 = 616.6467 uJ/second” but what is forgotten here is the area dimension, the square centimeter. So it should be written (let’s round it off for simplicity) is 600 microJoules/second/square centimeter, a bit easier to understand in terms of flux (don’t you just love that word!), or a little bit easier to understand, 600 microWatts/cm^2. (For those of you wondering where that comes from, a Joule is Watt-seconds, the hyphen standing in not for subtraction, but multiplication; Watts times seconds = Joules. Going the other way, a Watt = Joules divided by seconds, or Joules/seconds. A Watt is a unit of power; a Joule is a unit of energy, which is power times time, although if one said power over time, you get the impression of power in the numerator and time in the denominator of a fraction, which would be confusing in the case of the rest of the calculations. But energy is how much power is delivered during a certain length of time. Which is why energy units are used to determine exposure times, since exposure = Intensity X time.

“I need to know how much energy is hitting the plate: 616.6467 x 129.032 (area of the plate) = 79567.16” This calculation didn’t need to be made. We know with your laser power distributed over that much surface area we have a flux of approximately 600 uW/cm^2

“I can now start to work out the exposure time: 3000uJ x 129.032 (plate area) = 387096.00” In the exposure equation above, we use intensity X time = exposure dose. The desired time is what we are looking for, so exposure dose divided by intensity = time, and before substituting values we take the 3 mJ/cm^2 and change it to 3000 uJ/cm^2, but we are going to divide by 600 uW/cm^2 so we need to make the units consistent, so the ED (exposure dose, I love using that acronym in this case, it should become the official vocabulary!) becomes 3000 uW seconds.

So now calculating, we have 3000 uW seconds/cm^2 divided by 600 uW/cm^2 = 5 seconds, which is on a par with your calculation!

You can see how important it is to keep the units involved in the calculations, so that the uW and cm^2 cancel out and all you are left with is the time unit. I know, that is the least fun part of solving equations like this, but you really need to use them to verify that you have a sensible answer.

5” for a photopolymer seems almost too short, however 200 mW spread over a 4 by 5” plate is actually pretty hot! However, like Jeff says, test strips (like putting four exposure quadrants on one plate) is a good idea, as I don’t usually put much faith in the manufacturers’ figures as absolute, but starting points for the expo series. (No slight intended, Sergio, but Agfa and Slavich are way off in some cases!)

Oh yeah, if you want to include the attenuation caused by an off-axis beam, multiply the above by the cosine of the reference angle. Or if you are metering with a probe that can be placed on the plateholder at the same angle as the holographic plate, then that automatically compensates for the angle.

And the Gaussian beam profile is another thing to deal with, but an expo series of like 2.5, 5, 10 and 20 seconds should get you some results!

salvee Sun Jul 12, 2009 9:53 am #9
As pointed out by Ed, the gaussian beam profile will mean that the intensity in the beam at the edges of the 8cm circle will be substantially lower than at its centre.

I would move the plate much further down stream- to a point where the beam diameter is 16cm. This will provide a much more even illumination over the recording plate. Since the diameter has doubled (x2), the area will quadruple (x2 squared). So per square centimeter the intensity will be divided by a factor of 4.

The exposure will now have to be 4 times the original exposure- approximately 20 seconds.
