JohnFP Tue Sep 25, 2007 6:31 pm #4
I find I almost always use the 5x and 10x for the reference beams with large collimators and with no collimator, and occasionally the 20x if the collimator is small with a short FL.

If your not using a collimator yet, the a 5x would be the best choice if you can back the spatial filter up that far so as to get your desired illumination. The longer the fl of the lens the less diverging it will be. This will help you when you get a collimator so you not a mile from the collimator to get the conjugate (convering at same rate as the divergence of the original reference beam).

Remember, you want the beam diameter bigger then your plate so that you get a more even coverage of light. If your using a collimator that is about the size of the plate, then the light diameter should be a little bigger then the collimator.

Tom B. Sat Sep 29, 2007 5:50 am #10
> Justin W: If I understand correctly, I will want to find the point where said spatial filter has expanded my beam somewhat larger than my plate, and insert there a parabolic mirror that has a focal length of that very distance(?)... This parabolic mirror will reflect a collimated beam at that time, but.... seeing as how I do not want a collimated beam of light reflecting straight back to my spatial filter, I must cock the parabolic mirror at an angle, yes? You are correct on both points, but fortunately small errors in collimation and other minor optical aberrations are not at all noticeable in simple display holograms if the objects are not too far from the plate. When using a mirror collimator, you want to use a folded setup so that the angle between the expanding beam going to the mirror and the reflected beam to the plate is as small as possible. In this type of setup, often a black card or other light barrier is helpful in making sure that only the collimated light from the mirror hits the plate and object.
