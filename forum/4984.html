Dinesh Sat Aug 20, 2005 1:29 pm #25
I also think it depends on what you mean by "projection". If you take the classical idea of a projection as the magnification and focusing of an amplitude modulated surface onto a larger surface, such as a slide projected on a screen, then this is simply the recreation of a particular amplitude modulation and clearly a hologrm is not a projection and neither is a rainbow. If you take any wavefront, magnify it and focus it at another pojnt in space, such as the curved mirror images of eg the Haunted House ghosts, then again neither a hologram or a rainbow are projections. In the curved mirror case, the original wavefront of an object is copied and displaced on a larger scale by means of the mirror. The wavefront being produced by a hologram is generated by diffraction off a fringe structure and is not a copy or a displacement of an existing wavefront. The rainbow is caused by collimated sunlight through reflections and refractions - again, not a copy of an existing wavefront. In both hologram and rainbow, a new wavefront is created from another, different wavefront. If the existence of any image in space is considered as a projection, then reality itself must be seen as a projection, since any tactile sense is independant of visual stimulation. A hologram of a cat is just as much a projection as is a real cat which you cannot touch, especially since no visual test can distinguish between a hologram of a cat and a real cat from any given viewpoint. They're the same wavefront!

In the example of the H2, the H2 captures an interference pattern arising from the wavefront of the H1 and the wavefront of the reference. Both wavefronts are necessary, and yet the reference beam is not thought of as a projection. In my view, the H2 is the result of two wavefronts, either of which can be independantly created. So, if you had a small box with fine droplets of suspended water, shone collimated white light through it, placed a plate at an appropriiate distance and zapped it with a reference, you'd get a hologram of a rainbow. Recording it may be tough, since you'd need truly panchromatic film! A little more simple - have two H1's on either side of a plate. Have the H1 image of one reference the H1 image of the other. Let's say an H1 with a cat and an H1 of a model of the Enterprise (The old one, I'm a fan of the old one!). Now, if the resulting H2 is placed in front of the real Enterprise, brightly lit, then the hologram of the cat shows up. Which is now the projection?

Rant Phase 1: A rainbow has nothing to do with diffraction, projection, or wavefront reconstruction. When you're looking at a rainbow you are looking at the dispersed output of tons of spherical "prisms". (Prisms are in quotes because a sphere could not be a prism in the sense of the mathematical definition of "a solid having regular polygons for the bases and rectangles for its sides." But a sphere can be a dispersing element like the ever popular equilateral triangle prism. Read on.)

Think of the circumstance of when you see a rainbow. The sun is directly behind you and you are looking into the distance, your eyes pointing in the direction of your attached shadow, at a curtain of raindrops.

The sunlight coming from behind you passes you by, and enters the drops in front of you. The paraxial rays (through the dead nuts center and a bit further out) pass through the drop, and come to a focus very close to the outside surface of the water globule. After the focus, the cone of light expands quite rapidly, going off in many other directions other than parallel to the original incident ray. To an observer on the opposite side of the rain curtain compared our position the sunlight is attenuated, a dark curtain, not totally because of reflectivity and density but of a change in direction due to refraction.

Rays of light incident on the whole convex hemisphere of the drop that is looking at the sunlight are refracted inward as they enter the dihydrous oxide. We saw what happens to the central rays above, but after a certain distance from the center (the actual distance is left as an exercise to the reader!) the rays cannot escape the drop because their angle of incidence is >= the critical angle. The are Totally Internally Reflected.

The rays at the top of the drop are reflected downward by the concave surface they are now encountering on their exit path. But they are not reflected anti-parallel to their original angle of incidence since we are not dealing with a corner cube.

In the case of the rays of light that entered the very tippy-top of the drop these rays are reflected straight down to earth with an angle between 40 and 42 degrees compared to the original incident sunlight.

To find the angle with precision, the wavelength needs to be specified, as the white sunlight disperses at the first surface entrance refraction and a bit more at the second surface exit refraction. The red angle is not the same as the blue angle. (Again, the reader could work out the table of angles versus wavelengths, but better still look at one fo the popular books on optics, such as Seeing the Light, Falk, Brill & Stork, p.65; Light Science, Rossing & Chiaverena, p.3; The Universe of Light, Bragg, p.110. The scenario created above is related to the ray traces above by looking at the illustrations not dead on by in the plane of the edge of the paper! {Looking at the page's edge} [Somebody should illustrate optics books with holograms of these 3-D vectors!]

If the angles were computed, it would be found that the even further from the center, the edge rays would eventually no longer refract out of the drop but bango! get TIR'ed again back into the drop! Now this time they are heading skyward plus or minus 51 degrees relative to the incident sunlight, in addition to getting dispersed twice, but this spectrum is heading skyward, so only planes could see this rainbow!

But we haven't discussed the edge rays that entered on the earthward side of the drop. In this area, the rays that are singly TIR'ed head heavenward, and the double reflections head down to the devil, or us if we are so lucky, and this is the origin of the secondary rainbow. But this is a rare occasion, and very hard to photograph.

And rays even further to the edge would be triply TIR'ed, and end up who knows where but I did see in an Amateur Scientist in Scientific American that there theoretically could be 21 different rainbows! (A calculation best left to Dinesh!)

This dispersion that ends up in our eyes is from rays entering along a diameter that is perfectly vertical. Rays that are entering the edge that are at say, one o'clock compared to the vertical will not only do the dance above but come out at a skew, anti-altazimuthal to be sure, i.e., the one o'clock comes out at seven direction.

The top of the 'bow is directly in front of us. When your eye shifts to the right, the ray that will enter it will not come from the same level in the curtain of raindrops as the one that is at the top of the drop but from one lower. (To help imagine this in 3-D, look at the thin edge of one of the above optics books along the line of the downward heading first rainbow ray. Then shift your gaze, and you should be able to envision approximately where the dispersed and TIR'ed and skewed ray should be coming from.)

I saw in a comic book in my youth a Science Fact that a viewer in an airplane should be able to see a completely circular rainbow rainbow! Consider the above and you should be able to see how this could be true! But I haven't seen any photos (but I haven't looked hard!)

There exists a corresponding physical (wave-like) optical explanation.

As I said, light itself is invisible. You can't see light. You need matter in order to make light visible. If you consider a hologram as a series of lenses embedded into the emulsion of different focal lengths, then a reconstruction beam essentially is a beam that hits these lenses and is focused by them to different parts of space. Like any real lenses, the focal point depends on the origin point of the light, if the light comes from infinity, ie it's collimated, then the light will focus at the focal length of the lens. If not, the light will focus somewhere beyond the focal length. Now you have this multiple set of focused points of light. At these points, if matter is present, such as a fog of particles from a mist, then this matter interacts with the light and sends it out as a spherical, outward wave. The sum of all the focused points of light will create an image, provided the fog is extensive enough and lasts long enough. However, if there's no matter there, the light will simply diverge from the focused point. With nothing there to scatter it, whatever image data it contains will not be seen. If you changed the index at the focal point by, eg, creating a "hot spot", then the light at the focal point will change it's velocity, since the velocity of light depends on the index, but will still be invisible. You need some form of matter to scatter the light. It's vaguely possible to view the collection of points, and hence the image, by observing the area of focus with an IR camera if you can get the temperature differential high enough and/or your IR camera was sensitive enough. It's also possible that if the index change were high enough (and local enough) you'd get reflection due to the interface between two indices, but the index change needs to be very rapid. If the index change occurs within roughly lambda/2, the Fresnel equations will apply and the index interface will scatter. But you're talking a significant change in temperature within roughly 250nm for a spot that itself is about a micron or two across. I doubt it'd be stable for very long.

Bill[color=green:86e90251a1][/color:86e90251a1][color=red:86e90251a1][/color:86e90251a1][/quote:86e90251a1]

Not quite. When light propagates through a medium, let's say a slab of glass, the molecules of the glass are intersperced at roughly the same distance. As the light propagates, each molecule vibrates due to incoming light and creates light in all directions. Only the forward proagations actually "go through", not the side and backwards propagations. Hence a beam of light in the glass does not go to the sides or the back, only forward. Now if you slice the glass at some intermediate point, you have back reflection where none existed before. This is because there is an "abrupt" change in molecular density. The backwards propagating light from the sliced interface of the glass meets a "new set" of molecules (air molecules) which is not as dense as the ones in the glass. Hence this new set of molecules can no longer destructivly interfere with the backwards propagating light from the interior molecules of the glass and the backward propagating light comes into existence. However, this change must exist fairly quickly. If the change in molecular density is slow, the change in density is slow and will not create a backward propagation. Depending on the exact nature of the rate of falling off of molecular distances, the backwards beam will simply slowly die off over a few molecular diameters. Same with a "hot air bubble. The heat creates a different molecular density. hence the distinction between the molecular density of the "hot spot" must be distinct and quick. If the heat gradually cools down over a few molecular distances, the density difference is too slow and the back propagation will not be strong.

The shimmering image on the road is not caused by reflections, but by refractions. Hot air has a lower density, and hence a lower refractive index. What you have is not an abrupt reflective surface, but a startified medium of slow index change. As the heat shimmers, the reflected light from the road surface takes different paths, trying to find the "path of minimum time" through media in which it's velocity is constantly changing over small distances. You'll also notice that you don't get that same shimmering on a cloudy day. No reflection off the road surface, except when car headlights reflect off it over a bump in the road. Imagine water going through a sponge. A uniform sponge will allow uniform flow. However, if you have a "Maxwells demon" constantly and sporadically changing the local density of the sponge, the path of the water will be erratic as it wanders through the sponge.

Your "hot spot" will effectively behave the same. Unless it's very hot and highly localised, some of the incoming light will refract away from the direct path, but only by a few degrees.

By the way, only the press think Benton invented the embossing technique. He "invented" the techniqure of viewing display HIGS in white light by restricting the aperture. and so the dispersion. The so-called "Rainbow".

The fact that this is the main vehicle for embossed holography came from the fact that the spatial frequencies of these HIGS are low enough to capture image data as a surface profile. The average spatial frequency of a rainbow HIG is approx 1000 l/mm which is achievable in materials such as photoresist and thermoplastics (the frequency response of photoresists ca 1977 was approx 1500 l/mm with the response function falling exponentially at around 1200 l/mm). The average spatial frequency of a reflection HIG is about 3000 l/mm - too high for resists of the period and even now. Of course, once the image data can be captured as a surface profile, it was possible by electroforming to copy this data and so mass produce it.
