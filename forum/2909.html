Dinesh Sun Nov 21, 2004 4:55 pm #3
Pretty much what Phil said. Most commercial holographers work in relatively small studios and pretty much set their own prices. Since holography is not a "standard" object like, say, an umbrella, there is no "market price". I'm not talking about these embossed jobs that come out in the thousands or millions. These have a definite niche in terms of security or product identification. For display pieces on glass mostly holograms are seen more as a craft item than a collectible (although there are collectible holograms known to holographers such as Stations of the Cross or Rita) and so is sold based on the location, image concept, setting etc. A hologram of ours, the sailboat, sold for more when sold in a seaside town with the name of the town inscribed in the hologram because it fit the theme. There are also questions of markup and what the public will pay for a hologram (not much!). There are other factors. The longest procedure in making a hologram is lighting. You can simply front light it or light it from two different directions, and zap it in minutes, or you can spend days lighting it. We (actually Joy, not me) usually spend about 3 to 5 hours lighting a piece. All of this needs to be factored into the cost of any one piece. While I've never made one of these "optical effect" holograms, I wouldn't like to light one, it must not be easy.

I want to help you to compare the both holograms, I am making holograms and collecting holograms. I have both microsocopes in my collection.

In the rest of my posting i use the following shortcuts:

A: Microscope from Dutch Holographic Lab

B: alternative, smaller Microscope

First, in both of these holograms you can "look in the microscope" as if you

do with a real one. This means you go with your eye at the position of the ocular and see what's in it. This is a special technique in holography, known as "key hole technique". There are not many commercial holograms which use this technique, about 10 I think.

There are a few differences between the holograms:

These don't really affect price:

- object you see in the microscope:

In A you see an electronic chip in the microscope. This is nice, you can

also see the bonding wires on the chip.

In B you see an fly in the microscope. This is also nice, you can

see the hairs, and legs, and so on.

- A comes out nearly 25cm of the hologram plane, B comes out about 5 cm. This effect is really cool in hologram A, there are only a few reflection

holograms where the object comes out of the plane so far!

Wow! I never saw this before. You think you can really touch it. A is one of my favourite holograms in my collection.

These do affect collector price:

- size: A is about 8x10" (20x25cm), B is about 4x5" (10x12cm)

- material: A is on glass, B is on film, glass holograms have mostly a higher quality, and they can't curl. Most collector holograms are on glass.

- number of copies: This is hard to say how many copies from a hologram exist, if it is not numbered. Here I would estimate that from A exist perhaps a few hundreds, or even less. From B I think you can be sure that there are much more, perhaps thousands. B is a sort of massproduced hologram.

- holographer: A is a hologram from a well known holographer in the business, and nearly everyone who knows the microscope, knows that it is from Walter Spierings. I would say A is definitely a collector item, you can find pictures of it in books, for example in "practical holography" from Graham Saxby I think there is a picture of it. B is not known as such a popular object. Most collectors and holographers know it, but not many know who has made it.

- Year of production. This is hard to say. Holograms from the 70s and 80s are normally more expensive than newer ones. I think the master work form A is from 1984, by the way no one knows when the copy itself has been recorded. In hologram B there isn't a date known, but I think it's about eight to ten years newer than A.

In the end it's on you to decide what money you want to spend for a hologram.

I hope it helps you a bit.

Dinesh Wed Nov 24, 2004 3:49 pm #10
[quote:05fa669df7="strolls"]Do you have any advice on lighting for a n00b..? What can be achieved by taking more care over lighting, and what should one be considering when lighting a piece..?[/quote:05fa669df7]

It's one of those questions where you can write entire books! Danny gave us two talks on lighting holograms in both PCGGI ad PCGGII drawing from his knowledge and experience as a photographer.

Basically, I would look at shadows. If the object illumination goes sideways, throwing a shadow sideways, and the viewer sees the hologram from an overhead source, the shadows look wrong. The viewer may know it's a hologram but still feel "something wrong" about an overhead source throwing a shadow sideways. Also, use shadows to create an effect. Sometimes shadows can create a sense of depth or give an object a character. Sometimes the subject matter can suggest lightings. You'd wouldn't shoot a plane straight on or with a bottom lighting. You'd light it from the top with a weak bottom light so it gives the effect of being lit by a bright sky from above while getting reflected light from the ground below. If you do figures or animals, most people instinctively look at faces, so highlight the face, but don't just front light, it'll look shallow and featureless. Light it from a slight angle so that maybe the nose or eyelids throw a weak shadow. For religious figures, angels etc, a strong overhead light and a weak front light creates that "religious" look. Danny's used a spot light to enhance the effect. ( [http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1791](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1791) ).

Think backwards to what the viewer will see when the final hologram is viewed and see the piece as a window on reality.
