That is a good article for interest. But I contend that the best way of dealing with ascorbate developer stock and it is a way we have been successfully using for some years in our labs is to "A and B" it.

For A we have a 500ml bottle with:

20g ascorbic acid

3g Metol (4-methylaminophenol sulfate)

and top it up with 500ml deionized water

for B we have a 500 ml bottle of

50g sodium carbonate anhydrous

15g sodium hydroxide

top up with 500 ml deionized water.

(This one should be labeled "very caustic" )

Just use equal volumes of A and B from then on.

Now there are 3 bonus points for using Metol instead of phenidone.

1) is that phenidone is quite a strong silver halide solvent and tests have proved that metol gives brighter holograms.

2)The second point is that metol has a hardening action on gelatin and its effect on speeding up the development time over what you would have with just alkaline ascorbate means that even notoriously soft emulsions

juch as PFG-03 can be in and out of the developer bath into a stop bath (~5% acetic acid ) in around 20 seconds, before the gelatin is seriously attacked. Assuming of course your exposure level was good enough.

3) Metol is a weaker reducing agent or developer than alkaline ascorbic acid.

When Metol gets oxidized it goes really dark brown so this is a useful indicator to tell you when your bath is exhausted because it wont go severely dark until most of the ascorbate has been oxidized. A mild yellowing like weak tea is quite OK .

Dont forget to use the floating dish method of 2 closely fitting plastic dishes with the upper dish keeping most of the air out as it floats and acting as convenient agitator as well.

Acid ascorbate in the stock soln A will not seriously oxidize for a year.

(Slight yellowing is perfectly OK . )

jeff-blyth Wed Jan 11, 2006 11:44 am #12
> MichaelH: Yes, it's a rehalogenating bleach, EDTA.

OK that is the best type (DEwise) for Denisyuk reflection H's but not transmission ones.

So what happens with that type used WITHOUT FIXING in thiosulfate, is that the developed up silver gets re-oxidized to AgBr. But instead of returning you to square one and leaving you with a uniform coating and distribution of AgBr again just as it was before you exposed , it is energetically more favorable for the re-made AgBr to move over to the adjacent dark fringe made up of virgin AgBr and grow onto that dark fringe using the virgin AgBr grains as seeding centers. That actually requires the bleach solution to have some AgBr solvation ability to enable this carry over effect to occur. It has been found not to occur if the fringe spacing is too great as happens often with transmission Hs(ref. Hariharan). It also cannot happen if the bleach solution has no AgBr solvation ability such as with iodine-alcohol bleach. So the great thing about this carryover effect is that it theoretically uses all the original Ag in your emulsion to make the fringes whereas if you have to use fix you would have removed about half of your original silver content in the thiosulfate solution. The not good thing about it though is that the original

sensitizing dyes become chemically locked into AgBr grains making the emulsion very vulnerable to print out. (This doesnt happen with dev-fix-bleach because the dyes get freed up and removed either in the fix or with the help of a bit of alcohol in the final rinse. This is convenient for transmission Hs where printout can be a more serious issue.) So from

all this one can say that provided your carry over bleach system has worked cleanly and left you with empty antinodes with all the silver migrated onto the adjacent nodes, then by using ascorbate you are gong to cause total printout of all that AgBr with its residual dyes. You can then re-bleach it.

But it would be best to kill off those dyes so that they dont keep reforming absorption Hs instead of the more efficient phase Hs .

I found accidentally in a previous thread that pre-sensitizing BBplates with ascorbate mixed with Metol had a drastic effect of killing or inactivating those sensitizing dyes. This was found to be all due to Metol. So now we

could actually do with this dye killing effect! So that means putting metol in with your ascorbate(or just Metol alone would be fine) . Another sure fire way and probably the best is to kill off the dyes with dichromate soln.

But dont use dichromate as the second bleach as of course it is a reversal bleach and will remove the silver which is the last thing we want.

So I recommend doing the second bleach step again with ferric EDTA and then give it a good rinse in a dichromate bath. That will make it highly print-out resistant from then on. I hope I have not confused anyone with all this!

I think there's been indeed some confusion here about the nature of that ascorbate post-treatment.
