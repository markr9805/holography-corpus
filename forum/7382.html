Well info, take heart, your first effort looked much better than mine.

If you rotate the hologram and the imagine flashes bright then disappears then John is correct about it is most likely movement.

I can see your photo off your site and a couple of things come to mine.

The fact that it is green in color is likely to be over exposure. 30 minutes is a really long time (and could be related to your movement issue). I have a 100mw green system and it takes me abou 3-4 minutes with a slightly smaller beam diameter. Given it is a square function I would guess 30 minutes is a bit on the high side.

Here is what I suggest:

Try smaller glass (or cut the one you have in 4 pieces). Make your beam size smaller (or move the object closer) This would reduce exposure time

Place the plate right on the object (like a coin(s)) to reduce the possibility of movement

Bracket your exposure times by placing a card on top of the object and allowing portions to be exposed in intervals of a few minutes each, or simply cut a plate in 4 pieces and try a (let's say) 10 minute, 15, 20 and 30 minute exposure.

Once you find your optimal exposure time, let's start looking at your fixer. Your plate looks a little milky and I am unsure about you fixer mixture. But let's cross the bridge when we get to it.

Lastly, can you let us know how you coat and the type of gelatine that you use. The film can be a bit thick or the gelatin can be of low bloom strenght.

Best of luck and strap on your safety helmet as you enter the world of DCG

Good Start!

There are various combinations that will lead to milky/hazy DCG. But, right off the bat I would recommend for you(and everyone) to humidify your film @ 45-60% RH for 30 min /6hrs. (Depending on how thick your film is) You will be able to lower your exposure time, and your development time dramatically. I don't know if everyone is doing this??? But everyone should!!!!

Just head down to your local drugstore and grab a room humidifier. Run it in the room that you are going to shoot the hologram in. Leave your plates out in the room for the above-mentioned time (experiment with it).

Warning! If you move your film plates from a humid environment (storage) into a dry one (laser lab) you can get gnarly movement.

Warning! Clear film with a blue image equals too much humidity!!!!

Super clear, super broad-band holos!

This works. Do it!

Seventh Son Thu Jun 19, 2008 6:08 pm #15
I mistakenly assumed certain run conditions when I gave the humidity advice.

First, absolutely let your film stabilize after you have coated it. I wait 24hrs.

Secondly, I assumed that we were talking about using a decent gelatin.

I change my formula according to the %RH of where I’m working. I use different film formulas for winter, and summer. I always use humidity! I want my gelatin hard before I humidify it. If I make a batch that is too soft, then I can get mad variation if it’s say raining, or an especially humid day.

Dutchelm05- Use your film as the litmus test. If you are making nice gold holograms that are hazy then absolutely humidify your film (slow and steady humidity wins!) If you are making blue/green hazy images, then humidify, and lower fix time. If you are making crystal clear holos then pay no attention to my comments.

Why does it work? I can’t dazzle you with science speak on this one. But I tend to think of my film in terms of hard or soft. Harder film is narrower, and tends to be hazy. Soft film (according to formulation) likes to be broad, and will be clear with the proper pre-humidification.

Sincerely, George W. Bush

oui.

Film freshness (film age) - young broadband with milky tendency, old narrow band and clear tendency

young milky -yes. Old clear-yes. But the narrow/broad comment speaks of possible weak formulation?

Room Temp (exposure)- Warmer increased sensitivity

If your film is that sensitive, your formula iswacked. This is part of the DCG humidity thread check it.

Room Humidity (exposure) - More humid increased sensitivity

This is part of the DCG humidity thread check it.

Exposure Time - longer harder bluer narrower banded, shorter softer redder broader banded.

Not at all.

Dark reaction time - longer harder bluer narrower banded, shorter softer redder broader banded

This tends to be too much of a wild card variable. I don't employ it.

Fixer Time - too short milky soft , too long harder bluer

short milky-yes. Too long-can you go to long? Formulation!

Light or heat fixing (if applicable) - low light/heat softer broader banded milky, lots light harder bluer narrow band.

Don't know.

H2O rinse time - too short non clear film.

H2O rinse temperature - hotter broadband, lower narrow band

YES!

Having done a couple of SHSG holograms over the last weeks, I think I now understand the problems involved with the „fixing“ step in DCG.

With SHSG fixing is one of the most crucial steps. When a undiluted fixing solution is used, the layer may collapse. So one way to get around this is making a very diluted fixer and adding a compound to prevent excessive gelatin swelling. Most often this is sodium sulfate.

To come back to DCG, in the paper (Improving the remarkable photosensitivity of dichromated...in green laser light) you (Blyth/Lowe/Pecora that is) wrote:

> ...Kodak lists the constituents of part A as:40-45% water; 42% ammonium thiosulfate; 5-10% sodium acetate; 1-5% boric acid, 1-5% ammonium sulfite, 1-5% acetic acid; <1% sodium sulfite.

That's quite a bit of salts! As Jeff has pointed out several times, the important part only is the sulfite, which does the Cr reduction. Principally, the remaining stuff could be left out, since it doesn't contribute to Cr reduction.

However, these salts may play another important part here: they may actually prevent excessive gelatin swelling.

As I mentioned sometime ago, when I did a few DCG tests last summer, I did use metabisulfite within a 50% alcohol (IPA) solution. This seemed to have an impact on wavelength/bandwidth of the final image.

So based on that, I guess you may add a small amount of metabisulfite to a solution that controls gelatin swelling (based on say, sodium sulfate, IPA, glycerol etc. etc.).

As for the hardening agent my guess would be that alum may not be the best choice here - since it may compete with Cr hardening.
