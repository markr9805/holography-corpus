DJ Mathson Mon Aug 31, 2009 5:36 pm #2
It depends on the power of the two light sources. Since the cones are more sensitive to green I don´t think it would be an average if you use red and green light with the same power. I just tested a program that tries to calculate the perceived color when laser beams are combined and reflected from a white surface. According to this program, to get a pure white reflection you need, as an example, approximately 60, 60 and 100 mW of 457, 532 and 640 nm respectively. To get yellow you would need approx 6/10 of 633 nm and 4/10 of 532 nm, or 90 mW and 60 mW as an example. If you would use a longer red wavelength like 660 nm you would need an even higher amount of this wavelength, like 300 mW, since the eyes are less sensitive to this wavelength. Since both a yellow laser wavelength, or a combination of a green and a red wavelength, stimulate the 'red' and 'green' cones in a similar way the perceived result should be similar.
DJ Mathson Mon Aug 31, 2009 5:49 pm #3
To clarify, though, the yellow laser would be slightly more saturated than the right combination of a green and red laser.

DJ Mathson Mon Aug 31, 2009 6:38 pm #5
> Joe Farina: Thanks DJ. Say for example we tried 4/10ths at 532 and 6/10ths at 633, and made an "ideal" hologram of a white object. Do you think the image might be narrowband at ~583?

Short answer, for an ideal reflection hologram, no. I think you would get fringes for 633 and 532 nm respectively. So, if you would shine yellow light of, say, 583 nm on the hologram, this light would be filtered out. If you however would illuminate the hologram with white light (broadband), or with the proper combination of 633 and 532 nm, you would perceive the reflected light as yellow, and this hologram would look similar to a narrowband reflection hologram made by a yellow laser replayed by broadband white light or yellow light.

The perception of colors is very interesting, and complicated, since it is a mix of physics, physiology and psychology. Interesting indeed.
DJ Mathson Mon Aug 31, 2009 7:19 pm #6
Put in another way. Say you make two reflection holograms of a white object; one with a combined red and green laser, and the other with a single wavelength yellow laser. When you illuminate the holograms with white broadband light the first will reflect red and green light, and the other will reflect yellow light, but both will be perceived as yellow. If you then look at the first hologram through a red or a green narrowband filter, you will see a red or a green image, respectively. But if you look through either of the filters on the second hologram, it will turn dark.

Kaveh Tue Sep 01, 2009 11:43 am #12
> JohnFP: Our primary colors are Red, Green and BlueOur additive colors are Yellow, Cyan and Magenta

John, yellow, cyan and magenta are subtractive primary colors, not additive. They work by removing around a third of the visible spectrum each, so all three dyes together would give you black, at least ideally.

To simplify things, if you see an object as yellow, it is either because it is reflecting a high proportion of the yellow part of the spectrum, or that it is reflecting red and green, and not much blue. It turns out that in nature it is usually the latter. And the yellow that you see on your computer screen is made up in the same way. Green and yellow light mixing...
