Joe Farina Thu Oct 30, 2008 8:03 pm #6
I don't know about HOEs, but two traditional ways are 1) a pair of cylindrical lenses and 2) a pair of anamorphic prisms. As for the cylinder lenses, just imagine an ordinary "telescope" but instead of the usual circular lenses, use cylinder lenses to push those long ends of the ellipse back towards the center, without doing anything to the top and bottom. A good source for inexpensive lenses is Surplus Shed. Thorlabs offers a matched set of anamorphic prisms for this purpose, but they are rather expensive.

Jeffrey Fri Oct 31, 2008 8:20 pm #10
The most light-efficient method is a lens - go ahead and twist it around off-axis to play with shapes.

Holographically, just make a transmission hologram with an object beam the shape and direction you want the HOE output to look like. Instead of a diffuse object, aim that beam right at the plate. Remember you could have the output focus at a distance (a real image), changing shape as it propagates, not just change the beam profile. But it will only be as efficient as a hologram... a big cost in an optical set-up.

Ed Wesly Mon Nov 03, 2008 10:21 pm #23
Sorry to have taken so long to get back but I’ve been busy with stuff that led me nowhere. I kind of suspected that the idea would be to circularize an elliptical beam.

Sometimes being an expert in holography means you have to say, “don’t use a hologram”. And probably the best way to circularize an elliptical diode beam is to use prisms as others have noted.

But how to design a theoretically perfect HOE, considering 100% efficiency, no screwing up the beam profile, noiselessness, etc. you would have to think about the way that the beam enters the HOE and how you want it to leave.

In another thread I described a beam combining HOE for true color holography. The way it was made was that there was a 45-degree reference beam meeting with an “object” beam along the normal at 633 nm. When the HOE was illuminated with a 633 beam at 45 degrees, it came out along the normal. The input angles for the other colors, 515 and 476, so that they emerge along the normal also can be calculated using the simple grating equation in the Wiki, an exercise I shall leave to the reader as they say in the funny papers, no, I mean the math books.

But what happened was that the output beam was no longer circular; it became ellipticized. This is understandable since the input was coming in at 45 degrees. The ratio of the major to minor axis would be in the proportion of the sine of the reference angle? I’m guessing right now, but you know what I am saying?

So to make the elliptical beam circular, the minor axis needs to be expanded. The trick would be to have the input beam come in at such an angle that the output beam along the normal would be twice as wide as the input. Height would remain the same. Preliminary napkin sketching looks like 60 degrees from the normal should do it. Collimated beams assumed, of course.

An empirical approach would be to take your elliptical diode and have it incident onto a target card until the angle of incidence gives a circular cross-section. Measure this angle, and that will be your reference angle for the input, with your output being along the normal.

There are probably other ways of doing this, but this occupied my time happily rather than doing boring grading!
