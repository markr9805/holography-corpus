Din Tue Sep 02, 2025 12:03 pm #8
> JayFritoeswrote:↑Mon Sep 01, 2025 10:31 pm: I noticed that some of the stars go from defined to streaked or blurred as you tilt the card. Any idea what's going on there?

It's a holographic aberration. As I mentioned earlier, it's important to understand that the accuracy of the holographic image depends on the illuminant. If the illuminant follows the original reference beam, you get a reasonably accurate image (the spectral profile of the illuminant aside). If the illuminant does not, the image gets distorted. This distortion of the image as a result of incorrect illumination is known as a holographic aberration. You get the same effect if you use a magnifying glass to magnify some text on paper; as you twist the lens, the text gets distorted.

In this case, by tilting the card, you twist the relative positions of the illuminant to the card - you can either twist the card, or move the lighting to some angle - and so the stars distort and blur.

Myself and wife Joy gave a paper on holographic aberrations at the 2012 ISDH convention. To test the equations, we moved the illuminant through various angles and photographed the holographic image. As you can see below, as the illuminant direction is changed, the image twists and blurs.

lobaz Wed Sep 03, 2025 9:10 am #13
> JayFritoeswrote:↑Tue Sep 02, 2025 2:48 pm: Very interesting. How come some 2D-3D holograms don’t seem to have as much, if any, aberration under white light illumination?Does the same thing happen in dot matrix holograms? If not, how could you intentionally add it?

Aberrations depend on the distance of the reconstructed object from the hologram plane, or more generally on angles. The higher, the worse. Small aberrations can be achieved when using a reference wave at small incidence (close to perpendicular) and for objects near to the hologram plane.

It is not straightforward to answer the second question. A typical dot matrix mastered DOVID shows kinetic effects, i.e., nothing floatig in space. As such things appear to be in the plane of the hologram, you cannot apply the theory of imaging aberrations. Anyway, as "aberration" is a general term meaning "deviation from the ideal", you can have "aberration" in kinetic (dot matrix) effects as well. For instance, you design an area that should appear yellow when illuminated at a specific angle. However, in practice you may illuminate the DOVID not with a perfect plane wave, but with a divergent spherical wave, and the area will not be uniformly yellow. Thus, an "aberration".

Back to the aberration related to 3D imaging. Such aberrations depend on 3D geometry and not on a specific way of DOVID mastering. If you use a dot matrix machine (such that it can make gratings of various orientations and pitches), you can imitate a structure creating 3D illusion, and it will be prone to aberrations. If you wanted to exaggerate them, you could go in a different direction: make a holographic stereogram that shows images of highly aberrated 3D scene.

lobaz Thu Sep 04, 2025 1:00 am #15
> JayFritoeswrote:↑Wed Sep 03, 2025 10:21 am: I did read that holograms made with spatial light modulators in place of a real object have very small field of views. Is that right?

Not necessarily. If you use a transmissive LCD instead of a real object and illuminate it from behind by a collimated wave, then the display will emit in a narrow angle. A hologram records this behaviour, resulting in a small field of view. But it is not the result of neither hologram nor LCD, it is the consequence of the LCD illumination. If you apply diffusers on the LCD, it will emit light in a broad angle.

In practice, one often tries to make the viewing angle quite narrow in order to make a hologram brighter. To explain: a hologram just redistributes incoming light. If it redistributes light to a wide angle, it is naturally dimmer than when light power is concentrated into much narrower angle.

lobaz Sat Sep 06, 2025 3:28 am #17
> JayFritoeswrote:↑Fri Sep 05, 2025 2:55 pm: Can you explain what an H1 pseudo is and what it does? And what does it mean to displace the H1 pseudo with a different H2 ref.

I am not sure what Dinesh actually meant. I'll try to explain some basics, terms H1, H2, ref, pseudo are in bold.

Making a hologram can be a two step process. In the first step, you make a hologram of an object. In the second step, you make a hologram of the first hologram. The first hologram is called H1, the secons is called H2.

When making a H1 hologram of the transmission type, you place an object (#) to the right (east) of the holographic recording material (a glass plate with light sensitive layer) and illuminate it from the right by an auxiliary light () called the reference wave. When you take this hologram, illuminate it with the exactly same light as was the reference wave (now called the reconstruction wave), the observer (@) located to the left (west) of H1 will see an illusion of the object at the place of the original object.

Code: [Select all](#)
` |
|
observer @ | # object
|
|
H1 reference/reconstruction
`
If you move the reconstruction light, for instance to the north, the illusion of the object also moves to the north. Remember it: changing the reconstruction wave changes the illusion.

Until now, I have been explaining very basic behaviour of a very simple hologram type.

Let's make it a bit more complex. We can make a hologram of two objects (#, $). The observer will see $ behind #; when the observer moves to the north, he will see $ behind and slightly to the side of #:

Code: [Select all](#)
` |
|
observer @ | # $
|
|
H1 reference/reconstruction
`
Let's make it even more complex: let's change direction of light rays when reconstructing. Now the reconstruction light will come from south west instead of south east:

Code: [Select all](#)
` |
|
| # $ @ observer
|
|
reconstruction H1 reference/reconstruction
`
The reconstruction light now passes through H1 and focuses at places #, $. If you are looking from the left (not in the sketch), you don't see anything as light travels from left to right (away from you). However, if you put a sheet of paper near #, you will see there a focused spot of the shape of the object # and large defocused spot of the shape of the object $. If, however, the observer moves to the right (@), hee sees a pseusoscopic image of the scene: he sees the object $ in front of #. Pseudoscopic means "flipped depth-wise". Note that the illusion is somehow weird. When looking at the original scene, you saw # in front of $, i.e., $ was partilly occluded by #. This does not change in the pseudoscopic view: $ is still partially occluded by #, even though $ is closer to you. Also, when looking at the original scene, you see the west parts of both objects. This still holds in the pseudoscopic image: you still see the west side of the objects, despite you are looking from east.

We can indeed record this pseudoscopic scene. Think about it like this: can you make a photograph of another photograph? Yes, why not. So in the same way, you can make a hologram of another hologram (or more exactly of light produced by another hologram). Just put a new holographic plate and a new reference light:

Code: [Select all](#)
` | !
| !
| # ! $
| !
| !
reconstruction1 H1 H2
reference2
`
Thus, you take H1 and illuminate it by reconstruction wave 1. You put a new holographic plate, put it somewhere and additionally illuminate it with an auxiliary reference wave 2.

Now, what happens if you Illuminate H2 with the exact replica of reference wave 2? Nothing special: When looking at H2 from the right, $ appears to be in front of H2, # behind H2. The scene is still pseudoscopic compared to the original scene.

Code: [Select all](#)
` !
!
# ! $ @ observer
!
!
H2
reference/reconstruction 2
`
Finally, we can reverse direction of the reconstruction wave for H2, which should create a pseudoscopic image of what was recorded by H2, i.e., pseudocsopic image of a pseudoscopic image. Flip depth-wise twice and you have the orthoscopic (original depth) illusion of the scene. Indeed, you have to look at it from the left:

Code: [Select all](#)
` !
!
observer @ # ! $
!
!
H2
reference 2 reconstruction 2
`
Got it? If yes, you can think about what is possible. For instance, you don't have to position reconstruction wave to the exact opposite of the reference waves, you can move it deliberately. You can do multiple exposures on one plate. You can do H3 if needed. And so on.

Din Sat Sep 06, 2025 2:49 pm #19
There's no cross talk with white light illumination. Cross talk refers to two ( or more ) lasers, each one diffracting from the other laser's intended diffraction.

To make a 2D/3D, you have to record individual planes of images, each plane separated from the other plane(s) by some distance. This depends on the relative distance between the H1, the H2 and the 2D layers in space. There are a number of ways of doing this; at ABNH, we varied the distance between the different planes, and recorded each plane on a different H1. Now, you fix the distance between the H1 and the H2, record each H1 in turn, and finally, the H2 is a recording of the different planes.

So, for example, say you wanted a 2D/3D of two planes - one consisting of letters (an A, a B, perhaps a Q maybe also an O etc) and one consisting of numbers( 1, 5, 7, 3 etc). The final H2, as seen in white light, will show a "number" plane and a "letter plane" with the number plane in front of the letter plane. - somewhat similar to the Nordstrom card, but with just two planes. You start by making two transparencies - one with numbers and one with letters; you can do this by just printing on a transparency - black numbers/letters against a white background - and inverting the colours - white number/letters against a black background. You now set up the H1 plate position, then you shoot the "number plane" say, 5 inches from the "number H1", then you shoot the "letter plane" with the letters, say, 6 inches from the "letter H1". Now, you have two H1's, one with an image of numbers on a plane 5 inches from the H1, and one with an image of letters 6 inches from the H1. You now set up the H1/H2 system by placing the H1 plateholder, say, 4 inches from an H2 plateholder. You place the "number H1" into the H1 plateholder, and shoot the H2, but you don't process the H2 - the H2 has what's called a 'latent image' - there's an image recorded but you can't see anything. Now you replace "number H1" with the "letters H1" and shoot the H2 again. Now you develop the H2 and what you see is a plane of numbers one inch behind the H2, and a plane of letters two inches behind the H2. Two planes, one of numbers, and one of letters. There are complications, you have to mask the one in the back with a mask consisting of the front image, so you see each plane independantly, but the details are complex.

To get the different colours, you shoot at different ref angles with the same laser, using ref angles such that it will reconstruct at the designed colour for the specific angle of the white light recon. In the above example of layers of letters and numbers, suppose you designed it to be reconstructed with white light at 45 deg. You also want the letters layer to be in green, and the numbers layer to be in red when you illuminate the hologram in white light at 45 deg. If you use the 514nm laser line, and you referenced the letters layer, ie the H2 with the letters H1 in place, at 45 deg, then, if you illuminated with white light at 45 deg, the letters layer will show up in green - the illumination angle with white light is the same as the ref angle for H2, and the image will be green because of the green component of white light. Now, you want the numbers layer to show up in red at the same white light illumination angle of 45. You now have to calculate the ref angle with the green (514nm) laser so that the red in the white light at 45 deg will diffract to give a red image. You do this using the grating equation λ = d* sin(θ). First you calculate the grating spacing - d - that will diffract red (say 650nm) at 45 deg:

d = λ/sin(θ) = 650/sin(45) ~ 919 nm.

Now, you calculate the ref angle at 514 to give you d = 919 nm:

sin(θ) = λ/d = 514/919 = 0.559 => θ = 33 deg.

So, when you put the letters layer H1 in place, reference the H2 at 45 deg; Next, you put the numbers layer H1 in place and reference the H2 at 33 deg. Now, in the final hologram, white light illumination at 45 will show the letters layer in green and the numbers layer in red.

> JayFritoeswrote:↑Sat Sep 06, 2025 8:43 pm: Dinwrote:↑Sat Sep 06, 2025 2:49 pmThere's no cross talk with white light illumination. Cross talk has nothing to do with 2D/3D. Cross talk usually occurs with full colour/true colour holography. If you shoot an H1 of, let's say, a porcelain cat with a HeNe (red) and the same cat with an argon (green), at different angles, you get two sets of fringes: cat shot with HeNe and cat shot with argon. If you now reconstructed the H1 with both the HeNe and the argon, you should, ideally, see two images, one red and one green. But, you'll see four images because the HeNe laser will also diffract from the "green fringes" and the argon will diffract from the "red fringes". You'll end up seeing two red images and two green images. There is "cross talk" of the green laser diffracting off the "red fringes" etc.

Petr, the problem is that simply describing some visual effect, like "twinkling" (or even showing a image/video of it), without an actual definition, or even description makes it impossible to try and determine what caused that effect. The word "twinkling" is a metaphor that can assume a large number of possible effects; maybe the card is crumpled, or there's a crease over the hologram part, maybe the card is being twisted in such a way that the recon transfers from one source of light to another, nearby source of light, possibly "twinkling" doesn't actually mean that the image intensity is changing at all and that the hologram is viewed between two slits in the rainbow from two (or more) different source directions, maybe it's an effect of some aberration such as astigmatism, where one astigmatic image is changing to another astigmatic image. I can think of hundreds of possible effects where the hologram can "twinkle", whatever that means.

Most questions such as these, I think, fail to consider the core holographic effect is due to both the holographic image and the recon light. Hans used to bring this point up on several occasions when asked questions like this. You have got to refer to the recon, otherwise, what the image is doing, and why, is a meaningless question. Holography, as Hans has said several times, is a two-stage effect, recording and reconstruction. You can't understand one without the other.

A hologram is not some kind of 'dynamic photograph".

> JayFritoeswrote:↑Mon Sep 08, 2025 1:48 pm: Actually, I just read that rainbow holograms don’t really shift laterally because of altering the reconstruction light. So maybe the stars shifting is parallax?

That's not entirely correct. A rainbow hologram is basically the same H2 made from H1 that I have explained earlier. The only difference is that for rainbow hologram, H1 height is considerably smaller that its width. If you are looking through a H1 with your eyes in just behind it and move your head left or right, you indeed perceive parallax of the reconstructed object. However, if you move your head up or down even slightly, you don't see anything as your eyes are not behind H1 anymore (imagine H1 height is e.g. 1 mm). The same applies whan looking at H2 illuminated by a laser: there is left-right parallax, but no up-down parallax (the image disappears). If you illuminate H2 by white light and your eyes are at a position of H1, you see the reconstructed object in one colour. If you move your hed up or down, the image will not disappear, it will just change its colour.

That change of colour is a consequence of another fact I have not touched yet. If you are making just H1 (for simplicity) and want a perfect reconstruction, you must use reconstruction light of the same quality as the reference light - direction, divergence, wavelength, everything. If you, e.g., change the direction of the reconstruction light, the effect somehow resembles changing observer position. Changing wavelength may, in some circumstances, also resemble changing observer position.

So, when a rainbow hologram is illuminated by white light, it creates many reconstructions in all spectral colours. But each of them is visible just when your eyes are behind a respective H1 frame.

> JayFritoeswrote:↑Sat Sep 20, 2025 10:42 am: I read online that rainbow holograms aren’t as strongly Bragg selective as single-wavelength laser holograms, which means that the reconstruction light angle does not have to match the original reference beam perfectly to get the right image. That's a lot of various subjects. The sentence was definitely used in some context and has little meaning outside of it.

A common thin hologram works well with monochromatic reconstruction light. It is poor with polychromatic reconstruction light when displaying deep 3D objects.

A rainbow hologram is a trick how to overcome poor reconstruction of a thin hologram with polychromatic light using narrow H1 and full size H2.

This is a theoretical concept. It does not require any Bragg selectivity. However, in practice, you often prepare H1 and H2 in a way that has some Bragg selectivity, for instance using "thick" silver halide emulsion.

Bragg selectivity is vital for thick holograms, for instance Denisyuk (reflection) holograms. You can make a thick hologram with weak or strong Bragg selectivity, it depends on application.

It is thus difficult to say whether Bragg selectivity of a rainbow hologram is stronger than of a Denisyuk hologram. Sometimes yes, sometimes no. The only important thing is that Bragg selectivity is not a requirement for rainbows, but it is for Denisyuk.

"Single-wavelength laser hologram" is a too vague term, especially when you are interested in details.

"Reconstruction light angle does not have to match the original reference beam perfectly to get the right image" is also vague. Once the reconstruction beam is not the expected one, you get either aberrations or - in case of strong Bragg selectivity - no reconstruction at all.
