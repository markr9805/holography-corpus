Yes, that's true. It's known as dispersion and it's a serious problem in full colour transmission holography. You get ten images: the "true" coloured image and three dispersed "ghosts" on either side. I actually don't think he recorded in different colours, at least, not in the usual way. I think that he generated "coloured" photons using the plasmon effect. These photons then reconstructed the resist hologram in close contact with the plasmon surface. Because the photons were so close to the surface the reconstructed hologram then interacted with the same photons to create an "H2".

Dinesh Sun Apr 17, 2011 9:02 am #13
> PinkysBrain: Hmm, with a digitally created hologram using the method Zebra/Rabbithole/Ultimate/etc use it might then be possible to record everything with one wavelength. You could simply correct digitally for the change in diffraction angles during replay, recording with whatever colour laser is easiest. You'd have to record the hologram with reference beams from the three different directions one after the other in this case obviously, can't record the fringes for all the colours in one shot.

Yes, sort of. There are a number of digital methods. One method records, or images, the fringe structure that would have occurred had you recorded with several different wavelengths. You don't actually use any reference beams, simply a write beam, or an electron beam, to write the fringes. You caIculate the fringe structure that would have been recorded and use lenses to image the structure or an ebeam to write the structure. I believe that Zebra uses this method. Essentially, you break up the entire frame into tiny sub-areas and then record the view seen at that sub-area, then you repeat for the entire frame. You can also do an rgb colour separation of an actual image from a slide/frame/ photograph/video frame and then sequentially show the r component on, say, an lcd and record with the r wavelength (say, HeNe), then you show the g component and record with the g component etc. You can do this all at once over the entire plate or, again, divide the plate into sub-areas.

> BobH: Leith & Upatnieks proposed a three color scheme very early on in the '60s using three wavelengths to make a transmission full color image, with the three colors spatially separated to prevent crosstalk.

Yes, but they never actually did it, for lack of actual lasers. We independently came across the same solution for overcoming crosstalk, but we had to spend a little time to determine the best geometry for a given plate size. Separating the three beam directions won't actually get rid of the cross talk in any commercially available emulsion; it just widens the distance between the crosstalk images. If the emulsion were thick enough then Bragg selectivity would entirely get rid of crosstalk over small angles. We found that recording the transmission H1 with 633 (HeNe), 514 (Argon) and 457 (Melles Griot solid state - the one with two beams coming out the end) and separating the beams by about 25 degrees in the same plane would cause the crosstalk images to be far enough apart that we could then do an image plane H2 on a 4x5 plate (with the same lasers) without any crosstalk images showing on the H2. The crosstalk images ju-u-u-st moved off the edge of the 4x5. Of course, separating the beams by any greater angle would allow the recording of larger H2's. One problem we did find, by the by, was that the various lasers had different beam divergences. This meant that when we flipped the H1, we had to invert the beam divergences to get all three components lined up. Since we hadn't thought of that, the three images were displaced in all three directions by small amounts. That is, because of the mismatch between the beam divergences of the red, green and blue lasers and because of the differing directions of the three beams, the red, green and blue images did not quite superimpose to give an overall coloured image. It looked a bit like those coloured shadows you see in some science museums where you stand against a wall and are illuminated by red, green and blue lights. So, now I'm working on a new method to overcome crosstalk.

Also, by the way, we did dig up a scheme to record and illuminate colour holograms in a paper by Rudy, where the three beams came in from three different planes as well as three different directions. Effectively, he had a pyramid geometry with the plate at, say, the apex of the pyramid and the three beams coming in from the corners of the base of the pyramid. We had all three beams in the same plane. I emailed Rudy about this and I got the reply that it was a thought experiment - he had never actually done this. I mentioned that we had actually shot an H1 in three colours and "overcome" crosstalk by angularly separating the beams by about 25 degrees, but in the same plane. He was interested to see this and was actually planning a trip to San Diego to help do some H1/H2 colour shots. Sadly, he died before we could arrange anything. It was Rudy who pointed out that L & U had also thought of the same method. I'd always scanned the U & P paper and never noticed the last few paragraphs where they talk of the multiplexing technique.

NB: I said that the colour component shift was known as dispersion and Bob corrected me. It's known as crosstalk.

"Wavefront Reconstruction with Diffused Illumination and Three-Dimensional Objects"

which appeared in JOSA, November 1964.

There are no figures associated with colour recording and reproduction. The passage I put up is the last section in the paper and almost as if it was an afterthought, a theoretical extension of the technique in the main part of the paper. All the figures relate to geometries for a single off-axis beam, a optical processing diagram, some figures concerning Fraunhofer diffraction, a reconstruction of some transparencies and the famous train.

> BobH: Also, they did mention the volume requirements of Lippman recordings, so I'd imagine they would get the three reference beams as separated as possible

Yes, they do. However, the Lippmann technique is a straightforward interference filter. It's not coupled wave. In an interference filter, both beams have to be symmetric about an optical axis. However, clearly, their technique was not symmetric about an axis; this was their big breakthrough. So, if you assume that the Lippmann reference was a solution to crosstalk, then you have this odd juxtaposition of relying on a coupled wave formalism using an interference technique. As I mentioned, the coupled wave formalism was 5 years in the future. So, was their use of the term "Lippmann" due to the fact that the emulsions they were working with were simply not fine enough to take 3 sets of high density fringes and if the emulsion were thicker, then they could actually accomplish colour? So, it wasn't so much a case of crosstalk but more of a case of spatial frequency range? A thicker medium would have been able to record fringes in the z direction, allowing them to get "more fringes", as it were, rather than solving a crosstalk problem. I grant that they were aware of the crosstalk problem, but I wonder if the mention of Lippmann was not a solution to this, but more storage room. Of course, this is all speculation and interpretation, but I wonder if they did have the glimmerings of the coupled wave formalism 5 years before Kogelnik.

> BobH: It was all rails then... why would they confine their thinking to a plane?

Because rails are planar. All the rails had to be on the same plane as their optical table. Thus, "periphery" may mean three rails set around the periphery of the table and all "pointing" to the plate at, say, the centre of the table, but all in the plane of the table, as opposed to above the plane of the table (or below). In order to have a beam coming in from a higher plane, they'd have to have a rail coming in from above at an angle (actually, two angles) - much like an off-axis, roller coaster geometry. The "high" end of the rail would then have to be supported by some tall structure (remember Newport didn't exist either back then!) and also be fairly rigid. Then you'd have to have a number of supports all along the length of the rail to stop it from sagging. I guess I'm applying a kind of "Occam's razor" argument here, but I don't see them thinking of such a complicated real geometry

> BobH: In the very early '60s, they were using Kodak technical pan film first and then Kodak 649F. Those are both volume recording materials. I know that a transmission hologram on 649F will certainly show Bragg selectivity, and think tech pan will too. The material could certainly take volume recordings. My point was that the basic theory for transmission holograms in those days was Fresnel zone plates. They knew that the actual structures were a series of hyperbolic curves, so the zone plates were a heuristic device for analysis of parameters. But, zone plates are flat, they have no depth. So all the analysis was dependent on exposure on a flat area. Effectively, the actual data only existed on the surface of the plate; the depth of the plate was immaterial. For a reflection hologram, the data existed throughout the medium. So, they could use the same plate, but the depth of the plate was not characterised in a transmission hologram, they only concerned themselves with the variations across the surface of the plate.

This is not true for resist and thermoplastic. In these media, the data is represented by a variation of height over the surface of the medium, rather than a variation of some physical character of the surface (darkness, for example). However, the variation of height was assumed to be small enough that the height was used as a physical parameter on the surface.

> Ed Wesly: You don't necessarily need Kogelnik to predict if you are going to get crosstalk or extra orders, just the simple grating equation, which was around in the early '60's.

You're right, the grating equation will give you the cross talk positions. It's the elimination of them that you need Kogelnik for. All the source material I have (the books I mentioned) were all written in the early 70's and they all say words to the effect: "You can make colour holograms with three lasers. This was done by <X, Y, Z>. If you do this, you will get spurious images ("crosstalk"). You can overcome the spurious images if you make the film thick enough. In order to know how thick to make them, use the Kogelnik coupled wave equations to calculate the dephasing parameter" So, they say, colour was described (if not actually shot) in the mid-60's; colourwould have created crosstalk; Cross talk can be eliminated by a thick enough emulsion. How thick? Use Kogelnik! But, the workers in the mid-60's couldn't have used Kogelnik. So, are the books, written in the 70's, saying that this is how you solve a problemnow that, back then, they would have had? That was to me the curiousity.

Another interesting, but quirky, point is that Kogelnik assumes a perfectly sinusoidal set of Bragg planes. All the calculations are based on a perfectly sinusoidal set of planes. But, if you record a perfectly sinusoidal set of Bragg planes, you have no bandwidth and It's the bandwidth that determines colour! So you have the additional oddity that you're using a set of equations to eliminate colour problems when, by their very nature, these equations will not give you colour.

Well, it's all a little curious.

Chapter 17.6 in Collier and Lin is entitled "Multicolor Images from Volume Holograms". From the second sentence in the chapter: "False reconstructions are eliminated without having to incur penalties in resolution, contrast, or spatial frequency bandwidth. No special techniques in formation or reconstruction are necessary. What is required is a recording medium of sufficient thickness."

The chapter then goes on to calculate the "sufficient thickbness" by specifically alluding to the Kogelnik equations in chapter 9 of the book. I stress that they use a theory that was only published in 1969 to calculate "sufficient thickness". The next section (17.6.1) entitled "Transmission Holograms" then states:

" The absence of cross modulation in the multicolor images generated by volume holograms (presumably the holograms of "sufficient thickness" as calculated by Kogelnik) was first demonstrated with a transmission hologram recorded in Kodak 649F photographic emulsion as in Fig 17.7" This is the figure I copied earlier. As you can see, all the beams, both red and green, are inline, which would have caused a lot of crosstalk unless the film was of "sufficient thickness"; certainly the angular separation of the L & U paper is not being used - the beams are inline. Also, do I understand you and Ed saying that 649F wasn't available in the early 60's? At any rate, this first colour hologram is shown in plate V of the book and a reference is made to:

K. S. Pennington and L. H. Lin, "Multicolor Wavefront Reconstruction," Appl. Phys. Lett. 7, 56 (1965)

Now, how on earth can a paper written in 1965 have possibly used Kogelnik to determine an emulsion of "sufficient thickness"? In 1965, the equations used to determine this thickness didn't exist!

The picture shown in plate V, the picture of the hologram, seems to have no cross talk. However, if they did it according to the diagram, the plate is tilted at 45 degrees to the viewer. The fact that the plate is tilted and that the object is a transparency may be reasons why we don't see crosstalk. In those early years all the colour transmissions were all of transparencies. They could quite easily have done models, but chose to do transparencies. It's tempting to speculate that they tried models but couldn't get over the crosstalk on a model, but transparencies may have been more forgiving because a transparency is a very, very large number of point images.
