Tom B. Tue Feb 27, 2007 7:17 am #4
I would argue that the minute and hour hands also move very slightly each second and would come out just as faint or possibly even fainter than the second hand. Each slight movement would effectively start a new exposure, but because the movement was small (less than a hand width), the fringes created would compete with those from the previous exposure, reducing the visibility of the hands so they would come out as black, assuming they were originally shiny or light colored.

Rather than speculate further, it would be easy enough to try with an analog wristwatch and a small plate.

ErichRose Tue Feb 27, 2007 2:29 pm #6
You guys ever look at a running watch with the works exposed? It's humming along at a good clip. That spring is cranking and the gears are moving along pretty fast. It's only the hands at the end of the gear trains that are moving slowly. I don't see how any sort of image will be recorded since the entire watch is going to be in movement. We need things to be dead still. Close only counts in horseshoes and handgrenades.

It's a really neat idea and, if you have the time and extra plates to spare, an interesting experiment. But I would argue its a project for pulsed holography. Just don't want to see someone waste $'s on an impossible project.

I also want to say that if I am proven wrong it would be pretty cool.

BobH Tue Feb 27, 2007 4:26 pm #8
Steve Provence made an embossed achromatic hologram of a watch he called "Active Watch" around 1993. I have copies of it in my collection. As you pitch the forward and back, the hands move to different positions. The watch was not running durring the exposures, and he simply set the hands to his intended positions for each exposure. A very nice animated embossed image, appearing to be silver and white. There is a color artefact caused by his combination of vertical animation with the "open-aperture" achromatic recording technique, but they probably only bother other holographers and look like psychadelic trails. 8)

I think you can see it pictured on the Holobank web site.

ErichRose Tue Feb 27, 2007 10:26 pm #12
From "How things work" in part:

The electronics of the watch initially amplifies noise at the crystal frequency. This builds or regenerates into oscillation -- it starts the crystal ringing. The output of the watch crystal oscillator is then converted to pulses suitable for the digital circuits. These divide the crystal's frequency down and then translate it into the proper format for the display. (See How Digital Clocks Work for a detailed discussion of dividers and display drivers.) Or, in a quartz watch with hands, the dividers create one-second pulses that drive a tiny electric motor, and this motor is connected to standard gears to drive the hands.

Very interesting that DCGMan made one that worked, only blurring the second hand. And five seconds is a long time as movement goes. I bet it was a better quality watch with well built, smooth running parts. Cool.
