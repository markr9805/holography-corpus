Colin Kaminski Thu Feb 22, 2007 3:46 pm #2
There is a messy solution, however it is cheap. Get a piece of gray gel from a theater or anything that can attenuate the light. Like a partially silvered mirror any smoked glass be creative. Place this in the beam on the reference half.

Here is the messy part, the edge of the attenuator will cause all sorts of beam quality problems down stream, so you need to make sure these go in between the plate and the object.

I had a little attenuator I had used made from a Neutral Density Gel from a local theater glued to a piece of thin glass salvaged from a picture frame. If I had to make another one I think I would ditch the glass and try to make a "stretcher" for the gel as the edge effect was always a problem.

A red gel would work as well, I just had my choice and ND gels are more intuitive for 4th graders to understand. If you give it a try please post your results as I am working on a section for the wiki on single beam work. (Photos always take me for ever )

I have one flat front-surfaced mirror (4x6 inches) but I really wanted to use just a single beam.

Here's what I did.

I used a photometer that could measure relative intensities and I calibrated the meter on the reference beam. I covered the object beam and placed the meter probe behind the plate (used plate) and set the photometer on 100% illuminance using ref beam. Then I covered the reference and let the meter read reflected light from the object again from behind the plate. I could not get a constant reading but at some areas of the plate, more or less the object beam is 20%-30% less intense than the reference beam. Now if I take 25% as my mean, I could have a beam ratio of roughly 4:1(ref:obj). I achieved this by optimizing the geometry of my set-up (done by many trial and error) and spray-painted my object very very white. But this ratio is not for the whole area of the plate. The illuminance of my diode laser decreases at the edges. I haven't yet tried to shoot a hologram in this set-up since I have one more doubt in my mind. Tony Wed Mar 07, 2007 1:27 pm #14
Even though you don't have a beam splitter per se, this is really a split beam transmission setup. The stability requirements are more stringent than for a single beam reflection where the object and the plate can be locked together. I suspect object motion may be an issue.

Also, even though your object appears bright, it may be affecting the polarization of the reflected light in a way that reduces brightness. Try rotating the laser to change the polarization, and/or view the setup through a polarized filter (sunglasses) to see if the polarization is being affected.

It is possible to make a hologram that is brighter than the original object. This is mentioned in Steve Benton's lectures on holography:

[http://splweb.bwh.harvard.edu:8000/cour ... _Holos.pdf](http://splweb.bwh.harvard.edu:8000/courses/mas450/reading/chaptersPDF/Ch07_Platonic_Holos.pdf)

Tom B. Thu Mar 08, 2007 4:26 am #19
Saxby in the new "Practical Holography" edition refers to setups using spatially split beams, especially where a neutral density filter is used to reduce the reference beam brightness, as "bypass" setups, a term which I recommend.

Usually objects are not nearly as bright as we would want, so reducing the reference beam brightness helps to get the reference/object beam ratio closer to the ideal value which resuts in brighter holograms at the expense of a longer required exposure time.

The ideal ratio depends on many things, but is usually supposed to be higher for transmission holograms than for reflection holos. Ranges for ideal reference:object beam ratios found in a quick check of a few handbooks range from 10:1 to 3:1 for transmission holos versus 4:1 to 2:1 for reflection holos.
