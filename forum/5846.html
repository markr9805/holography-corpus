Hans Fri Oct 20, 2006 3:17 pm #1
During the last weeks I have investigated why so often my MBDCG plates loose their blue colour while drying. According to Jeff's webpage this is because the PH of the emulsion has dropped too low. Interestingly, plates that have been dried in house stayed blue, but faded rather quickly (several hours) when moved into the garage.

I have tried the following experiments:

-- Pre-adjust the PH of the cocktail to 8.5 with NaOH before addition of the TMG and Acetic Acid.

-- Add more TMG than usual to start with a higher initial PH.

-- I have tried six different types of gelatin. Both Type A and B, and photographic gelatin.

-- I have tried to dry in a box with an ammonium drenched sponge.

-- Tried to dry the plate in a box with a wet sponge (water).

(The last two methods kept the plates blue, but the plates faded when taken out of the box to let them dry further.)

None of these methods seemed to work and keep the plates blue in my garage for longer periods.

-- Then I tried to make a PH9.2 buffer with Borax and Boric Acid. The plate stayed perfectly blue and usable this time, but as the gelatin dried, the emulsion had become saturated with Borax/BoricAcid and this caused some crystallization of these components. A slight haze had become visible in the plate that also showed after exposure (a holographic image had formed of these crystals).

-- Then I tried an emulsion with Borax, adjusted to PH9.2. Added TMG which increased the PH to 10.6. I brought the PH back down with a little Acetic Acid. No microcrystals of Borax after drying this time, but the MB faded after drying.

-- As a last experiment I tried to make stock MBDCG with Boric Acid instead of the usual Acetic Acid. The plates stay perfect blue now. No microcrystals and good sensitivity.

Here is the recipe:

---100ml DeIonized Water

---6.8ml TMG

---Add small amounts of Boric Acid crystals until the PH drops to about 10. (about 1 gram total).

---1.2ml Potassium Chromate (4%solution in DI water)

---7ml Methylene Blue (Diluted to 0.4% in DI water)

---Add 10g gelatin.

---Check PH again.

Why does this work, while all the other tests failed in my cold and damp garage? I am not sure yet, but I have a very strong suspicion which I have not been able to confirm with Jeff yet:

As the plates dry in my cold garage, the water evaporates more quickly than the Acetic Acid which causes an increase in concentration of Acetic Acid. Because the alkaline TMG also evaporates faster than the Acetic Acid, the PH drops.

So, at first glance the substitution of Boric Acid instead of Acetic Acid would seem counterintuitive because still the water and TMG evaporate out of the plate, and this time none of the acid can evaporate. One would expect the PH to drop even more quickly than when using Acetic Acid.

I believe Wikipedia gives the correct answer:

> Boric acid does not dissociate in aqueous solution, but is acidic due to its interaction with water molecules:B(OH)3 + H2O ⇌ B(OH)4− + H+Ka = 5.8x10−10 L/mol; pKa = 9.24.Polyborate anions are formed at pH 7–10 if the boron concentration is higher than about 0.025 mol/L. The best known of these is the tetraborate ion, found in the mineral borax:4B(OH)4− + 2H+ ⇌ B4O72− + 9H2O

Now in my solution, the Boric Acid concentration is about 1g/100ml. This is equivalent to about 0.016 mol/L.

Perhaps, as the water evaporates, the Boric Acid concentration becomes high enough to go through the above transformation and starts to suck up the H+ ions and thus prevents a further drop in PH. Interesting, an acid that becomes a base.

I don't know if my theory is correct. I know very little about chemistry. The bottom line is that the new formula works perfectly and worries about fading plates are a thing from the past. I have cut one plate into six smaller ones. I will expose one every two days and check on how long they will remain usable.

Yes John. During one of my experiments to make the MBDCG plates a little more stable, I tried to store freshly coated plates in the broom closet in my house instead of my cold garage. When I noticed that the plates were still perfectly blue in the morning, I could not find a box to transfer them in. So I walked with them in broad daylight back into the garage.

I believe that MBDCG exposure is a cumulative effect. If you pre-expose, the overall hardness of the plate increases (thus requiring a higher swelling temperature). If you then do the holographic exposure, the local fringes harden more (on top of the general hardening of the pre-exposure). I believe a post-exposure does the same thing.

I walk the plates into my garage in about 3 seconds. That is not nearly the same amount of exposure as a one minute post exposure under my laser. So I believe the brief walk in day-light has minimal effect on overall hardness.

Hans Sat Nov 11, 2006 9:19 am #14
> -I see that you've moved the addition of the gelatin to step #2. Is there a cooling period after the gelatin is dissolved and before the other components are added?

I use a small hot plate on wich I place a metal pan with water (water pre-heated to about 35C). I adjust the setting of the hot plate to keep the water at that temperature. I mix the solution in a small glass jar that is placed in the water pan. That way the temperature is much more stable and controllable as temperature variations are buffered by the larger volume of the water in the pan. I don't let the solution cool after dissolving the gelatin. If you get a lot of foaming though, you can let it cool and gellify first. That will reduce the foam a lot already. Alternatively you can scoop the foam out of the solution with a plastic spoon before or after it has gellified. If you use the "in a hurry" method, almost no foaming will exist.

> -You specify Potassium Chromate, and I had to go back and re-read Jeff's procedure. Now I'm wondering if I missed something along the line. Is it potassium chromate (K2CrO4) or potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7)? I have plenty of dichromate, but I don't think I have any chromate - are they interchangeable with a molarity adjustment? (Jeff?)

I use Potassium Chromate. I don't know if Dichromate will work. I think Jeff used Potassium Chromate instead of Ammonium Dichromate because the alkaline Potassium will not evaporate out of the gelatin and because of that cause a drop in PH. I tink using the Potassium Chromate instead of Potassium Dichromate has the added advantage of not having a significant dark reaction. Re-heating the solution will not cause part of the Potassium Dichromate to break down into a Cr(III) solution (which does happen about a certain temperature with Potassium Chromate). In the past many researchers used Ammonium Dichromate. But in that case it is much harder to keep the PH at the desired level. But it sure does work to some extend. An interesting note is that some researchers have added glycerin (same amount glycerin as the amount of gelatin) to a MBDCG solution that was made up with Ammonium Dichromate. They then pulsed the coated plate with what they called a "infrared annealing pulse" (a process which I understand nothing of). The plates became self developing and real-time holograms were made in red light.

> JohnFP: What does the Rhodamine 6G increase sensitivity to, green?

Blue and green.

> I seem to believe the MBDCG should already be sensitive to blue and green.

Yes, the pot. chromate is blue sensitive (but methylene blue is not) – at the same time two important drawbacks weaken the blue sensitivity:

1) the chromate concentration is quite low – compared to a "normal" DCG formula;

2) it's based not on DI-CHROMATE but on CHROMATE (which inherently is less light sensitive).

That's why it's always better to dye-sensitize (even for “blue” wavelengths) such a system.
