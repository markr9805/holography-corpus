Silver Halide does not usually yield exceedingly bright results in a Single Beam Reflection geometry. To really tune in the processing and get the maximum brightness you can achive with this geometry, take the plate and lay it on a mirror. In other words use the mirror as your object. A front surface mirror or polished piece of Al or Stainless steel will work too. You can also use a regular mirror but may get some wood grain from the reflection off the front of the glass. Also if you can, try to shoot at 56 degrees off normal (brewsters for glass).

I have (not often) processed with D-19 and KDiCr with VRP-M with addequate results but got better result with the "nastier" chemicals. Hopefully others that are more educated with that material can chime in with processing tips.

Could you tell us what safelight you're using when shooting in the green? Even if it's an LED, you might want to put some additional gel filters in front of it.

Maybe a small point, but I always start the clock before putting the plate into the developer, and get it into the bath as fast as possible. I also agitate constantly during development.

Two minutes in running water should be adequate, until you're getting results you really like. It makes processing go a little faster.

I'd leave out the mouse pad. You want rigidity on everything for a Denisyuk recording. Be certain the plate is stable with three points of contact on whatever it's resting on, and that your objects are just as stable. Forget about vibration isolation. Worry about air currents between the plate and object.

Be sure the stray light from the laser, when blocked, is not fogging the plate. Don't build a house around the laser and prevent its cooling properly, but maybe a large sheet of foamcore with a little hole in it for the beam to go through.

I am not one of the wise ones you were likely to hope to hear from, solar, but I'll stop by nonetheless....

If that hologram pictured above is a Denisyuk on VRP-M shot with a green laser - then you, my friend are ahead of the curve.

I'm posting not with processing help like you are requesting, but with a note about VRP-M and an interest in not seeing anyone else getting their @$$ kicked by this emulsion and its fickle tendencies.

Here's a quote from Slavich: "The quality will be poor as VRP-M requires high carrier modulation to achieve good diffraction efficiency. Since the Denisyuk technique is typical of small carrier modulation the brightness of the final reflection hologram will be disappointing. For Denisyuk type holograms with green laser radiation you should use PFG-03C or PFG-04."

In plain English that translates to: "Don't be surprised if your green laser Denisyuks stink out loud on VRP-M. This emulsion requires a lot of object beam, and the fraction of the beam that actually penetrates the plate is further reduced to the fraction of that that is scattered back onto the plate by the object. VRP-M likes a hot object beam and anything less will give dim results."

You're doing well to get a visible image doing SBR with VRP-M.

What can you do to get a brighter object beam?

Kicker mirrors (can be a tough request when you have your object real close to the plate)

or

Go split-beam (requires more equipment and a much higher degree of stability)

Ok, I think you are using VRP plates with a JD-2 bleach?

The JD-2 is a Dichromate bleach I think. That means that the longer you expose a hologram, the more the hologram shifts to the green. Could it perhaps be that your holograms have shifted so much into the blue causing you to not see a result? It might be there, but just not visible.

You could try to make your hologram visible by immersing the plate in a 7% solution of citric acid (Jeff's invention) and then drying. That should bring the image back towards the green.

But it's best not to use that JD-2 bleach for your VRP plates. Use Philip's re-halogenating bleach. It holds the colour.

And you really don't need the phenosafranine either! Its use was to avoid print out, and whether it helps or not is sort of debatable. (D. Vila, E. Wesly, "Controlling the effects of ultra-violet light on holographic emulsions", Proceedings of the Third International Symposium on Display Holography, Lake Forest College, 1988.)

Phenosafranine is used as a desensitizing dye in photography, where you could dip the exposed film in a solution of it and then develop negatives by inspection! I think it might have been a discovery of the Lumiere Brothers, but I am not near my library so I might be off on that. It was deemed necessary with the advent of panchromatic silver halide gelatin emulsions, as no color of light would make a good safelight.

Not too many latter 20th century photographers would even know of this technique, as most everybody works with controlling developer time and temperature. The technique's downfall was #1 the high price, #2, the nasty stains that it left behind. The stuff is not water soluble, so clean up is with some sort of alcohol. Somebody told me that this was the chemical in the so-called dye packets they place in piles of money so that one would get caught red-handed!

So you can delete the isopropyl, which is the solvent for the dye, the glycerol, which I am not so sure what it's doing there, and just use 150 g of FeNo3 and 30 g KBr. Although most publications acknowledge this as bleach to be used after fixing, I have inadvertently used it without fixing, just like any other of the rehalogenators, and it works just peachy in that mode, too!

Also, on the sulfuric question, 2.82 grams of sodium bisulfate = 1 ml sulfuric acid. Jeff calls this sodium hydrogen sulfate I believe, and I don't think you encounter any HazMat shipping charges with this powder!

BobH recalls (his memory must be better than mine!) that Phillips mentioned that the phenosafranine was used as an anti-coagulant. But what is coagulating I am not sure; maybe this was to stifle grain growth? Also since the phenosafranine is red, another reason for its inclusion was to reduce scatter in green-lit transmission holograms. His bottom line was that he continues to use the bleach I mentioned above with the appropriate amount of pheno for his transmission hologram work.

Martin mentions that the glycerol is there to prevent the FeNO3 from attacking soft gelatin. Certainly I would never accuse the Agfa gelatin of being too soft. Of course, another thing I would never accuse Agfa emulsions of being was consistent.

One time I got some Safranin-O from Photographers Formulary, as Bill Wilson, one of its founders, said that it seemed to be similar to the pheno stuff.

One issue we didn’t address was the dilution, 1 part stock to 4 parts water. So you can get a lot of bleach for fairly reasonable cost (ignoring the expensive dye). Ferric Nitrate is used in some metal patinas, so there might be an alternative venue to getting some of this stuff for cheap.

For more info on the history of these bleaches, see Ed Wesly, “A Toast to Nick Phillips” Leonardo, Vol.25, No. 5, pp.439-442, 1992. If anyone would like a .pdf of this paper, please PM c/o this forum and I will try to get it out as soon as I can!

And the question about the bisulfate; all I know is that adding 2.82 grams of sodium bisulfate (aka sodium hydrogen sulfate across the pond) (close enough to 3 grams for all practical purposes) is supposed to be the same as adding 1 mL of sulfuric acid to whatever volume of water. I got this tidbit from a book called Dignan's 150 Photographic Processing Formulas, which had been sold by Photographers' Formulary.

Ed Wesly Mon Jan 12, 2009 5:12 pm #35
Couple of typo's popping up here - it's 2.82 g of sodium bisulfate = 1 mL sulfuric acid. And only 10 mL of acid in the bleach.

Most high contract/high energy developers work fine with holographic films; the Kodak series of D-8, D-11, D-19, and even Kodalith developer. Their basic photo film developer, D-76, (= Ilford ID-11) is too low of a contrast, plus it has a lot of sulfite in it which would also lower efficincy by removing some silver halides.

Surprisingly Kodak's mainstay paper developer (Dektol) works quite well with holographic products! I've never seen its published formula, unless it is exactly D-72, but both Dektol and D-72 give identical results when used with photo paper.

So if you find a copy of the D-72 formula and compare it to D-19, they have the same ingredients, and to make the working solution of Dektol/D-72 have the same amount of developing agents, metol (Kodak Elon, they do it just to confuse everyone into buying their chemistry) and hydroquinone, you would dilute the paper developer two parts stock solution and one part of water, as opposed to the photo darkroom dilution of one part developer to two parts water.

It's curious that Kodak is still (as far as I know at this writing) producing photo chemistry for B & W papers although they don't manufacture them! We shall see how long that lasts! But there might be a possibility that some other B & W paper developer with a stronger than photo paper dilution might work just as well!

...just that:

The run-of-the-mill JD-2 potassium dichromate bleach does the job, it just shrinks the emulsion some and leads to a blue shift.

That's like getting a pre-swell for free. Your pseudo-color options are opened way up by introducing the possibility of blue in your replay. A little fiddling with swelling agents after development opens up darn near the whole visible spectrum to you.

In fact, I made, a while back, an H2 on VRP-M that replayed an amazing dark blue that was an authentic cobalt under halogen light. I decided to give it no post-swell at all because the color was so gorgeous. (no, not the one in my avatar - the turquoise one in my picture is a second example of the colors available after processing with plain ol' JD-2 - it started off near-turquoise and I just tweaked it there with some table sugar after processing)

However - As we've noted, emulsion shrinkage leads to alterations in the fringe spacing, and therefore may not be entirely appropriate for transmission holograms. Maybe you could try the fancy bleaches for your transmission work,

but then I maintain that you may have some fun experimenting with the pot. dichr. bleach for your reflection grams. A simple post-swell following a bleach that shrinks allows a green laserist working on VRP-M to tune from blue all the way through the longer wavelengthswithout the hassle of pre-swelling your film. Slavich recommends Sorbitol. Maybe in your chemical shoppings you can find some? Other things work. I have had success with both table sugar and clover honey.

Just a suggestion... Beautiful colors available.
