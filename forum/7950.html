Joe Farina Thu Aug 27, 2009 5:35 pm #7
Thanks, John. Yes, the curves are from the 5th Edition of The Artist's Handbook of Materials and Techniques by Ralph Mayer. Should be the same book you have, as I recall.

DJ, I only got those two beams together to a minimal degree so far, with no spatial filter. I use a "telescope" in front of the 315 to vary beam diameter and divergence. It takes 3 lenses to do this, 2 will not suffice. Ed Wesly told me about the three-element telescope, called a "Donder Zoom Telescope" which in my case is simply 2 positive lenses with a negative lens in between. (I think you could reverse this and have 2 negative lenses with a positive lens in between, and it might do the same thing.) Definitely works OK. (The lenses need to be shifted around to get different diameters and divergences. I use a Thorlabs rail and rail carriers, and mount each lens on a separate post with lens holder, so that I can easily slide them back and forth.) If there are only two lenses, you can vary the beam diameter, but not the divergence. I confirmed this in my water tank experiments.

I intend to match the 532 to the 633 beam (the HeNe is my "standard," or "laser to be matched to" considering its superior beam characteristics (as opposed to a 315 or a Melles Blue). At the moment, I am having problems with my spatial filter and need two larger pinholes: I want to try 15 and 20 micron diameters.

DJ, one thing to keep in mind when combining beams is the point in space where they need to be spread out for the reference beam. This is a specific point in space, say for example 4 feet after the beam combiner (in my case a dichroic mirror). This is where the spreading lens or spatial filter will be located. At that particular point, the diameter and divergence both need to be changed, to match the other beam. If there were only two lenses, both the diameter and divergence could be changed, but not at that particular point in space. So that's why a zoom system with three lenses is needed.
