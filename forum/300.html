I was going to comment too, but didn't want to pile it on. Chemicals in former food bottles is a very common practice and really not a good idea. The only chemical spill I've experienced while working with holography was when someone with a PhD in holography (that's right) put waste resist developer into an empty 5 gallon water jug. A week or so later, developer all over the floor. Good thing the floor had a big slope near the wall and it stayed pretty much along the edge. On other occasions, I've seen chemicals stored in the refrigerator in old food bottles and tupperware trays.

I've lived with holography for 30 years. Holography in my apartment, holography in my house, and living in the holography studio/lab. Jeffrey Weil Fri Aug 19, 2011 7:38 pm #8
Hello Holomark,

Glad your not upset.

You need some plastic darkroom jugs like these, you should be able to get them at any photo store that sells chemistry. They love stuff like this, it can sit on the shelf forever. If they sell any chem at all, they will have jugs.

[http://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/7 ... ottle.html](http://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/70862-REG/Delta_11140_Datatainer_Chemical_Storage_Bottle.html)

they come in smaller sizes too

[http://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/7 ... _with.html](http://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/70860-REG/Delta_11120_Datatainer_Storage_Bottle_with.html)

And then there is the accordion bottle. With one of these you can squeeze all of the air out of the bottle. I don't like these types. My feeling is if your mixing up this much chemistry, and have to worry about aging, your mixing up too much.

I always mix up stuff fresh with a scale and a magnetic mixer, only what I need. I feel it's worth it but plenty of great holograms have been made with stock solutions.

[http://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/3 ... ottle.html](http://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/318712-REG/Kaiser_204198_Accordion_Storage_Bottle.html)

The next thing you should do is read up on basic lab safety. On the net that must be pretty easy to find.

You might want to look into getting a separate storage cabinet for your holography chemistry. Keep it separate from everything else and pay attention to what you store next to each other. Anything that might react violently or generate heat should be kept apart in your storage area.

No matter how well you set it up something can always happen. Get MSDS sheets for all your chemistry and keep them clearly visible to anyone that comes into the room. Hung on a wall on a clip board for example. The sheets for the non-dangerous stuff are just as important as the dangerous. If something happens how will anyone know it's one of the safer compounds?

For yourself there are a few things you might need. Fume hood, respirator, eye protection, proper gloves, etc...it all depends on what your using.

When you handle full strength acids wear shoes. I almost never wear shoes in my lab when I'm working.....except for when I'm moving a bottle of acid. If that falls and your not wearing shoes you'll fall into the puddle...not good. The stuff you'll find on lab safety on the net probably won't cover home lab situations like not wearing shoes. Those basic lab instructions are for school and industrial environments. It wouldn't occur to the writers that their work would be applied to a home holographer situation. So you'll have to expand on that information a bit and use some common sense.

If you have a "normal" household sink that has a downward facing faucet you might want to get an eye wash station. This is a little bottle of salty water that sits on the wall waiting you when you get something splashed into your eye. It would be very hard to reach the valves and hold your head upside down to wash out your eye in a kitchen sink. With one of these you put your eye into a cup on the top of the bottle and squeeze the bottle. The water rushes up and gets the stuff out of your eye.

[http://www.uline.com/Product/Detail/H-2 ... z-capacity](http://www.uline.com/Product/Detail/H-2387/Eye-Wash-Station-Supplies/Single-Bottle-Eye-Wash-Station-16-oz-capacity)

I never had to use one but did install them in most the labs I've worked at. If you have a darkroom hose instead of a faucet and easily accessible valves you might not need one. You'll have to decide based on your lab setup.

Maybe now that were back on track everyone else can post their solutions to your solution problem.

> Jeffrey Weil: ou might want to look into getting a separate storage cabinet for your holography chemistry.

It helps if the storage cabinet is metal and fireproof. It may not matter with a home lab, but if you have a large number of chemicals and a fire, the chemicals will mix and exacerbate the fire. Fire is a real hazard and I am fairly careful to avoid fires and to limit the effects of a fire. I have two fire extinguishers at opposite ends of the lab and I have a service to check them once a year.
