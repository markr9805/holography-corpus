[quote="JohnFP"]semb, I am not sure of the difference you point out.

Simon is refering to the later addition to that paper after your contribution John. This is to use a little bit of dichromate as hardener instead of the alum. This method I am using right now on experiments with the Ag diffusion system because it does not undermine the silane pre-subbing

that occurs when one uses aldehyde hardeners and also gives you the luxury of hardening without hurrying. As explained in this extract in our paper.:-

Gelatin formulation

In our system we used a high bloom strength gelatin ,[ 260 bloom “limed ossein” from Croda Colloids].

0.3g glycerol was first added to a glass beaker followed by 100 ml de-ionized water and a magnetic follower .

12g. of gelatin granules were then vigorously stirred in to the cold solution on a magnetic stirrer hotplate.

The solution in the beaker was then heated in a non-magnetic metal water bath and the temperature of the solution was monitored and kept to within 50 to 60 oC for about 40 minutes then finally 1.0 ml of a 5% potassium dichromate solution was stirred in.(This 5% dichromate solution will be used later to photosensitize the coating). It was then filtered through nylon mesh into a fresh beaker which was then kept at about 60 oC. while the glass sheet was warmed with a hot hairdrier.

The Meyer bar, ~ 7 windings per cm., length 30 cm , diam.1 cm., warmed in hot water, dried and placed at the far end of the sheet and a puddle of the gelatin solution was made on the glass across the width and extending nearly half way down the sheet . The Meyer bar was then drawn down the sheet at a steady constant rate. ( The draw down took about 15 seconds).

Notes:The problem of coating gelatin solutions with hardeners is that they rapidly increase the viscosity of the solution and commonly cause gelling before the coating operation is completed. The approach which appealed to us in the latter stage of this project was to return to one of the original ideas from the early work at Bell Telephone Labs(5 ) . Namely this was to include in the gelatin solution a very low concentration of a “potential hardener “ which could be activated later. The simplest practical answer was indeed to use ammonium or potassium dichromate. So the gelatin solution is prepared but then finally, just 0.5% of dichromate (based on the weight of dry gelatin) is stirred in. Either the ammonium or potassium salt is OK here. It should be emphasized that this initial use of a very small quantity of dichromate should be viewed quite differently from the necessarily high concentration of dichromate needed to make the holograms later. Our use of glycerol at this point acts as both a plasticizer and catalyses thermal hardening if required at this point. The coating is then carried out but to ease the clean up operation later it is best done under subdued lighting. The great advantage of not coating the gelatin down with all the high concentration of dichromate present for full photo-sensitization is that then the troublesome limited lifetime of unexposed sheets with their constantly increasing hardness from a dark reaction is avoided. Also the coating does not need to be done under red safelighting. Sheets can be stored indefinitely without refrigeration and used to make consistent freshly sensitized plates as needed.

Once dried in cool air-flow the coating can either be lightly hardened by leaving it in direct sunshine for at least 20 minutes or left in ambient daylight for a day or two. Alternatively because the formulation contains glycerol, the gelatin can be hardened in an oven at around 100 oC for about 20 minutes (which is more convenient than 2 hrs at 150 oC. recommended in the original 1969 paper( 5 ).) The glass sheet is then cut up into convenient sized plates.

Jeff

Why does the sensitizing solution need to be chilled?
jeff-blyth Mon Feb 05, 2007 5:12 pm #26
> DJ Mathson: Why does the sensitizing solution need to be chilled?

Because the completely unhardened gelatin is inclined to swell up too much in water at room temperature and detach from the glass.

It takes nearly a minute for the alum to harden it enough to stop this happening, therefore it needs to be well below room temperature while the alum gets to work.

When I first started playing with this technique/forumula, I too did not have a plate for the oven or a cooling plate so here is what I did.

In the oven I used stack of 3 clean glass plates larger then the hologram plate.

For cooling I took the plate out and pressed it against the side of the refrigerator for a few seconds then slid it over to a new spot on the fridge and repeated this every few seconds until the plate was cool.

I have very good results but then upgraded to two pieced of steel.

Knox unflavored gelatine works extreemly well and the advantage is, because it is food grade it is well monitored. I am really going to have to say it looks like your emulsion is washing away, especially from the run marks.

Ok, so you are using a higher plate and coating temperature that I use. I use 115 - 120F. I see that I did not mention how to make the gelatin on the wiki in the G307 forumula but in "A Beginner approach to DCG" on the wiki I mention a 100:12 (water:emulson) ratio and a cooking and coating temperature of 120F. Sometimes I even go to 100:14 ratio with 120 cook/coat for veil coating. Now if you are using a lower ratio or higher temperature or both you will get a much thinner coating which my be why it is washing away. What ratio water to gelatin are you using?

IF you see streak marks after about 5 minutes after coating, don't worry, you can hold the gelatin coated plate horizontal with the umulsion upward and gently heat the underside (glass side) of the plate with a blow dryer and the gelatin with liquify and gravity will smooth it out.

There is a difference between milkiness and crystallization.

The first picture is surely milky and seems to indicate a lack of hardening and/or water and/or alcohol temperatures too high. The basic idea is if the emulsion is hardened more (longer exposure, chemically or thermally) it can withstand higher temperature processing temperatures. You could try to keep water and alcohols cooler or harden the emulsion more (by baking longer). This might even be cause by the gelatin coating issue I suggested above.

In the second image, it looks like the emulsion was not dried enough with the forced hot air. Even if the emulsion slight washed away you should be able to get rid of the crystalization. I see this all the time and if you take your heated air dryer and keep drying it (if it is a hair dryer, put it on high and keep it only a couple of mm of the plate at the center and slowly work outwards in a circle) the crystalization should disappear. That picture looks like you stopped the air drying too soon. Or you could take the hologram and put it back in the over for 15 to 30 minutes.
