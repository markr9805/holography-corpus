Tony Sun Mar 04, 2007 7:06 pm #2
The end goal of adjusting exposures and light ratios is to maximize brightness of the resultant hologram. To do this, we need to make the fringes have the highest contrast possible, and adjust the exposure so they use as much of the film's dynamic range as possible.

To get the greatest contrast, the light from object and reference would need to be equal and have aligned polarization. Most objects are not mirrors (unless we're making an HOE), so they do not reflect all the object light to the film; and they cause the light to become more randomly polarized.

We use a variable beam splitter to adjust the ratios of light hitting the object and film so that after reflection from the object, we can have the desired ratio.

I'm not sure where the guideline ratio of 4:1 for transmision holograms comes from, but I imagine that it is decreased from 1:1 to some extent to account for the fact that light measurements are averaged over areas, and that allows for some peak intensities, such as specular glints, to remain within the dynamic range of the film.

Adjusting spread lengths might in some cases fly in the face of another holographic constraint, that of keeping the path lengths of reference and object beam matched, within the coherence length of the laser. Of course, if your laser has a long CL (i.e. 315M) tehn that matters a lot less.
BobH Sun Mar 04, 2007 7:21 pm #3
The rules change when using a diffuser in the object beam because interference also happens between different points on the object (intermodulation noise). The reference beam is made stronger so that the fringes made by it and all the points on the object overcomes the fringes that cause intermodulation noise.

It doens't matter how or where you split the beams, or what the losses in the individual beams are. The only ratio that counts is that at the recording plane (unless there's thermal effects in one beam because of all the light going in that direction). Although most efficient interference occurs with a beam ratio of one (as in the interferometer), most efficient recording of that interference may be at a higher ratio (ref/obj) to avoid overmodulation of the recording material. Overmodulation results in higher order gratings being recorded which deplete the modulation capability available for the primary grating, making it dimmer.

Geezertron Mon Mar 05, 2007 8:05 pm #12
Great questions. Great points.

Food for thought:

Photons do not interfere with eachother -They interfere with themselves. One of the most important features of interference is that even if only one photon (or electron for that matter) at a time is passed through an interferometer, the absorptions at the detector will add up to an interference pattern. And yes, I agree it is more intuitive to think of the beams as wavefronts, but you have to recognize that the "wave" (an abstraction representing the probabilities of a single photon being absorbed at any particular point in it possible path)is one wave when it interferes at the plate. That is, the object and reference beams are a single wavefront that is overlapping onto itself (trillions of em actually, all conveniently in step in the case of a laser). The fact that you use a beamsplitter to split the wave rather than just using a flat mirror to reflect part of an expanding beam onto itself (the simplest interferometer) doesn't change this.

So every single "particle" that is absorbed, and contributes to an interference fringe, was actually a single wave that was split in two (or more), that effectively took both the reference and the object paths, and interfered with itself at the plate. What blows my mind the most about all of this is that the object part of the wave can interfere with the reference part of the wave even though each part likely arrived at the plate at very different times.

Really high beam ratios are not that unfeasible. When I worked at FermiLab with Hans Bjelkhagen, he wrote an internal paper about the Beam Balance Ratio and concluded that even reference to object ratios of a million to one would be able to be recorded! The bubble chamber holograms we were making had ref:obj ratios in the hundreds!

When I first was introduced to making transfers into photo-resist, the conventional wisdom was a 40:1 ratio! (I think this was a Ken Harris innovation.) In a hologram of an object that has tones, the interference patterns of the dimmer parts are not as strong as those of the bright ones. Yet we never notice parts of objects missing because they were not bright enough.
