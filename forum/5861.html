Ed Wesly Sun Nov 05, 2006 2:49 pm #2
I don't have the original Siebert article, but I do have a copy of "Techniques for Pulsed Laser Holography of People" by D. A. Ansley, Applied Optics Vol.9, No.4, April 1970. The author was with the Conductron Corporation of Ann Arbor, Michigan, and the manuscript was received July 3rd 1969.

This paper has a reproduction of the Siebert hologram, and cites it as a reference: L. D. Siebert, Appl Phys. Lett. 11, 326 (1967). Might be rough finding that one!

This paper notes that the hologram was recorded on Agfa 10E75 film, and the laser output was 250 milliJoules.

Interesting dope on the "Conductron laser". They show a photograph of their model C-10 ultra-coherent pulse laser, with a schematic. They used a dye Q-switch for 50 nanosecond pulses and to control the longitudinal modes, although mentioning the use of etalons in some instances. A mirror after the oscillator but before the amplifier derived the reference beam as shown in the schematic, but I wonder if that was really practical. A table lists the spec's with the usual ruby wavelength or 694 nm, 1 Joule per pulse, with a 45 second rep rate, less than 50 nsec pulse width, greater than 2 meter coherence.

There is a big section in this article on laser safety, and drawings of experimental configurations for making holograms of humans.

It would be interesting for any of the people involved with Conductron (or Multiplex in the other thread) to chime in on these discussions. This paper notes that D. A. Ansley received his BS in 1962 from the Institute of Optics, University of Rochester, which would probably make him a retiree today!

Ed Wesly Thu Nov 20, 2008 10:50 pm #7
If you have 200 milliJoules, and you need around 50 to 200 microJoules per square centimeter for exposure, then you should be able to make holograms around 1000 cm^2 in area, which is not too small! 8" by 10" = 20 by 25 cm = 500 cm^2, so you should be able to make something almost 30 by 40 cm in a single beam reflection mode.

Pulsewidth is like 2000 times as long as a Q-switched ruby, but you still might be able to record living flesh. Maybe in one of the papers above they discuss the speed of the movement of surface of skin, but you know my motto, always give it a try. But 50 milliseconds is really long, like only 1/20" in camera jargon, so everything needs to be about as stable as for CW style holography.

If you can't record skin, you might be able to record "iffy" non-stable objects, like those made out of plastic or paper, like origami models. Good luck!
