dcgman Thu Nov 24, 2005 6:09 pm #3
Let's list the possibilities of what's happening. If others add their thoughts, we can start to narrow down the causes. Here's my thoughts

You're probably seeing the fractal growth of the gel changing from one state to another.

Question: are the regions turning 'milky' the driest or the wettest regions of the gel?

Perhaps the harder regions of the gel are drying first, 'pushing' the remaining moisture to the softer regions of the gel, which expand and start looking 'milky' until they, too, have dried completely.

To summarise: the drying technique results in an initial redistribution of moisture in the gel, which is seen as a fractal growth of the 'milky' regions. Eventually, the 'milky' regions dry.

Of course, the above reasoning could be stood on its head: perhaps the moisture is initially redistributed to the harder regions of the gel, making the harder regions appear 'milky'. Upon complete drying, the regions no longer appear 'milky' because the gel is hard in these regions and so no permanent damage has been done to the gel molecules.

Feel free to criticise.

> jean: Sometimes, the milky state occurs in the last ipa bath (100%) at room temp., I don't warm the ipa baths (too dangerous). I suspect the gelatin pass over the yield point in the very first warm water bath.

Yes, I would also have assumed the milky (opaque) appearance occurs when parts of the gelatin had been attacked by hot water, highly alkaline/acid solutions or some oxidizers.

However, there's an interesting paper by Magarinos/Coleman, Holographic mirrors, SPIE 523, p.207)). The authors state:

> The original dichromated gelatin process for holographic application was proposed by Shankoff. He explained that high value of index modulation was obtainable by a fast dehydration of the film after it had been exposed to light and swelled with water.This theory can not explain the very high values of diffraction efficiency (close to 100%) that require very large refractive index changes. Several additional theories have been proposed: a) The voids and cracks by Curran and Shankoff, b) Chemical bonding of isopropanol to chromium atoms by Meyerhofer, c) The small air vacuoles by Case and Alferness and d) The molecular chain spring by Chang. Each of these theories explains the behavior of particular holograms but are not adequate to explain a general model. Graube pointed out the possibility that the gelatin can react differently to the process depending on its harness and other parameters.The new theory proposed is more general in explaining the different efficiencies and noise in the holograms. It is based on the experimental observations of the reaction between isopropanol and the gelatin in different concentrations. Graube has reported that gelatin can be precipitated by the alcohol forming amorphous aggregates. Not only does this occur but, depending on the state of the gelatin the alcohol will produce no precipitates or precipitates of different transparency. Most important, it can produce an isopropanol-gelatin gel which remains after the film is processed and dried.The new theory is illustrated in Figure 3. The gelatin, after it has been exposed and washed is in a swollen state, the swelling being proportional to the initial hardening of the gelatin and the energy in the exposition. The gelatin is then introduced in the isopropanl bath and different reactions can occur. The gelatin is classified very soft when the parts that have been less exposed to the light (minima of interference) are dissolved during the wash and the solution is trapped between the insoluble parts which have received more light (maxima of interference). In the alcohol the gelatin in the solution is precipitated as opaque amorphous grains. When the film is dried the alcohol evaporates and the gelatin grains form the walls in the volume which have contained the gelatin solutions and now will contain air. The result is the internal cracks that will cause large noise (scattering) but also will produce high efficiencies due to the large index modulation between the solid gelatin and the air in the cracks.

Incidentally, I wonder if different dehydrating solutions would lead to different results. Such solvents would imply ethanol, methanol, acetone or methyl ethyl ketone.

> Several alcohol baths may be used, graduated in water/alcohol ratio and temperature; or the plate may be immersed in alcohol immediately after the water wash. The rate of progression from 100 percent water to 100 percent alcohol can strongly affect brightness, color, and bandwidth; and the temperature progression can affect brightness, color, bandwidth, and scatter. Isopropanol is the best alcohol to use, but ethanol, methanol, and acetone also work. Using ethanol and methanol typically results in dim, very low noise holograms that reconstruct at wavelengths shorter than the exposure wavelength. With isopropanol the reconstruction wavelength and bandwidth can be varied over wide ranges by proper choice of temperatures, concentrations, and rates in the water to alcohol transition.
