eric343 Fri Apr 29, 2005 11:06 pm #1
Today, 33 students spread over two different intro-to-studio-art classes worked under my direction to each create their own hologram. 90% of them succeeded.

I had a total of two hours with each group of students spread over two days (two periods), Monday and Friday.

The setup was the Saxby lab-stool-and-sand-cup design using the Integraf diode lasers, PFG-01 2.5x2.5 plates, and JD-2 developer. I had three lab-stool-and-laser "stations" set up on the concrete floor of the darkroom.

On Monday, I gave them an explanation of what we were going to do and how to choose an object, then took them into the darkroom for a demo. Both demos worked perfectly, producing a decent if not spectacular hologram.

Today, I gave them a quick refresher run-through, took a look at the objects (and directed many of them to spray them white), and turned out the lights in the darkroom. The workflow was such that three would do their holograms at a time while another three stood by and watched, to get their eyes adjusted to the very dark [my safelight is too dim] darkroom and get an idea of what they were about to do.

Each put their object on the glass plate "table" resting on the crossbars of the lab stool, in the middle of the visible rectangle of illumination. They then put the card in front of the laser, and put a plate ("sticky side up," which I pre-determined and handed them the plate in the correct orientation) under their object. After waiting 30s to 60s, they exposed 7s (one-one-thousand...) and developed using the "Until you can't see the edge of the tray, but can still see the safelight" as a guide. Rinse-bleach-rinse, and they took the holograms outside where they dried them with a blow-drier. (Not exactly the best way, but simple and fast.)

That is excellent Eric, I think we should all push that glass table top system for Holography teaching globally..

I hope my post of 6 March in reply to yours was instrumental for your success. The key point being that provided the object is able to sit FIRMLY ontop of the plate, just about all the many ambient vibrations in a schoolroom do not cause a problem because the object and plate "ride the storm together" as I once said way back ("Holosphere" Oct '79).

One additional practical tip I would mention is the problem of objects that have a potential to do a micro rock when you tap the plate gently with fingernail and listen out for vibration. (I always did the finger tap test with every piece). The obvious thing one goes for is to stick it down with a tiny bit of sticky gum ( "BlueTack" in UK). I used to find it important to not allow the object to rest ontop of such stuff or it could take over an hour to stop a microcreep occurring. If it is tolerable from the pretty picture standpoint I advocate using the gum on many hard objects, it can make the difference between a bright image and a subdued one. It should be used to hold together only the hard points of contact between object and plate.
