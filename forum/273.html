Tom B. Sat Jul 23, 2011 3:10 am #6
Not a stupid question - I'm still trying to figure it out. I made some crappy ones out of 1/2" angle iron and was not thrilled

with the stability or ease of use.

First principle - the plate (or film sandwiched between plates) must be rigidly attached to the holder. On ONLY one side,

it must make direct contact with metal or some other rigid material. The other side can use a compliant material to

evenly apply pressure to hold it against the base. If both sides are rigid, you risk putting the plate under stress where it is

undecided where it should settle down. For a film sandwich, best choice of pressure points is not well understood.

Second principle: the holder and base must be rigidly attached (no slop or ooze permitted) to the table holding the object,

which should similarly be rigidly attached. OK for plate holder but not possible for a fuzzy dolly type object.

For improvised setups where blu-tac or similar putty-type materials are used, always make sure that the plate or object actually

touches the table, then apply blu-tac to stabilize. If instead it is sitting on a wad of blu-tac it will not be stable as it

gradually sinks into it. Similarly with an elevated object, sit it on a rigid support, then apply blu-tac to stabilize. If the object wants to

rock and roll, you may need more rigid support or something better shaped to support it.

I sometimes ignore these guidelines and often get away with it, but it's best to try to keep the object-to-plate setup

stable and immune to vibrations. Wasted plates = hours subtracted from productive holographic production.
Justin W Sat Jul 23, 2011 2:13 pm #7
Hello. I have had had consistent success holding filmplates with magnets. I purchased from the web a nice handful of rare earth magnets (black epoxy-coated cubes 3/8" in size). A good way with this method to avoid putting any stress or strain on the glass is to attach at three points of contact. Two rigid steel uprights firmly anchored to your table are required for this. Two strong magnets on one upright and a single strong magnet on the other should do the trick. This technique is best used with a filmplate (flexible film laminated or otherwise index-matched to a single sheet of glass - black glass for transmission and clear glass for reflection), as the anchors will cast a small shadow and this is fine on the plain glass border around the film on a filmplate featuring a piece of film smaller than the glass. If the magnets attached to the steel uprights are strong enough (as most rare earth magnets should be), they will have the strength to pull straight through the glass to a 1/4-20 nut or similar mass of steel, which will hold the glass firmly against the three magnets on the steel.

For ease of use and and flexibility of table geometry, as well as low cost, I use a pair of the Harbor Freight magnetic bases as the uprights. The square magnets on the round upright posts will seem a little wiggly when not in use, but when the glass is against them and the nut on the other side of the glass takes hold (careful not to let it snap too vigorously against the glass as it will crack it), the magnets will orient themselves to present a nice flat side for the glass to hold against and stay firm under the magnetic forces.

With really thick glass you may find that the 1/4-20 nut doesn't hold as firmly through the glass as you would like it to. If this works out to be the case simply replace the nut with a (4th, 5th, 6th) magnet.

Back for a quick edit: This technique may well hold a filmplate securely-enough to the uprights to defy gravity, but I wouldn't chance it - even if it appears to be held firmly in the air, it's likely to be slowly sliding down. Set up a situation that features this method where you have the bottom edge of the filmplate either resting on the table or on a solid support, then let the magnets worry only about keeping it pressed to the uprights.

Good luck!

Justin W Fri Sep 23, 2011 11:48 am #16
Hi John! Sorry I didn't reply earlier to your question regarding the orientation of nuts/magnets in my plateholder; I haven't been online much lately.

I've found that with normal thickness glass (~1/8"), that the magnets have enough hold to secure the glass with only steel nuts or similar bits of steel pinching the glass there. With 1/4" glass, a magnet on both sides may be required.

I guess you've experimented some there and have figured this out already. I'm thrilled that you've had success with this idea! I've found it to be a very versatile arrangement on my steel table - very easy to set up and downright user-friendly in the dark at exposure time.

Note to anyone interested in implementing this idea - John's question regarding whether to use a magnet on both sides of the glass is mostly a matter of glass thickness and magnet strength - and - If your magnets are powerful enough, you may accidentally break glass thinner than 1/4" by using a magnet on both sides! Be careful of that. Whether using a bit of steel or a magnet on the second side, always be mindful to click it to the glass as gently as possible. I've found that touching the nut against the glass slightly above or below the magnet on the other side and letting it seek its own position is a good way to avoid breakage.

The plateholder is built of glass, held together with sodium silicate (water glass). The "test object" is a series of frosted glass rods. Joe Farina Sun Sep 25, 2011 11:48 am #19
Hi Kiffdino. Thanks.

Your're quite right about everything you said. It's a specific plate holder for the kind of sculptures I'm planning to use. It's designed for a horizontal reference beam, parallel to the table, for Brewster's angle, with the plate held in place by gravity. (In this case, the reference beam is coming from below.) It wouldn't be practical for a variety of objects, unless you have some skills with materials and techniques used for solidly supporting objects from the glass base, up close to the film plane. Say for example you wanted to make a hologram of small porcelain cat. I would drill a hole in the porcelain cat using a daimond core drill, drill another hole in the glass base plate, and insert a glass rod into the porcelain cat, and into the glass base, to bring the cat up to the position from the film plane where I wanted it. Actually, I wouldn't drill directly into the glass base, but I would use a secondary piece of glass as a base (resting on the other glass base in the plateholder) and drill into that, so that the cat and support assembly could be removed. Kind of like in the photo, where those glass rods are cemented to another base. As cement, I'm using sodium silicate.

I've included some photos of another plate holder I built, which is more adjustable. It uses three steel fingers, and the plate is again held by gravity. The reference beam needs to be elevated, and coming down from an angle, in this case. It's mostly built from aluminum, but there is a frosted glass plate on top, so that the object can rest on the frosted glass, and light can come from the other side, for backlighting, if desired.

Joe Farina Sun Sep 25, 2011 1:03 pm #21
The wet diamond bandsaw is good for cutting, but the cut edge is quite rough. To smooth the edges, wet sanding is needed. If you're just doing a few holograms (not a production run) you can simply wet-sand the edges with ordinary silicon carbide paper, with the paper wrapped around something hard like an acrylic block or piece of hardwood. But it's imprtant to keep the glass and sandpaper wet at all times with water (maybe with a spray bottle, or something that's constantly dripping on it, etc.) because if the glass gets too hot, it will crack. You can proceed to finer grits to get a smoother finish, but doing it by hand gets quite laborious.

To grind and polish glass on a production basis is a real can of worms, it takes a lot of time and money for the equipment.
Joe Farina Sun Sep 25, 2011 1:08 pm #22
I should have mentioned, that if you intend to hand-lap glass, it should probably be done by fixing the silicon carbide paper to a flat surface, and keep it very wet, maybe in a shallow pool of water, and rub the edge of the hologram on that. With the coarse grades of paper (especially 120 or even 220) the glass will heat up very quickly, almost instantly. But by the time you get to 400 and 600, much less water is needed.
