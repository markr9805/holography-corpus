Ed Wesly Mon Oct 27, 2008 8:16 pm #6
What usually makes holographic stereograms unviewable is way too much subject motion, creating what are called "time blurs" for lack of a better term. Many CG suffer from that. Even the famous Kiss Multiplex has a little bit of it when the hand moves and the girl winks.

Other problems I have seen with Geola printed stereograms are that you must stand in a particular spot at a certain distance to see the image. This is not good for a myope (near-sighted person) like myself, who likes to stick their nose in a good Denisyuk holo, at least until I get in the way of the beam. I have also seen these things be very noisy, like the black areas are hazy, which may be more of a problem of materials rather than technique.

One of the best holographic stereograms (and in color) I have ever seen is of a cute Japanese girl playing some sort of guitar/synthesizer. It looks like a little person on the embossed sheet. However she does not play the instrument, as some would be tempted to do, and under laser light I discovered that there are just two frames of info, a left and a right view, which would account for the sharpness.

I would have to question the point of having 1024 viewpoints, especially if there is a constant motion going on. We made some holograms from Spiderman movie footage at CFC, and final result was just a swoosh of red because of too much motion.

Jacques Tue Dec 23, 2008 4:06 am #12
Hi,

I read a lot of speculations on synthetic holography (computer generated holograms) in the past few months on many news websites, forums and blogs. So I decided to enter the discussion in your forum.

First of all, a bit of history about the RabbitHoles technology. In 1998, a small group of researchers in Montreal (Canada) decided to develop a “holographic printer” (in fact we should call this a “holographic imager” since there is no print but an optical imaging process). I was part of that team. After about one year we started to have vibrations problems. Geola’s pulsed lasers seemed to be a good solution so we went to Vilnius, Lithuania. At that time Geola wasn’t much into computer generated holograms. They had this impressive holographic camera and monochrome (green) YAG pulse laser. We became aware of the potential and ask them to develop a RGB pulsed laser and a first monochrome prototype of a holographic imager. In the meantime, we founded our company called XYZ Imaging, and started a close collaboration with Geola.

This first prototype was with the same laser. We made monochrome computer generated holograms on Slavich glass plates. We got the RGB laser system with the second prototype and printed full colour holograms on glass plates up to 60 X 80 cm. Then the development moved mostly in Montreal and we developed a machine that used 1 meter large rolls of films. In 2003 the holographic imager was ready. We could print large (usually 1.5 X 1 m) full colour holograms in a few hours. Unfortunately, R&D in a dark laboratory isn’t enough. Business problems started and the company was sold to XYZRGB and then to RabbitHoles. They continue improving the technology.

What the imager does is exposing the emulsion with the split laser beams, one beam passing through a LCD displaying the processed images. 1280 rendered images, corresponding to as much points of view, are used to make a hologram. A series of overlapped holographic cells (holographic pixels) are exposed on the emulsion. 0.8mm or 1.6mm wide in the Geola / XYZ Imaging / RabbitHoles technology. When lighted correctly, as with any kind of hologram, the 3D space and volumes are reconstructed with horizontal parallax. Full parallax is possible, but instead of 1280 images, you will need 1,638,400 images for a square hologram. A few hours of rendering will become many days. Vertical could use fewer images, nevertheless for now, horizontal parallax is more practical.

Now, don’t expect synthetic holography without problems. But at least, you should recognize that it brings many solutions to problems that holographers became accustomed to. This technology is vibration free, large format, full colour and automated, with no limitations to subject matters since the images are computer generated. Yes there can be time smear as in other multiplexed holograms. A hologram such as my “Tractatus Holographis” ([http://www.i-jacques.com/tractatus_en.html](http://www.i-jacques.com/tractatus_en.html) ) is showing this optical effect. As soon as this hologram was seen by people, I got a lot of comments on the blurry effect of the page turn. Many liked it and though that it was made purposely. It may have some aesthetic value but to me it’s still a defect. Large displacements of image features on many holographic cells will cause a distortion, but animations on smaller areas won’t produce it. It’s a relation between the amounts of holographic cells covered by the movement and the amount of points of view or images. In “Tractatus Holographis”, the page turn produce time smear but the small perspective drawing and the 3D illustration of the viewing pyramid doesn’t. Maybe these distortions could be solved with technological improvements, but composition can have a very positive effect. How about the 30 degrees rule in cinema for example? We learned to cope we these kind of impact on content in other image medias, why shouldn’t we be able to deal with these new limitations? After all, the biggest problem I have is when I say that I make holograms. There’s always someone saying “oh yeah, those green blurry images?” I get annoyed by the fact that, in the public eye, holography is still in the seventies. At least now I can make my images on my laptop, send them to the imaging company and a few days later, I will receive a large full colour hologram.

We have here a new media. It’s still holography but it is different from analog holography. It has its own optical characteristics and limitations, with particular effects on perception and also on content choices and composition. There is a whole new visual language to develop here. If you want to know more, check Linda Law’s articles in June and July 2008 issues of Computer Graphics world:

The cost of producing these holograms can be quite high, and I have a suggestion to reduce the cost to individuals.

If a group of 3D artists get together and decide to produce artwork for a single size ‘rabbit holes’

hologram, than they can share the set-up costs and it becomes cheaper to produce each one. So if the set-up cost of a 50cm x 60cm hologram size is $3000, and 3 artists decide to produce holograms at this size, than the set-up cost could be split between the 3, resulting in each artist paying 1000$ set up cost + cost of printing. Of course, all the artists would have to place the order at the same time. This could be done via a web-site (or perhaps here) where artists sign up for a certain size and then have to produce and deposit their artwork with the web-site by a certain date. What do people on this forum think of the feasibility of setting up such a web-based 3D artists' co-operative?
