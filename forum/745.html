BobH Thu Aug 01, 2013 9:39 pm #5
I used a technique that worked pretty well, to reduce an image about 30%. What I did was:

1) made a 12x16" transmission hologram H1 of a 3" diameter object (a skull),

2) reconstructed that to get a real image and made a 12x16" reflection hologram H2 tuned for 633nm so that its reconstructed image would project waaaaaay out in front of the plate,

3) reconstructed the H2 to provide a real image in the aperture of a 5" diameter, very fast AR coated negative achromat lens (I got from the Surplus Shed 20 years ago) so the image appears reduced when looking through the lens toward the H2,

4) made another 12x16" transmission hologram H3 to record the reduced image,

5) made a 4x5" image-planed reflection hologram H4 tuned to reconstruct at 514nm,

6) sent that off to be contact copied as 2x2" reflection holograms H5 in DuPont's photopolymer film (before they restricted it).

That's a heck of a lot of Hs! Everything was done on Agfa 8E75HD with a Spectra-Physics 907 He-Ne (except the last step). One would think the result would be a mess of noise, but careful processing and index-matching the plates helped enormously. When the image was projected into the center of the lens, no reduction. As the image was moved back from the center of the lens, it reduces nicely. I'd like to hear Dinesh's analysis of the distortions one should expect (lens concave side toward H2), but they are minimal compared to the "ballooning" typically seen in such images. Anyone wanting one just PM me your address and I'll send it off. The pic below shows the lens in a scene I did with Ron & Bernadette Olson very soon after meeting them around '92 I think.

Dinesh Sat Aug 03, 2013 9:05 am #7
> BobH: I'd like to hear Dinesh's analysis of the distortions one should expect (lens concave side toward H2)

It's difficult to say exactly without numbers such as focal lengths, distance of object (d_o), distance of image (d_i) and some of the holographic characteristics such as beam divergence (or convergence) and differences thereon between the various H's. When you say: "lens concave side toward H2", does that mean there was only one concave side?

What I think I can say is that the lens appears to have a larger diameter than the image, which would help reduce coma and astigmatism. Assuming that the object was on-axis, this would cut down on coma and astigmatism, since coma and astigmatism are functions of object points off-axis. If an object point is off-axis just a little coma starts to appear, as the object point is placed further from the axis, astigmatism starts to come in. So, the larger the object compared to the lens diameter, the more is the image prone to coma and astigmatism. By the way, the resolution of a lens is also proportional to the lens diameter, so the larger the lens, the better the resolution. Since you used lasers throughout, chromatic aberration does not become a factor. You may have got some longitudinal mismatch (magnification along the axis), but if the object's transverse dimensions were much larger than the object's longitudinal dimension, which it seems to be, then you'd probably not notice.

> BobH: they are minimal compared to the "ballooning" typically seen in such images.

This "ballooning" is typically a mismatch between the beam divergence of the reference beam and the recon beam, assuming we mean the same thing by "ballooning". This "ballooning" is caused by field curvature, in which the image of a planar object is no longer planar but bulges at the centre. In holography, this is usually a function of beam divergence. Remember that if you reconstruct an H2 referenced with a collimated beam, or a diverging beam - as in a Single Beam Denisyuk - by a diverging white light source diverging through a different angle (Denisyuk case), you'll get field curvature. I mention field curvature from a holographic point of view in terms of beam divergence mismatch in the section on third-order Seidel aberrations in holography towards the end, but I can't remember the exact equations right now.

Dinesh Wed Aug 07, 2013 8:03 am #13
> Ed Wesly: Dinesh wrote:"Because?" in reply to Kaveh1000If you look at my post above,it is because longitudinal magnification is the product of the x and y lateral magnification. Basic fact of life in optics.

I'm not sure what x and y is, since there is no definition of the axes. I assume that by 'x' and 'y', you mean the absolute dimensions of the image irrespective of the dimensions of the original object, along some plane. If so, then the longitudinal magnification is not just the product of the absolute lateral dimensions of the image space, there is also the absolute longitudinal dimension of the image space. You're probably thinking of the relationship,

M(l) = -M(t)^2

in which case, it's not 'x' and 'y', but delta(x) and delta(y).

This derivation assumes a thin lens. If the principle planes are not co-incident with the focal planes, then the separation of the planes needs to be factored in.

However, the relationship above does not hold for holography. The equations for holography are based on the phase relationships between object space and image space, and so on the phase relationship of the object and image space. The scaling of the spatial frequencies is also a factor that does to enter into the simple lens equation. It is possible to create an "equivalent" lens equation taking into account the "equivalence" of the reference phase sphere, but the analogy has it's limits. This is all explained in section 5 of the aberrations paper (which jsfisher has kindly put up in the "reader" section of the wiki. paper) and, in more detail, in the Meyer and Champagne papers.

One point of caution is that in the Meyer and Champagne papers, the diffraction mechanism is Raman Nath. The equations do not hold for Bragg diffraction. One of these days I might work on aberations in Bragg Diffracted Holograms.
