BobH Sun Jan 14, 2007 8:42 am #5
I'll put a plug in for a surplus breadboard from Newport, TMC, or maybe Thorlabs. Watch for them on ebay. Maybe look for a used 4x8' table. You can often get them for very little if you can move them. Why spend the time building a homebrewed version of the real thing when the real thing may not be out of reach?

I bought a 4x8' breadboard from Newport 25 years ago and I still have it. It offers the best surface for serious holography and it's easily moveable by two men with a couple of piano dolllies. Its local rigidity allows components to be bolted firmly, minimizing the effects of vibrations (whether airbourne or comming through the legs). It's also flat, a feature overlooked by those building otherwise excellent homegrown clones. And by flat I mean +/-0.004" which means your bases make proper contact and don't rock. It also means you can slide a spatial filter around on the table with the pinhole in and not need to adjust it much when it gets to its new position.

My old breadboard has been on seven different tables, mostly interlocked cinder blocks (holes toward the width) on plywood a panel. I used lumber sideboards and squeezed the blocks together with 5/8" all thread running through the holes.
Joe Farina Sun Jan 14, 2007 1:30 pm #6
I use granite. This has become more available in recent years, since it is commonplace for kitchen counters in homes. The difference between this granite and the kind traditionally used for isolation tables is it's thickness. It is thinner than what is ideal. Especially in the 1960's, thick granite isoation tables were often used, in the range of 4 to 6 inches thick! So if the thin ("kitchen counter") granite is used, it needs a rigid support structure underneath it (at least for a tabletop of moderate dimensions).

In my opinion, as long as this support structure is very rigid, the thin granite will have about the same performance as a thick slab. There is a benefit in using the thin slab/support structure method, in that the weight of the granite is not overwhelming, and it will probably only need two people to move it into position. My table is rather small, and I can manage to move it by myself.

For the support structure, I use perforated steel tubing, which is square in cross section, 1.5 inches X 1.5 inches. Then I run 5/8 inch threaded rod through the center of some of these pieces, and firmly tighten these rods on the ends with bolts. It looks like a short "ladder" with three "rungs." The threaded rod goes through the three rungs to hold the whole thing firmly together. For legs, I use 2 inch plumbing pipes (6 of them). I attach (with bolts) 6 female-threaded "sockets" to the frame so that the legs (which are male-threaded at the ends) can go into them. I forget what those sockets are called, but they are also used in plumbing.

All of these items were from the home-center (Menard's). I'm not sure if the other home-centers like Home Depot or Lowe's has the square steel tubing. For vibration isolation, I use a racquet ball under each leg, held in place by plastic fixtures that I also found at the home center (I don't know what these fixtures are for, but they screwed onto the 2 inch pipe and held the balls in place nicely). One essential piece of equipment for all of this is an abrasive cut-off saw, which is necessary to cut the tubing, rod, and pipes. I use a 14 inch abrasive saw which I bought from Harbor Freight for $49. Trying to do all this with a hacksaw would be pure misery.

Well, that's my approach. The holograms I wish to make are very demanding with regards to table stability (i.e., split-beam reflection holograms with long exposure times), so that's why I took the effort to go this route.

Since I have used all three talbles, Sand, Concrete and Steel, I thought I might as well chime in.

Sand:

Adv - Cheap, easy to set up, easy to dismantle.

Dis - Dirty, optics or other objects can fall into sand, difficult to reposition optics, difficult to change inner tubes, difficult to keep beam in 2-d plane, bases need to work with sand.

Concrete:

Adv - Difficult to build, rigid if done correctly, easier to move optics but still not ideal, easy to keep beam in 2-d plane.

Dis - Expensive, very difficult to dismantle, difficult to change inner tubes, can get dusty over time with wear, bases have to be static.

Steel:

Adv - Easily move optics, easy to keep beam in 2-d plane, accepts magnet bases, easier to move then concrete, stays clean, bases can be static or magnetic.

Dis - Can be expensive.

This is the first time I ever had a steel table and I love it. I had a piece of steel from years ago that was about 3/8 inch thick and was almost 4' by 8'. I got it from a steel yard and was not too expensive. But I think it weighs in excess of 500lbs so it was a little difficult to move and put in place. Here is some photos of the construction.

[http://holograms3d.com/TheLab/NewLab/NewLab.htm](http://holograms3d.com/TheLab/NewLab/NewLab.htm)

In these photos I am using static bases but since then, have upgraded to magnetic mounts. The magnetic bases have a smaller footprint and are easy to move and position. I love this table. It is by far the easiest to work with. I doubt I would ever go back to sand or concrete.

So, if you have a choice, I think steel would be best but either of the three tables have proven to work.

For wet room. I once had to run two hoses from the basement to the processing area. Both hoses joined and had a valve on them so I could get cold, warm or hot water. For a drain, I used the black flexible drain and ran it back down the basement and tied it into the septic system. I to am on well water and septic tank. As you will find you are doing to use a lot of distilled water I simply put the used chemicals back in the gallon jugs and about twice a year I take the jugs to the land fill. There they have a special place for chemicals, paints etc...

Good luck with you lab and hope to see some holograms soon!

I guess I'll chime in too. I have used both marble top tables and steel, and I strongly prefer steel because you can use magnets to hold practically anything. If you happen to bump a mount while adjusting another, it stays put with magnets but moves with just gravity holding it in place. For cheap and dirty mounts I use 1/2" iron pipe nipples and flanges. I glue 3 magnets on the base - no rocking or movement. My current table is 16 guage steel on top of hex soda can matrix, with a steel skin on the bottom too. Light & stiff.

On the water issue, due to concerns about chlorine and other contaminants in the tap water, I use bottled water for mixing chemicals and rinsing plates. I too have a septic system and I'm very concerned about what goes into it. Therefore I re-bottle all wash water and used chemicals, and wipe all utensils, trays, etc with paper towels before rinsing them in tap water over the sink. The full bottles of waste go to the local hazmat collection 2x yearly...

(I do silver only, no DCG)

Joe Farina Tue Jan 16, 2007 6:24 pm #21
Let me say a last word about steel versus granite. Steel is fine for work on silver halide material, whether it be a Newport table with a steel top, or a homemade steel table. And there's no doubt about the convenience of magnetic mounts.

But if you want to take the plunge with DCG, and want to use a split-beam setup with limited laser power, granite has the decided advantage of lower themal expansion. This is a significant consideration when it comes to long exposure times. That's what makes fringe-lockers a necessity for certain setups on steel tables with resists, polymers, or DCG (unless you're using an Invar table, or as Rallsion says, bathe the whole table in a constant-temperature bath of oil).

On the other hand, almost any kind of table should be fine for simple Denisyuk holograms on DCG. Actually, split-beam DCG holograms seem to be something of an oddity, perhaps the difficulty factor is a big part of it. The problem with strait, unassisted Denisyuk holograms is that they invariably have "devastating" frontal illumination. Using bounce-back mirrors (assisted Denisyuk) is of very limited benefit, hence my interest in split-beam holography.
