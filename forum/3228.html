The calculation alone is OK. However, the justification for the calculation is not stated. Of course, in a naive way, you can always divide the numbers, but without justification of the numbers they're meaningless.

In this case, you start by assuming a 2" diameter. Let's work in cgs, since that's the final result is computed in cgs. In this case, the diameter is 5cm. If the diameter is 5cm, the circle will fit a square of side:

5/sqr root(2) = 3.5cm

However, Michael states :

"I cut a stack of glass plates ranging in size from 19x38x2mm to 38x38x2mm."

so the larger square would not be illuminated at the corners. In fact, there's an approx 15% error between your illumination and the necessary illumination for the size of the sample.

You're also assuming complete Lambertian illumination. The actual illumination profile of the beam would follow :

I(y) = I(0)exp(-(alpha)*(y^2))

and so create a further error.

Exposure is not something that can be exactly calculated and seen to work. Any calculation must be viewed as an approximation driven by the molecular structure of the emulsion. The whole point of exposure is to create a set of fringes that then modulate the incoming reconstructuion beam.The level of modulation of the reconstruction then recreates the wavefunction, hopefully linearly. However, the level of modulation is dependant on the fringe visibility:

I_v = (I-0 - I_max))/(I_0 + I_max)

a la Michaelson's criterion, which in turn depends on the physio-chemical reactions within the emulsion. The macroscopic "sensitivity" is an overall averaging of the an idealised number and will vary by various physical factors. For example, as the plate is cooled, the reaction kinetics will change which will, in turn, alter the "exposure". To simply say:"This is the exposure, so I'm surprised you didn't use...this" is a little simplistic in a practical situation.

[quote:3f8c99aca2="Jim McPherson"]So what is the effect of varying the emulsion thinkness?

-Jim[/quote:3f8c99aca2]

Many and varied. Assuming you're shooting a volume hologram, ie that the fringe seperation is approx 1/10th of the emulsion thickness, it can be a decrease in bandwidth, an increase in absorption, an increase in scatter and/or an increase in noise (depending on what "noise theory" you prefer.

Let's take the point of view of the reference wave as it goes through the emuslion a la Kogelnik. Following Kogelnik, we assume that at the first surface (where the two beams first meet) some of the energy is reflected and some transmitted through into the emulsion. So already there's loss due to Fresnel reflection. This is dependant on the angle of the beams and the polarisation of each beam. I believe that Kaveh showed the relationship of TE and TM waves against reflectivity some time back (it is very index-dependant) and angle dependant loss can be calculated by the Fresnel formulae. . So, OK, what's left of the two waves go into the emulsion. As they go in, the reference beam increases and the object decreases due the energy interchange between the two oscillating modes. The beams however lose energy due to absorption, so the beam ratio changes as the waves travel through the emulsion. Of course, since this emulsion has been chosen because it absorbs this particular wavelength, the absorption is probably high. Since the beam ratio entering the emulsion is angle dependant, the beam ratio inside the emulsion is not what it is outside the emulsion. Also bear in mind that the index causes a change in angle inside the emulsion so the inter-beam angle is also changing. All of this results in a change in beam ratio as the waves travel into the emuslion. The thicker the emuslion, the greater the change caused by these effects. As the beam ratio changes, the fringe contrast changes. So the deeper you go into the emulsion, the weaker is the Michaelson fringe visibility. Thus, since delta n is now reduced, so is the efficency of the hologram. Now also, as the fringes are being created, the newly created fringes are diffracting the incoming waves away from the depth of the emulsion. So the thicker the emulsion and the longer the exposure, the weaker still are the fringes in the back area of the emulsion. It's a relative simple matter to now assume a variable absorption factor (the complex part of the refractive index is now z-dependant) and plug the new values of c_r, c_s and alpha into the standard Kogelnik equations to come up with new terms. These new terms will show a variable efficency inside the volume of the emulsion. However, as new (weaker) fringes are being created, the statistical variation of the fringe population decreases (statistically, sigma is dropping) so the hologram gets more and more narrow band. In addition to all of this, local density variations in the emulsion are causing multiple scattering events which contribute to low frequency gratings which may have a high contrast because these low frequency gratings are caused by local events (sort of a drunkards walk model) and so the beam ratio is stronger than the ratio causing the "desired" gratings.

So, you now have a variable, depth-dependant eficency, a decrease in bandwidth that's also depth dependant and an increase in noise and random scatter. the image therefore gets dimmer, noisier (maybe, depending on which way the emulsion is pointing), and probably more difficult to wet-process, due to the loss in liquid absorption. However, for narrow band radiometric holograms, this may be the ideal scenario.

Yes, pretty much. The "ideal" for emulsion thickness for display is about 10 microns. This is assuming a HeNe and an interbeam angle of about 40 degrees. You want to get "thick" gratings so you can get the brightness, but the downside is that the angle is restricted (In a Denisyuk, the angle over a particular colour is restricted - the hologram changes colour as you turn it due to Bragg mismatch). There is a factor known as the 'Q' factor that determines this, but as a rule of thumb, if the grating spacing is approx 1/10th of the emulsion thickness, you'll get a thick or volume hologram. You also want approx 10 Bragg planes or so. In the case of a HeNe (633nm) with an interbeam angle of 40 degrees the fringe spacing is about a micron or so. Hence a 10 micron emulsion will give 10 planes. This is about right for brightness without making it too narrow band.

I don't know about a standard reference. When I first got a sample from Sergio, he mentioned that it was important to get a particular emulsion thickness. I can't remember what, but I think 20 microns (remember this was a material thay made 2 years ago, so this is may not be true of present materials). However, the emulsion came as a liquid, so we had to figure out a way to get the required 20u. We looked at sveral possible spacers from sticky tape to trash can liners to Saran wrap, either singly or in layers. Tape was far too thick, but saran wrap and trash can liners were about right. All these materials have their widths on the side of the box, so it's pretty simpe to figure out. We have a micrometer screw guage so it was possible to find the thickness of materials that were not stated anywhere, however it's also possible interferometrically. Create a set of fringes, then sandwich two plates together and put it in the path of the fringes. Note fringe displacement. Now sandwich a piece of tape bvetween the glass, insert into interferometer and not new fringe displacement. The hologram only records beam intensities through the fringe visibility. the medium is inherently non-linear. In this case,

I_r/I_o /= I_re/I_c

where

I_R is the ref, I_o is the object, I_re is the reconstructed wave and I_c is the reconstructing wave. I've used /= as a non-equality. There's also the problem of non-uniformity in the fringe distribution. The resulting bandwidth will alter the intensity spread due to the non-uniform frequency response of the various fourier components in the planes. There'll be a re-distribution of the initial power, in one set of input frequencies, to a redistribution of output power across the output bandwidth in a non-linear manner. This will also cause divergence. Remember, too, that a gaussian is infinite.
