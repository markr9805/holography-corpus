Does functionality determine nomenclature? Is a TV not a TV when it's turned off?

Conversely, (or perhaps similarly) is any diffractive structure, such as a CD, a hologram when it’s diffracting? What if you cannot stop it diffracting, such as moonlight through a hazy atmosphere or an oil slick in sunlight? Also, standard “woolworths” film is roughly 200 l/mm. Thus the grain size is about 200nm. So, if the film is standard, but slow (such as perhaps ASA800 or 1000) then the grain size could well get up to 400nm and therefore diffract blue light. This will only diffract when you take it out of your pocket.

It seems that you must define not only function, but also intended function, and then go on to describe the limits of the intended function.. So, if the “hologram” diffracts light so as to create a particular waveform which is interpreted by human eyes as a cat only when exposed to particular set of wavelengths at a particular angle(s), then it’s a hologram. Of course, one benefit is that the particular form of diffraction no longer matters. All you need to is to state the expected diffraction and, if it does diffract like this, it’s a hologram.. [quote:db48d1bf3b="MarkCavin"]Trees don’t violate conservation laws. An up tree cannot become a downed tree without following the intermediate steps between the up and down state.[/quote:db48d1bf3b]

Why not. In any quantum system, the probability of the state of the system upon observation is a statistical weighting of the probabilities of all possible states. The specific wavefunction into which the generalised wavefunction collapses is determined purely by chance (in the Copenhagen interpretation). Energy and momentum conservation is considered in terms of the final state of the observed system (hence the weighting of the collapsed system) and the uncertainty relationships. Thus the exact time and energy of an expelled electron from a beta decay is statistical, but fall within certain limits. So, if you count all trees, then count the number of trees in designated states (up, down, sick, no of branches etc etc - you can pretty much chooses your own scanning set for trees!), the probability of the up or down state is a fraction of that state as a ratio of the complete set of all trees. Energy, momentum and "tree-number" can be taken care of by some sort of uncertainty relation involving the power spectrum of the Fourier of "tree states". In this case, an observation of a down tree (or an up tree) simply becomes a matter of the probability of down trees based on abservation of "already down trees". The tree in the inbetwen states would now be the ratio of the sum of obsrved falling trees (|ft>) + observed down trees (|d> ) and so would be]

|t> = alpha\ft> + beta|d>

with <t|t> = 1

[quote:db48d1bf3b="MarkCavin"] It’s not nature’s problem that you forgot to put the tape in the recorder. [/quote:db48d1bf3b]

No, it's not. But if the recorder were to be seen to work (ie the tape is rolling) and yet no noise is recorded, can you definitely say that the recorder has not recorded because there's nothing to record or that the tape malfunctioned? Of course, you can always test the tape, but all you'll get is information on the tape at the moment of testing. The state of the tape at the moment of the tree flling is unkown. You could have two recorders. Then if both failed to record and yet were stil rolling, you'd conclude:

a) there was no noise made

Each of these states now affects the state of the tree itself etc, etc.

This is really the problem of Schrodingers Cat. The wavefunction of the observer is never taken into consideration nor is any definition of "observer". Clearly, the observer also needs another observer and you end up with Zeno's paradox of infinite regression ( [http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/paradox-zeno/#4.1](http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/paradox-zeno/#4.1) )

Tom B. Sat Jun 25, 2005 10:31 am #31
The idea is that when coherent (single frequency) light from a laser is split and spread out so that part of it directly lights up the recording plate (the reference beam) and the other part lights up an object which reflects light back to the plate ( the object beam), you record "interference fringes", sort of a microscopic zebra striped pattern of light and dark, the pattern and angle of which is determined by the wavelength of the laser, the relative angles of the beams to the plate, and of course the shape and position of the object. You need very high resolution film for this - ordinary photo film is much too coarse. After developing the film, you end up with a very complicated diffraction grating.

In single-beam work (much simpler to do), the light passes through the plate and then reflects back from the object to the plate, so there is no need for a separate beam-splitter.

The central miracle of holography is that when you light up this recorded fringe pattern from the proper angle, you get a 3D image of the original object! The recorded fringes are somehow acting as a diffraction grating which reconstructs the original wavefront. Much like recording and playing back sound on wax cylinders a la Edison.

So why is this amazing? For me it's the unexpected reciprocity between fringe recording and image reconstruction.

Light is a wave motion. There are ideas about light being a particle called a photon. This is sort of, but not completely true. Whether it's a particle called a photon or a wave depends on how it behaves under certain conditions. For us holographers, it's a wave. For people working in high-energy atom smashers, it's a photon. Maybe an analogy better suited for you is: for film it's a wave, for TV it's a photon. So-o-o, being a wave, something waves. Imagine a rope attached to a wall with you holding the other end. Now wave your hand fairly quickly. As you move your hand up and down, the rope waves - parts of the rope are going up (the upstroke of your hand) and parts of the rope are going down (the downstroke of your hand. If someone were to take a picture of the rope, then in the picture, any given point on the rope will be a certain distance up or down. For instance a point 18" from your hand may be half way up and a point 23" away may be quarter way down. This distance of the parts of the rope from it's "unwaved" middle position (ie above or below the center position) is known as the "phase" of the wave. Now suppose someone next to you were also waving a rope and you take a picture of both ropes. If both of you were to start at exactly the same time and wave both ropes in exactly the same manner, then the picture of the waves created would be exactly the same. In both cases, the point 18" away from the end would be halfway up. In this case, the ropes ("waves") are said to be in phase - they're going at exactly the same rate and any given point on either rope is exactly in the same position relative to the unwaved baseline. If your friend were to start waving at a slightly later time, then his wave would be displaced from your wave by an amount depending on how much later he started waving. This point 18" from the end would not be half way up but would be a little higher, if the rope is going up and lower if it's going down (since he started later). The waves are now out of phase.

Let's say you both started at the same time, but his rope is longer than yours. Then could you figure out how much longer by looking at the photograph? Yes, you could count the extra waves (or partial waves) that the extra length of rope makes. If you had three or (let's go nuts!) several million ropes all going at exactly the same rate but all of different lengths, then it's possible to tell how much longer any given rope was from each other. If you decided on a "reference rope" to which all length measurements are made, then you could say, 'This rope is 10" longer and that rope is 5" shorter' . The words 'shorter' or 'longer' as measured against you standard 'reference' rope. Laser light behaves like this because all the waves - 'ropes' - are all waving at exactly the same rate. Thus, if you illuminate an object, you can think of every point of the object as someone, at that point, waving a rope. The 'wall' is the plate. Since all the rope wavers are at different distances, the phase of the light from any point of the object to the plate will be slightly different. By comparing all those phases against a standard reference, you have information of how much longer each wavepath - 'rope' - is and so how much further is that particular point from the plate.

The problem now is how to record these differences in phase. How to make a "photograph" as in the rope analogy. Clearly you cannot really take a picture of a light waving as you can of a rope. What we can do is this: take the phase (remeber: the height of the "rope" above it's baseline) of any wave and add it to the phase of your standard 'reference'. Now, convert this adding procedure to a greyscale. So, if the phase of the rope is all the way down and the reference is all the way up, then the reference is (say) 24" down (negative, -24) and the rope is 24" up (positive, +24), so the net effect is zero. We'll assign the greyscale level of 0 to this - all black. If the rope is all the way up (positive, +24") and the reference is also all the way up (positive, +24"), the net effect is 48 and we'll assign a greyscale of 255 - all white. If, at any point, say 18" off the end, the rope is 16" up and the reference is 7" up, then the addition produces +23. This will have a greyscale value of {23/48}*255 or 122. Thus we have a greyscale picture of the phases of the rope, as compared against the reference. This set of greyscale images is "automatically" done for us by nature. Superimposing two or more light sources causes the light to "automativcally" perform this addition in a process called "interference". The greyscale map produced will be a set of "interference lines" . In the case of light, these interference lines are tiny. In holography, they're approximately 1000 of them per millimeter or, 25,000 in an inch. From these interference lines we can extract information of the distance of any point of the object from the plate.

How do we now extract this information? . This kind of structure has an interesting property. Since the structure was created by adding the phases of two (or more) waves, if you were to pass either wave through it, the wave is altered by the structure to re-create the other wave. In the rope analogy, if you were to restrict the rope by having a thin slit between you and the wall, the phase of the rope would change abruptly on the other side of the slit (providing the slit were narrow enough). The interference lines are slits and so if you were to pass your original reference wave through this set of slits, the slits would alter the reference to re-create the phase structure of the object waves. But remember, the phases of the object waves carried information on the distance of every point of the object to the plate. So now, you've recreated a wave that carries with it a "depth" profile of yor object. Replace the plate with your eyes and the phases of the object waves will be iinterpreted by your brain (which sees the world by interpreting phase relationships) and recreate the depth information. Oh, this process of altering the wave by means of slits is called "diffraction".

The problem is that there are really many types of diffraction, but basically everything is diffraction. It all depends on the type of diffraction. Although I know it raises hackles amongst some, there is Raman-Nath diffraction, there is Bragg diffraction and, in particular, there is the diffractive field structure of a slide in a slide projector, as calculated by the Fresnel-Huygens integral. There are excellant examples and excercises on calculating the diffractive patterns of slides in Goodman's book on Fourier Optics. Just for the hell of it, I once calculated the output of a rainbow hologram assuming it to be a transparency and then a hologram. I used the Fresnel-Huygens integral with the slit as a unit impulse function and then convolved with Raman-Nath. There is a pretty straightforward overview [here](galileo.phys.virginia.edu/%20classes/531.cas8m.fall04/l16.pdf).

So-o-o. Bob. Does a hologram "play" like a record player? I'd say, "at a stretch". The encoding of the data in a record is a pattern on grooves. These grooves enable a needle traversing them to alter a magnetic field, which in turn transfers the energy of the magnetic field to kinetic energy of air molecules which in turn cause variations of pressure. These variations are interpreted as noise. Therefore the recording of the data is accomplished by artificially altering the data from pressure waves into physical grooves by three processes, all of which are artificial: The alteration of pressure waves (sound) into a variation of a magnetic field, artificially created - at least, the creation of the magnetic field is artificial insofar as the current densities have to be created and controlled - the conversion of the magnetic field into physical motion and the etching, by machine, of a platter transferring the physical motion into "hard wired" grooves. The extraction of meaningful data (to humans) involves the same three processes in reverse.

In the recording of a hologram, the interaction of the two beams of light is a natural process whose only "artificiality" - if you like - is the laser source and the recording medium. However, ideally, even the artificial source is not needed. We talked a while back of creating a hologram with sunlight and a filter, which is possible if the sunlight were to enter through a small hole. Thus it's possible to make a hologram with a tiny hole through which sunlight enters (it needs to subtend less than 10 minutes of arc while the sun subtends approx 0.5 degrees or 30 minutes of arc) with a leaf acting as a filter. The only artificial part will then be the recording medium. In extracting the holographic data, ordinary (natural) light is made to alter by interaction with a structure in the material. This interaction creates a wavefunction that simulates an object. However, looking at a real object also simulates a wavefunction. Holography works because the simulated wavefunction from a "real" object is exactly the same as the wavefunction created by interaction of the reconstruction beam. I think questions of "diffraction" are almost moot because the wavefunction generated by interaction of a natural source with a real object is also diffrctive and also works by "interference". The only difference is the type of diffraction - Raman -Nath, Bragg or Fresnel-Huygens. The various electron dynamics are all the same. So, looking at a tree in sunlight is pretty much the same as loking at a hologram of a tree in sunlight. The (diffracted) wavefront in one case is generated by the electrons in the tree and in the other by the electrons in a material that was once photo-sensitive. The exact form of the resulting image and it's effectiveness as data will be determined by the diffractive regime being used. Thus the data acquired in a hologram (sorry HIG!) will be only visible through a fairly narrow range of angles and colours if it's Bragg difractive, while the data accessibility through a large range of angles and a lot of colours is characterised by Raman-Nath diffraction.

Is a transparency in a slide projector akin to "image generation"? Well, one obvious difference is that the data in a slide is accessed by alteration of only the amplitude of a reconstruction source. Can this be described as "difractive"? Yes. The alteration of any light field is diffractive. can the data in a slide be accessed without the actual machine (slide projector)? I'd say, yes, but it negates the meaning of "slide" You can certainly see the slide by holding it up to sunlight and altering the sunlight appropriately to cause data extraction. This is diffractive alteration just like looking at the tree. True, no phase information is altered or created, but you can certainly set up and solve an appropriate Fresnel-Huygens integral. Is this data meaningful in the context? I'd say, no. The point of the slide involves magnification and projection opnto a surface, also artificially created by the machine. So, a slide is certainly "image generation" and it's "diffractive" but it does take artificial components to genrate the image in a meaningful way, as per it's intent.
