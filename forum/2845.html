Dinesh Thu Oct 28, 2004 2:04 pm #2
We got three students through a program by Palomar College. The class went over a two day period. We started on the morning of the first day with an overview of principles and theory. Then we set up an H1 geometry while the students watched. I went through the whole geometry and which beam was which and what their purpose was. I then shot an H1 and had the students each make and develop one for themselves. On day 2, I went through a brief overview of whilte light holography, Denisyuk and H1/H2 geometries and why we see the holo in white light. We had the students each bring in an object of their own and, with some help form us, set up their own single beam Denisyuk. In the afternoon, they, again with some help from us, set up a reflection H1/H2 setup with the H1 shot on the previous day. They then each shot an image-planed reflection H2. We worked throughout on a low power HeNe with PFG-01. I had originally intended to get a box of 25 plates but Alec Jeong pointed out that we could get 4 packs of 6 so each student could have their own set of plates.

The most important lesson I learnt was that after all these years I tend to get a bit blase about the laser and the chemistry (not that I was ever particularly careful ). Safety was the one thing we stressed, both in the laser lab and in the handling of chemicals.

I'd like to have covered rainbows, but we had no time.

Ed Wesly Fri Oct 29, 2004 4:09 pm #4
I am in semi-retirement from teaching holography at the moment. But I do make a hologram in the Optics class I teach at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago (SAIC).

I started teaching holography at the Fine Arts Research and Holographic Center, aka Chicago Museum of Holography, better known as Gallery 1134 in 1980. Then I got gigs at Columbia College, Chicago, and SAIC in the mid-80's. I retired from those two places when I got a full-time job, but still teach Optics and a couple of other Physics classes at SAIC once a week during the evening.

At Columbia we used 5 "Big Beams" for isolation. We got good results even on the fifth floor near an elevator. Every student made theri own SBR.

At SAIC, when things peaked, we had one 4' by 10' Newport table with a S-P 127, illuminating a set up that could be modified to do stereograms (from film), front-lit objects, back lit objects, and one-step rainbow shadowgrams, up to 30 by 40 cm. Another Newport with a S-P 124 was used for the transfers, either rainbow, achromat, or reflection. Every student got to make their own portrait and whatever, as there was 24 hour access privileges.

Some very interesting work was done by my students Jesus Lopez, (Four Dimensional Figure Studies) who was instrumental in taking the lab to the acme of perfection; Dean Randazzo, who was the inspiration for making the stereogram machine, and his body of work using family movie footage and other things that created some very moving images (in more ways than one); and Matt Schreiber whose insane energy created stuff that I had never seen before and haven't seen since in the way of disturbing images (octopi and A-bombs all in one holo, for example!).

Alas and alack, the stereogram system has been taken down, and I don't think that there is anyone there capable of resuscitating it.

I am of course available as a consultant or to take on private students.
