I haven't seen this topic discussed on the forum, so I thought I'd start a thread to throw open the discussion:

Is the revolution in 3D printing going to help or hinder display holography?

On the one hand- why have a hologram when you can have a real model?

On the other- will it fire up holographers imagination, allowing them to combine 3D printed objects with holography in creative ways?

I'd be interested in people's views.
jsfisher Tue Jul 09, 2013 7:29 pm #2
It is possible 3D printers might have an impact in a completely different way. Unlike small milling machines (which are in the same relative price range), 3D printers don't require a machinist skill set to operate. So, could such a printer help in the fabrication of optical mounts and other assembles useful to the hobby? Could an all-thumbs smuck like me make, for example, a serviceable spatial filter out of hot-shot ABS?

Dinesh Wed Jul 10, 2013 11:44 am #4
Not sure you can make the filter. The resolution of 3d printers is of the order of ~50u, too large for any practical filter for holography.I'd thought of using 3d printers to make objects about 6 or 7 years ago, when they first started appearing at trade shows at a reasonable price. But, the output is on cardboard or plastic, too weak for long exposures,and the price was pretty high around 2006. Also, as Jeff says, plastic opto-mechanical components aren't really a good choice for opto-mechanical components for holography. The prices have come down a lot, typically a couple of thousand, but the raw material ain't cheap from what I gather.

Here's an interview of someone in Lux Research, a major player in the 3D printing world: [http://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=8727](http://www.azom.com/article.aspx?ArticleID=8727). There's also a lengthy article covering 3D printers in this month's issue of OPN.
Jeffrey Weil Wed Jul 10, 2013 12:10 pm #5
With a little help from some inserts I think you could make a working filter. The internal threads, screw threads and pin hole are all too small to be printed. But by using off the shelf metal screws, metal threaded inserts glued into the filter where needed and a metal pinhole I think it would work fine.

But that filter only has to hold it's own weight still. I would always use a proper metal mount. It would also have to be pretty beefy to compete with aluminum on performance. This would not be a compact unit.

I think you could also make good lens mounts, not the rod and base, just the bit that actually clamps onto the lens itself. Also, wave plate rotators, adj mirror mounts using the metal screws and inserts, etc... I think you could make lots of little bits. Just not things like plate holders, rods and bases, big mirror mounts etc...For that it's best to go with brute force, lots of mass, lots of weight and made out of as few pieces as possible. Every joint, every place where things are bolted together is a chance for problems. Machine things out as few pieces as possible.
Ed Wesly Wed Jul 10, 2013 12:23 pm #6
I thought I’d be first to go to bat and say that it will, or could, but there have been some posts since I started writing this last night and finishing after a rough morning’s class.

Tony Coleman had a wonderfully modeled head as his contribution to the Holo Forum pass around, and made mention in a different thread here that he was going to pursue such a path, making 3-D models and holographing them. See: [nlutie.com/ewesly/HoloForumBox2013.ppt](nlutie.com/ewesly/HoloForumBox2013.ppt) Let’s hope he chimes in on his plans for future art!

It could be argued that since anyone could make that same model with the file, why bother holographing it? But holography is more than just making verisimilitude, or a realistic depiction of an object; it has the potential for the objects to interact, like by overlapping in space, or to create a sculptural space by their placement in depth, or color play with the RGB purity.

Gone would be the days of poorly crafted models where you could see runny paint and glue drops by having perfectly generated ones. Objects could be made any size, and could be pre-distorted to take into account the aberrations of the holographic process. It’s almost like being able to make anything you can think of, as long as you could program it!

I prefer this quasi analog approach to the computer generated stereograms like those offered by Zebra and Geola. Every time I see these things they are so hard to look at; yeah they are bright, but the signal to noise is usually out of control, making it hard to believe that you are looking at a real object. Lots of time the designers puts in way too much motion and the time smear makes object unrecognizable, unless you look at it with one eye. And even then the geometry is somewhat off sometimes.

It might be possible to cleverly construct optical mounts out of plastic that are interferometrically stable, but aren’t there some 3-D printers that sinter metal? But those might be outside the price range of the hobbyist. Now Jeff snuck in another post as I was finishing up and yeah, I would agree, that shapes could be made out of plastic with metal bits thrown in for threaded pieces. Certainly rotating mounts for wave plates or lens holders would be a boon for us!

But using a 3-D printer to make displays with built-in lights, there’s some food for thought!
lobaz Thu Jul 11, 2013 9:42 am #7
Actually, in June issue of OPN there is a short article about open source 3D printed optic components. See [http://www.plosone.org/article/info%3Ad ... ne.0059840](http://www.plosone.org/article/info%3Adoi%2F10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0059840) and [http://www.thingiverse.com/jpearce/coll ... rce-optics](http://www.thingiverse.com/jpearce/collections/open-source-optics).

Metal optomechanic components are great but just too expensive for non professionals. There is no doubt that proper metal pieces work better than plastic - but the question is if these plastic substitutes can work somehow and how to design them. I think it can be a bit similar to integrated circuit design: as far as I know, it is virtually impossible to make precise electronic elements such as capacitors, resistors etc. inside an integrated circuit. So the challenge is how to design the circuit so that it works with imprecise elements as well.

I agree with Ed that 3-D model predistortion offers great opportunities.

And why a hologram and not a 3D printed object? Because of the space. One box of holograms can display objects that would fill the whole cupboard. And of course, there are many objects that cannot be printed yet, either with fine detail such as jewellery or with special look such as gemstones.
