Ed Wesly Wed Aug 09, 2006 7:55 pm #4
Or another variation of this same question could you make holograms with only white light, sunlight or artificial?

And the answer, at least with gratings, is a resounding yes! And who else would have done such a thing but none other than Emmett Leith himself!

The trick is to use an unslanted grating after the white light source to start a + and - 1st orders dispersed, and then at the end of the interferometer have the blue part of the spectrum line up on top of blue and the red on red. I forget how he did it, but there may have been more gratings involved instead of turning mirrors. (It would take me a bit of research time in the archives to find it which I can't afford this week.)

But I remember him delivering a paper on it at a SPIE conference. The reason why he was doing it was that he was convinced that these types of fringes would be smoother, not filled with noise like fringes from a coherent or laser source. For better diffraction efficiency.

I asked him if it were really hard, (wanting to set one up myself at LFC), and he said it was so easy he could do it with his eyes closed! After a couple of seconds, he said, no, wait, with just one eye closed! Such was the humor of the great man!

nemo Sun Aug 13, 2006 11:34 pm #14
I believe that it should be possible to make a coherence filter by using holographic film. Here is what I think, someone tell me if I'm off base.

As light travels, it varies it's electrical, (and magnetic), amplitude continuously. However for any given coherent beam, it does not do so with respect to any given point in space. If the beam's amp is zero when it hits the surface of a film, then as long as the frequency of the light isn't drifting, (ie. the laser is warming up), the amp at that poing should stay zero. X number of nanometers into the film though, the amp will be at a max. Developing the film, you should get something that I visualize as wayvy straw holes. Holding that developed filter up to the sun should allow any light that just happens to be the correct freq, and just happens to hit the film at the correct amp, and just happens to be traveling the correct direction, to slide thru.

Yes, those conditions will filter out most of the light, making the thing rather ineffecient, however when you think about how much energy you put into a laser, versus the amount of coherent light you get out, maybe it's not so bad.

When using the filter, you'd obviously want to get a lens somewhere before the filter, to get the light going the correct direction, (maybe to capture more, also). Then you'd want to get an etalon in the picture to get started with a known phase.

Making the filter should be as simple as shining a laser beam at a thick film holo plate. I suspect though that you would need a self developing film.

This filter, (if it is technically possible), should have two features desired by holographers. By plumping it up, or drying it out, it should be tunable, (although I question how easy that would be). It should also allow a brighter beam than your standard laser pointer.

walschuler Wed Aug 23, 2006 1:55 am #19
This is an interesting thread but I am somewhat surprised that the term coherence has not really entered into it. The Sun is neither spatially nor temporally coherent. It is fairly spatially coherent in that it is far enough away that its light is nearly parallel when it reaches us. But it subtends half a degree of arc, as BobH pointed out. A certain kind of amplitude interferograms can be made for distant stars, which are so far away that they ARE point sources for us. We occupy, in that case, a tiny part of the wavefront and one part is correlated with another. More than a hundred years ago Michelson used small mirrors mounted on a beam mounted on the Mt. Wilson 100 inch telescope to resolve in that way the disk of the bright star Betelgeuse, a red giant star of large diameter. Since then Hanbury-Brown and Twiss used a different technique to do further interferometric work. And others (Labyrie- sp. uncertain) have used speckle interferometry to get diameters and see giant sunspots based on the spatial coherence within the tiny cells of seeing caused by the Earth's atmospheric currents. In a laser, being in just a single transverse mode (TEM) guarantees spatial coherence. The usual choice is TEM 00 as it gives a well behaved gaussian beam cross section. In another part of the forum there is a discussion about realligning a HeNe laser. If the allignment is not perfect you can get other modes, such as TEM 01, with 2 horizontal spots, or TEM 10, with 2 vertical spots. This requires a larger bore tube than is now conventional, however. Current tubes are so narrow they cut off the higher order modes. If they did not, then there would be the possibility of transverse mode hops. That would not help holograms.

The second aspect, temporal coherence, is about spectral purity. The coherence length, a term you all know, relates the path length distance over which waves can still interfere to the bandwidth of the source. I tell my holography classes, to give them a feel for the extreme purity and concentration of laser power into its bandwidth the following: if you put a 10mW HeNe laser in a satellite orbiting the Sun at the distance of Mercury, it is so narrow band that if you covered your telescope with a filter of bandwidth equal to the laser and no more, then the Sun would be invisible - black - and the laser would show up as a red dot as it transited across the face of the Sun.

The formula for coherence length is: c.l.= (wvlngth squared)/(bandwidth)

and you have to be careful about units. For a typical visible wavelength in the yellow of 5500 angstroms (550nm, 5 x 10-5 cm) and a bandwidth of 3000 angstroms, about the bandwidth of the whole visible spectrum from 3500 to 6500 angstroms, the equation yields a coherence length of just about 1 micron (10-4cm, 10-3mm). This is a pretty disappointing result for holographers, but this micron-thick zone is the basis for Lippmann photography (see that part of the Forum) and is why interference can record color. For white light just the first micron or so is useful for Lippmann. Coventional holographic emulsions are about 6 times thicker, so the photons recorded more than 1 micron away from the mirror surface (or emulsion-air surface) are useless noise. This is also why you can get Lippmann images that show depth using a reflecting film in imperfect contact with the emulsion, if the light source is narrow band enough. I have recorded the ripples in the plastic film, adhered by alcohol and other fluids I have used in Lippmann experiments, with narrow band illumination from a conventional sodium safelight. In effect, these are mm-deep holograms. Sodium safelights radiate just the D-lines, which are only a few Angstroms in width and wavelength separation, so the coherence length is around a millimeter.

If one could arrange to equalize all path lengths to within that coherence length, broad band light could be used. For example, I think most of you realize that if in a conventional setup you assign the beam splitter and the film center as the foci of an ellipse, and set all your objects on the ellipse and make the reference beam path length equal to the object beam path length(s), since all objects lie on the ellipse all will have the same object beam path length. With only a micron of coherence length all these distances would have to be the same within that one micron. This also means no object with an interesting shape could be imaged, as the surface relief would surely be greater than one micron and only particular portions would be within the required path length difference. This severe requirement has not stopped me from thinking about the problem occasionally over the years. There may be a clever wavefront-splitting technique that would get one into the game. I think Ed's diffraction grating scheme is one of those, for a limited subject. Furthermore, spectrum dispersion ups the spectral purity at each location on the emulsion, so the coherence length is locally larger. That is why the spectrum is the easiest Lippmann test subject.

A filtered mercury vapor lamp was used by Gabor to do his 1946 proof-of-concept demo hologram of an essentially zero-depth transparency. With great care and perhaps a sodium lamp one might conceivably achieve one micron accuracy with multiple objects. There may be a norrower line lamp commercially available that would allow a few microns of depth. I would be happy to talk further about such a project.

This is a bit long, I know...Hope it is helpful.

There's yet another paper: Phillips/Martens, Aspects of the copying of holograms using incoherent light, SPIE 600, 1985. Some excerpts:

> SummaryWe have shown that effective contact copying of holographic surfaces can be achieved using our published method and some care in the choice of filtered natural light.Advances in emulsion technology have strongly suggested that this method of replication of holograms will become an important commercial technique in the future.The problem posed by the requirement of intimate contact of the copy and the master surface can be relieved by firstly cleanliness and secondly by removal of the pressure relief layer on the film.Our evidence to date is that the ability of filtered natural light to 'contact copy' Lippmann holograms or create Lippmann reflectors is just as good as that of the Sodium discharge. Exciting developments in silver halide emulsion technology make this area of study of great interest in the future. (p.126)All three-dimensionally encoded interference structures of the holographic type have common property. They have built in to them interference fringes which are sympathetic to both a signal wave and what we usually call a reference wave. (...)The sympathy of the fringes to two or more different types of wavefront can only be achieved if the relative phase of the wavefront in any locality in the recording medium is held constant. For this reason hologram can usually only be made in light of organised phase (coherent light). Any relaxation of the stringent control of coherence usually results in problems, except when say the simplistic Lippmann reflector is to be fabricated or perhaps when certain forms of optically simplistic white light interferometers are use to create fringes. (p.123)We have undertaken simple experiments to achieve the copying of both relief image holograms and Lippmann volume holograms using the light from a Sodium discharge source and subsequently light from a tungsten-halogen lamp with a high quality single spectral line Sodium filter placed in front of the lamp. (p.124)It was relatively easy to create nearly perfect Lippmann reflectances with Sodium discharge light. (...)The major area of problem lay with our ability to create good contact between the recording medium and the mirror behind it. We had established that mirror were best made of metallised mylar, but that dust or surface irregularities due to filler in the mylar were a major problem.Areas of poor contact displayes severe cosmetic fringe difficulties which were nowhere near as predominant in the case of Sodium discharge recording. (...)Contact copying of holograms certainly works with this methodology but if the master surface is that of a Lippmann hologram then of course we need to match wavelengths of recording light to the holographic structure in the master. (p.125)
