Well, both. The model for Rayleigh scattering is basically that the scattering center is a dipole. Also, the dipoles themselves need to be well seperated so that there is not the usual phase combinations in dense media causing forward propagation. Any driven oscillator at high frequencies oscillates at pi out of phase with the driving force. The blue of the sky will only be true at rare matter densities. Lower down in the atmosphere, the density of molecules is too thick for Rayleigh. However, Einstein and Smoluchowski (independantly) determined that the reason for blue skies lower down in the atmosphere was due to random statistical variations that caused random rarefication and so caused Rayleigh scattering. In gelatin, you have giant molecules in close quarters so that any dipoles, if they exist, will have many complex phase inter-relationships between each other. It's difficult to see how the w^4 relationship requirement of Rayleigh scattering can predominate. Due to the large size of the gelatin molecule I would rather expect Mie scattering. The fact that scattering in DCG seems predominantly white seems to strengthen this.

"Why should there not be dissolved gelatin in the processing solution?"

If "integrity" of the layer (e.g. layer thickness) is paramount for volume processes, we may care about how much bulk gelatin is lost on processing. General wisdom on

dichromated processes suggested a crosslinking/hardening/tanning action on colloids like

gelatin. So if Cr "hardening" additionally implied solution (at least of small

quantities of gelatin), there would be something wrong with that theory (I wouldn't be surprised though).

"Is it that "hardened" gelatin cannot dissolve?"

It depends on the extent it has been hardened/crosslinked/tanned previously. The more

it's hardened, the higher the water temperature (and, incidentally, the higher the pH) it will resist to.

Or, in other words: the more the gelatin has been hardened, the less it will be

swelled by water.

"... then surely the rate of dissolution from "hardened" gelatin must depend

on the level of hardening?"

Yes but there might be yet additional factors to play a role:

bloom strength and the gel's individual history (the mechanical strength of a gel might greatly differ if

you applied a 1% gelatin solution or some 20%).

"Have any experiments been conducted to determine such a ratio?"

[quote:6c4431b375="Colin Kaminski"][quote:6c4431b375]The holographic properties of bulk DCG, as in a volume hologram, are supposedly due to either bubbles... [/quote:6c4431b375]

Could this be the source of the Rayleigh scattering?[/quote:6c4431b375]

I don't think so. Rayleigh scattering specifically implies a forth power law. As you know, the amplitude of a forced oscillation is:

A = (F/m)/((w_0^2 - w_d^2)^2 + gamma^2*w_d^2)^0.5

where w_0 is the natural frequency

w_d is the driving frequency

gamma is the resistance or drag.

In optics, the E field of the incoming light causes the positive-negative centers in a simple atomic system to oscillate and so form an oscillating dipole. The oscillating, charged dipole then sends off em waves of it's own due to it's oscillations, these waves having an amplitude dependant on the amplitude of the dipole. If you apply the above equation to a dipole, you replace F by the Coulomb force due to charge seperation in the dipole and the w_d is the frequency of the incoming wave. You now get the amplitude of the scattered wave due to the dipole and you can see that there is an inverse w_d^4 dependance. In the above equation there is (( w_0^2 - w_d^2)^0.5) in the denominator but the intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude so the intensity of the dipole radiation is inversely dependant on (w_o^2-w_d^2)^2 or, if w_d, the incoming frequency of light, is very high, on w_d^4. Since lambda is inversely proportional to w, the amplitude of the scattered radiation is much greater in the blue end since the ratios of scattering for red and blue will now be:

I_blue/I_red = (w_red)^4/(w_blue)^4 = ( lambda_blue)^4/(lambda_red)^4

which is huge.

A bubble is not a dipole so it should not have this w_d^4 behaviour. Also, the above analysis depends on the fact that the scattered radiation is truly scattered. If the matter density is high, this scattered radiation will soon drive other nearby dipoles. Since there is a pi phase shift between driving frequency and driven frequency, you soon cancel out the two, interferometrically. Mie scattering also behaves like this for small particles but red-shifts and eventually white shifts as the particles ("bubble") size increases, so it's possible that the bubbles are a source of Mie scattering.
