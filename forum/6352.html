Tom B. Tue Jul 24, 2007 4:39 am #6
Yes, I see it on p. 99, 3rd edition. Definitely a misprint or mistake.

As he points out, holograms are pretty forgiving of exposure time errors, but a factor of 4 should be enough to see a difference.

To truly check exposure correctness you need to know how dark the film gets when developed for a given time (say 30 sec to 2 minutes. Most people use 2 minutes). For reflection holos, the density (a photographic measure of darkness) should be about 2.0, which corresponds to 1% light transmission. One useful comparison is a piece of film exposed to bright light and developed to give some idea of how dark the film can possibly get. You don't want to get this dark

I use a safelight to observe the film periodically while developing and stop when it's dark, but before it is totally opaque. You will want to check your safelight to see that it is really safe. One way to do this is to place some coins on a piece of unexposed film and place under the safelight, removing one coin every 15 seconds or so until only one is left, then develop the film. If there is any red leakage (or film sensitivity to non-red light) the non covered area will be darker and the coin shadows can be seen, giving some idea of how long the light is safe for.

If you are getting good density after development , increasing the exposure time does not help at all. Unless the exposure is badly wrong, the hologram brightness depends much more on the beam ratio than anything else. For Denisyuk holograms this means the object should be as bright as possible. If you want instant gratification, pearls (real or fake) on black velvet right next to the film work very nicely. Resting the plate on the object is OK (and very helpful for stability, too) as long as the object is soft or smooth enough that it won't scratch the emulsion.

floh Wed Jul 25, 2007 3:24 pm #12
You can have brightness in denisyuk as well...

important is the light ratio object/reference, it should be 1:1 what's never possible. 1:4 is ok and possible on bright (white) objects.

Split-beam makes you to get close to 1:1 if you are able to adjust the ratio. The negative side is that you need much more stability on your table.

For stability it its the best when your object touch the plate (or film between two glass plates). Try some white hard objects.

Find the exposure/development time where your film is as much black as possible but you are able to see your safe-light thru it. then bleach until complete clear (no overbleaching possible).

Colin Kaminski Thu Jul 26, 2007 4:03 am #14
Just taking some of the light around the film and illumination the object can brighten up the image. It takes careful carding to not get unwanted light hitting the film. The nice thing about this setup is it keeps the wide field of view of a Denisyuk.

More fun is H1 transmision to H2 reflection copies where one can place the film "inside" the object.

I have not tried any H1 reflection to H2 reflection copies. "Proximity Copies". This could be a useful trick but make sure to develop the film with a non-shrinking chemistry so as to keep the replay frequency as close to your laser as posible.
