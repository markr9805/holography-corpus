I have made two Coating Rods. One for thick coatings (80um) and one for thinner coatings (50um). When I use the one for thick coatings, I get a nice and uniform coating. The one for thinner coatings gives me a strange coating pattern, where almost no gelatin is left behind at the start (where it is pulled down the glass), and a lot at the other end of the glass. The distribution of the gelating looks like an inverted Guassian shape.

![Image](http://www.hologramhans.com/pictures/Coat.jpg)

I suspect that this is because more gelatin is left behind on the edges of the glass, which flows back to the middle after the coating rod has passed. I keep the plate exactly level during coating.

Does anyone else also have this problem, and how do you prevent this from happening? Do you wipe the edges clean after coating, or do professional Meyer bars not have this problem?
Joe Farina Sat Sep 23, 2006 2:00 pm #2
I haven't seen this specific effect, but I can offer a few comments on Mayer Bar coatings. Just after the bar is drawn across the glass, there will be puddles of gelatin on any area not contacted by the wires of the bar. These puddles will creep back to some extent. (If someone wanted to make a mess, then I suppose a plate could be flooded with gelatin, and a bar wider than the glass could be drawn across, spilling gelatin over all the edges. This might get messy and wasteful, but the coating would probably be quite uniform, since there would be no puddles to creep back towards the center. I've never done it this way, though.)

In my opinion the crucial factor in Mayer Bar coatings is the temperature of the glass (to be more specific, the "temperature history.") If you coat a room-temperature piece of glass, it will gell almost instantly when using a #24 bar, and there will likely be "Mayer Bar lines." If you warm the glass gently using a hair dryer, the uniformity will be much improved because the gelatin will have a little time to level out. But if the glass remains warm for too long (e.g. if it were resting on a hot metal plate) then those edge puddles will creep back, because the gelatin remains in the liquid state for a longer period (plus, dust will have an ample opportunity to settle on the coating).

So, ideally, the glass should be at the right temperature for the right period of time. That's why I built the water-cooled/water-heated coating plate. This is simply a piece of 1/4 aluminum plate that is held firmly against a coil of soft copper tubing, through which hot or cold water flows. I flow hot water through (to warm the glass on top of the plate) and as soon as the coating is made, I switch to cold water to gell the coating. This provides the right amount of warmth for the appropriate time period, and the puddles don't have much of a chance to creep back towards the center. (But I still use a large piece of glass, 14 X 14 inches or so, and cut off an inch or two of glass along all the edges.)

For bar coating with already cut plate here is how I do it.

![Image](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/CoatingTech.JPG)

I tape all cracks. If not the gel runs under the glass and makes a mess on the back of the plates. The entire thing is on a thin black plastic trash bag. I then pour a nice puddle at the top and quickly pull the bar down the plates applying a fair amount of pressure to the bar. The two plates at the top are only for the puddle and are not used for making holograms. I let the entire thing set for 2 hours. I then peel the tape off and the plates come out perfectly even coated and even have a nice border around them for o-ring sealing. This has to be the best method of coating I have done so far. I am doing 5x7's this way. It does waste a little not really that much. For the even coating of the entire plate it is worth it.

I the take the trash bag and soak it in some old used fixer which breaks down the CrVI to CrIII. The simply throw away.

Heating the plates is not necessary as long as the bar is pulled quickly after the puddle is poured. But heating the glass and bar is definitely better. I still want to implement the heating and cooling table but have not been able to get to it yet. I am quite positive that heating the plates and rod to a very stable temperature, the same temperature of the gelatin, the immeditatly cooling the plates from the bottom will see in dramatic increase in even coatings for thicker emulsions.

I also use this technique when I buy fresh glass. I only have to tape around the four edges (tape not shown).

![Image](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/ScoredPlate.jpg)
