Here's a paper I stumbled across this afternoon while looking for pictures of early lasers. It describes using Gabor type holography in two separated optical paths to provide a 3D view. Just wondering what Dinesh and others here think of it. I neglected to print off a copy, but the library where it is is only a few blocks away. While the paper was published in April 1963, it was received in May 1962 and the work done in October 1961, after Leith & Upatnieks did their off-axis work with binary 2D images but before they moved on to continuous tone imagery (when Upatnieks was in the service).

Dinesh Mon May 02, 2011 11:51 am #2
Hard to say from just the abstract. They charge for full paper. The abstract has some comments that seem a bit odd, considering the time period involved.

"This process is being used to visualize the molecular structure of matter"

- I don't think anyone in 1962 could "visualize the molecular structure of matter". If by that they mean that they could resolve features down to nanometer length scales as a hologram, they'd have had to use coherent X-rays which were not available in 1962. The Gabor paper specifically uses wavefront reconstruction as a technique of improving the resolving power of an electron microscope, which does resolve (very large) molecular structures. However, the Gabor technique could not have worked with actual e-beams and molecular structures in 1962, which is why he used his (very small) transparencies as a proof of concept. So, if the abstract meant, "This process could be used..." instead of "This processis being used...", then that would be more accurate. However, I get the feeling that the authors had not actually made any wavefront reconstructions of actual molecular structures; they simply assumed that the Gabor paper was actually being used as the paper suggested, ie that someone was actually making inline Gabor holograms of molecular structures.

"Three-dimensional reconstruction is possible by means of a synthesizer which has two optic axes. The light in one arm of the synthesizer need not be coherent with respect to the light in the other arm."

I don't know what they mean by "synthesizer" or "optic axes". If by "synthesizer which has two optic axes", they mean a system with two beams impinging on a photosensitive substrate, then it would imply the L & U technique. However, this a technique for construction, ie for recording, not for reconstruction. They specifically say that they're reconstructing with two axes. You don't need two beams to reconstruct an already constructed hologram. They also say that one arm need not be coherent with respect to the other. It's a little confusing to draw out the meaning here. Reconstructing a hologram does not need a coherent beam, although an incoherent beam, especially one in 1962, would have dispersed so badly that the image would be unviewable but still reconstructed. They may be talking about both recording (for which two mutually coherent beams are necessary) and reconstructing (for which one of the two beams may be used and not necessarily be coherent). In other words, they seem to be lumping both processes in one sentence. Without reading the original paper, we don't know if this was a poorly understood thought experiment in which they could not separate recording with reconstruction, or the abstract was vague.

The whole thing gives me an impression that they read Gabor's paper, thought that the Gabor technique was actually being applied to real molecular structures, and had the bright idea that by splitting the beams off-axis one could make 3D images of actual molecules. These images could then be reconstructed by a reconstruction beam that was not required to be coherent. I get the feeling that they did not actually do it though. As I say, it's hard to say without the full paper, but if I'm right then they had read the Gabor paper on wavefront reconstruction of molecular structures and realised that by splitting the beams, they could get actual images of real molecules but never actually did it. Since the first paper on split beam holography by L & U was received in June 1964, this paper may have preceeded the L & U paper but was missed because L & U actually made real objects whereas these authors talked of imaging molecules.
