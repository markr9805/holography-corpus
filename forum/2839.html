Hi,

I wanted to shift the replay of my reflection holo to a gold colour, so pre soaked with sugar. I used 5% on the right and 15% on the left hand side to see which concentration gave the best results (as this was first attempt at this).

The picture below shows how my holo appears after drying. Part of the 5% side has good looking green hologram, the rest of the 5% and all of the 15% side is black. Funny thing is, after about an hour of drying, a lot more of the holo was visible, on both the 15% and 5% sides.

![Image](http://www.geocities.com/rja4096/sugar1.jpg)

All except in the blue hatched area, was a visible holo when drying.

The procedure to pre soak was as follows:

1. Under safe light, vertically submerge half of a plate in 5% sugar solution (with a little photo-flow, at room temp) for 3 minutes.

2. Allow to dry for 2 hours in light proof box.

3. Under safe light again, submerge other half of plate vertically for same time (forgot to add photo-flow).

4. Allow to dry for number of hours until making exposure.

One thing I did notice, when in developing solution, the 15% side did not turn black as quickly as the 5% side, so I developed for 15 seconds instead of usual 10.

Please let me know if my thinking is correct, but my holos usually appear red, so based on ROYGBIV, next time I may try a 2% solution to shift to yellow.

I’m using PFG-03m plates with JD-4.

Any advice would be greatly appreciated, Thanks, Rick (in very rainy UK).

Tom B. Tue Oct 26, 2004 4:35 am #3
In your description you didn't mention squeegeeing the plate after soaking. I find this to be absolutely necessary with TEA swelling to get a uniform color shift. If the plate is dried vertically without squeegeeing, the excess liquid piles up at the bottom and creates excess color shift. I agree that it looks like the right side of the plate got shifted into the UV, and I expect this was the bottom side.

If squeegeeing the soft PFG-03 emulsion without damaging it is not possible, maybe spinning the plate at high speed would get rid of the excess liquid?

Tom B. Tue Oct 26, 2004 6:23 am #5
> rja4096: Forgive my novice, but is squeegeeing just wiping the plate (both sides?) with a damp cloth???Rick.

No - a squeegee is a wiper blade, usually made of rubber, for removing water from a surface. A fresh automobile windshield wiper blade (cut shorter and glued to a handle) should work. You only need to squeegee the emulsion side (the sticky side). The non-emulsion side should just be wiped off with a damp cloth to keep droplets of damp sugar-water from drying there.

A bit of practice is needed to avoid leaving streaks. Use smooth, even strokes, not pressing too hard, not too fast or slow. I usually use 2 or 3 strokes from different directions each time. You could practice in bright light with an exposed dud plate (repeatedly soaking and squeegeeing) to get a feel for the technique. Use extra photo-flow if you are having problems with the wiper sticking-slipping (squeaking). There were some past discussions on this forum about squeegee technique if you can find them.

MichaelH Wed Oct 27, 2004 6:52 pm #13
It seems to me that once you get past

1) Make sure your squeegee doesn't have nicks in the blade.

2) Make sure your squeegee is clean.

everything else is very subjective.

You've got:

1) Speed of the swipe across the film (for 4x6" film my swipe takes about 1 sec).

2) Angle of the blade to the film (about 45 deg off normal).

3) How much pressure to use (Firm but not too hard. See what I mean about subjective?)

4) To use one or more swipes across the film (one unless I need another ).

5) To use a wet or dry blade (dry).

6) The brand of squeegee (Hmm. Don't remember the brand off-hand but it's what Safeway carries).

Everyone's got a few duds lying around. My suggestion is to practice on them until you find the technique that works for you.

Some ideas suggested by the comments -

A simple jig for plate squeegeeing sounds like a great opportunity for inventive design. Right now, I just flop the plate down on a paper towel and go at it - it's always a bit awkward, but I had never thought about trying to improve the process. In addition, some mechanical arrangement such as the suggested rails that would keep the wiper absolutely level and help control the downward pressure would also help a lot. Now that I think about it, I suspect that if the blade is not level, it's liable to get nicked from going over the sharp glass edge and the pressure across the plate will be uneven. For practice, I think one could learn a lot from repeatedly wetting and squeegeeing a bleached plate (there's never a shortage of dud holos!) with good lighting.
