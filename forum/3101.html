Tommy Sat Feb 05, 2005 4:02 pm #4
Lasers can have more than one separate tube, in the same resonator. The plasma doesn't need to be contiguous. Imagine how a pulse laser has on resonator and then multiple amplifiers, or a gas laser with external optics (the resonator extends into the air). I read a description of a multi-tube HeNe laser in the Laser FAQ ([http://www.laserfaq.org/](http://www.laserfaq.org/)), but I don't remember where I saw it.

The tubes involved would need to have Brewster's windows (instead of mirrors) to reduce losses and avoid extra reflections. Removing the mirrors from a sealed tube, and keeping it sealed, (much less replacing them with brewster's windows) seems like quite a trick.

Dinesh's point about the location of the waist is important as well... the mirrors on a typical HeNe are curved, so the actual resonating region is shaped like an hour glass (if both are curved) or a triangle (they also use one curved and one flat mirror, so its as if the hour glass shape is folded over on itself). You would need to replace the mirrors with ones of a different focal length when you extend the resonator.

So, IMHO, if you have tubes with Brewster's windows it would be fabricating a opticly stable frame, replacing all the optics, and then a "simple matter of alignment"... The laser faq has descriptions of that procedure, to put it into perspective.

It would be a cool project, but I don't think it would result in a very powerful, or very stable, laser.

I vaguely remember seeing in a Scientific American article using a second un-mirrored gas laser tube as an amplifier for an oscillator tube with mirrors. This is quite a common configuration for solid-state lasers, viz. my article on JK pulsed Ruby lasers in the now-defunct holosphere, .pdf available on request.

I have seen an argon with two tubes inside of a common resonator at the Liconix factory (now defunct) circa '90. They had bought the assets of American Laser and were reviving their argon line. To have a higher power model to compete with the S-P 171 and Coherent 200 series they cascaded a pair of 5 Watt tubes. Each had their own power supply, but they were inside of a common cavity, utilizing one pair of mirrors and Brewster window terminated tubes. A bellows connected the two tubes to keep the windows clean.

Nick Phillips spoke of an S-P 471: a pair of 171 argon tubes cascaded in a common cavity. This was the world's record for high power Argon laser, maybe 100 Watts or so all-lines. This was when he worked for the Who at Holoco, circa late '70's early '80's. It was a monster that was hard to stabilize with a beam hard to look at.

Having seen that Scientific American laser article I had always thought it would be a great idea to use something like the S-P 117 single frequency stabilized He-Ne as a seed laser injecting into a 125 50 mW tube. This way the output power would be high and coherence would be extra long.

But practically, would there be enough gain with a single pass through a glowing He-Ne tube? The atoms are all revved up and raring to go, so a nice beam shining through them should be able to pick up a few photons more. But then again, the typical He-Ne output coupler leaks out only about 3% of the intra-cavity power, which kinda implies low gain.

It would be interesting to see what happens with the beam of one laser going through the tube of a second with the mirrors off. This would be the simplest way to gain power, instead of coupling two resonators together. The biggest problem would be in making sure the divergence of the first laser doesn't let the beam bounce around the walls of the second tube.

The onus is on you, dear friend to see if this noble experiment will work.

Joe Farina Mon Feb 07, 2005 3:55 pm #8
An alternate method for using two lasers of the same wavelength for holography would be to phase-lock them. This was described by McGrew in Proceedings of the International Symposium on Display Holography, Volume 1 (1982). The paper was called "An inexpensive fringe stabilizer for long exposure holography" (pages 189-193). The fringe-locker is needed to phase-lock the two lasers, and McGrew describes building one (without providing a schematic or disclosing the actual electronics used, however). The one I built was based on a number of sources, including McGrew's paper. I think the schematic is still on Colin's website.

John, to get that paper I had to buy the entire volume from Lake Forest College. I don't send out papers because it takes up too much of my time. Volume 1 was well worth the price, but I can't remember what I paid, because it was quite a few years ago. The person who sold it to me was Virgina Crist (at Lake Forest College).

I will quote two paragraphs, though.

"Another very interesting and potentially widely useful trick is to phase-lock two lases together so that they are mutually coherent. If two lasers are phase-locked, one may be used for illuminating the object and the other may be used for the reference. Phase-locking is accomplished by mounting a laser's high-reflector mirror on the speaker of our system (he is referring to the fringe-locker) so that the mirror motion controls the laser's output frequency. The output of the laser and the output of a second laser are combined with a beamsplitter to form an interference pattern which is sampled by the optical fiber ends. (He is referring to the sensors for his fringe-locker, since he used fibers to feed the light back to his phototransistors. In my fringe-locker I simply used photodiodes.) The interference pattern will be continuously changing: the fringes will race past the fiber ends at a rate corresponding to the frequency difference between the two lasers.

If one of the lasers is carefully adjusted so that frequencies are matched, the interference fringes will momentarily stop moving. The fringe stabilizer will take over, locking the frequencies of the two lasers together so that any slow frequency drifts in one or the other of the two lasers will be compensated for and the two lasers will continue to generate exactly the same frequency of light."

You stabilise the shinola out of one of the lasers and then you match the other one. A sort of bootstrap operation . One laser forms a "reference" source that you use to control the other. Eventually, you hope, the matched laser will allow the other laser to "lock" on and phase match.If the reference laser drifts then you're chasing your own tail, hence one laser needs to be pretty stable. We tried this technique about 20 odd years ago and it didn't work very well. The coherence length of the two lasers was, if memory serves, less than the coherence of either one alone. It was also extremely difficult to maintain the phase matching. There have been some papers on it which I think I have.

John, your diagram looks O.K. to me, but I have never attempted to phase-lock lasers myself. There is a diagram on page 91 of the 7th edition of Holography Marketplace showing the arrangement, and it's slightly different from yours.

After thinking about it, though, if you need a lot of HeNe power, I would opt for a SP-127, and forget about the phase-locking scheme. Sam Goldwasser has a nice one that he says is doing 39mW, which is quite a high figure for this laser. He's asking $2,500 for it, although I don't know how firm that price is. He may also be willing to trade, since he has a wide interest in lasers. This is assuming you don't want to go through the hassle and expense of a SP-125. An etalon for this laser is also very difficult to find. Also assuming you don't want the Russian or Japanese equivalents of this laser, which are very rare indeed. I wouldn't go for the OEM eqivalents of the SP-127 (the 107 or 907) because, even though they're cheaper and more common on the surplus market, they are typically gassed for power, not coherence. These are known as "multiple isotope" lasers and have substantially less coherence length than a single isotope laser, which the SP-127 often is. The high-power Siemens HeNe laser is also typically gassed for power, not coherence, since most customers simply need a bright red light, and coherence is not a factor for them.
