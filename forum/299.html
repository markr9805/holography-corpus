Dinesh Sun Aug 21, 2011 5:43 pm #14
The "tone" in a hologram is not the same as the "tone" in a photograph. I didn't really know what you photographers call a "zone system", but it seems to refer to the ability of a photographic emulsion to capture tonal variations - grey scale variations - in a negative emulsion and to transfer that same grey scale faithfully to a positive print.

In holography, the variations in grey scale on an image is dependant on the variation in ratio between various parts of an image and on the polarisation or depolarisation of the scattered light from the object.

The efficency of an image, or parts of an image, is proportional to the fringe modulation and integrity, which is itself proportional to the gamma of the film (and the developer). The fringe is sinusoidal, so perfect capture of the sinusoidal fringes would require a smooth capture of the sinusoidal variation from the peak of the sinusoid to the trough. If the emulsion cannot capture this range, the fringe is not captured sinusoidally and there is loss of linearity. This would mean that additional Fourier components are generated which would result in noise (from the spurious low frequency Fourier components).

If there is perfect sinusoidal capture, then the fringe modulation is dependent on the ratio hitting the emulsion at a specific point. If, at that point, the ratio is lower than at a neighbouring point because of a decrease in reflectivity from the object to that point, then the fringe modulation is also lower and so the efficiency is also lower. Thus that specific point is seen as dimmer than the surrounding point and will lead to an apparent tonal difference. So let's assume that the film and the processing has resulted in a perfect sinusoidal response all over the emulsion. Then, given a point X on the emulsion and a point Y near it on the emulsion, the light from the object reflects to produce a higher intensity at X than at Y. This would give a higher modulation ( a higher peak of the sinusoid) at X than at Y. When reconstructed, assuming perfect reconstruction with no aberrations, the light reconstructed from X would be brighter than the light reconstructed from Y. If X and Y were near each other and recorded from the same single object, then tonal variations would show between X and Y.

One other factor that may effect this is the polarisation at X and Y. Given that the reference is linearly polarised and that the object is diffuse (and thus the reflected light is depolarised), then the angle between the polarisation angle of the light from the object to the point X and the polarisation angle of the reference may be smaller than the same angle at Y. This would result in a higher modulation at X than at Y. So if the polarisation angle of the reference beam is horizontal, the polarisation angle of the reflected light from the object to the point X is 10 degrees from the horizontal and the same angle is 20 degrees to the point Y, then the fringe modulation at X would be greater (by cos(20)/cos(10) ) than at Y. This would make the object seem to show tonal variation , despite it showing no such variation when illuminated by white light.

All of this, of course, refers to monocoloured holography. Coloured holography has the same basic idea, but there are now three sets of fringes, so there is "inter-fringe interactions" to take into account resulting in, among other things, cross talk.

Dinesh Mon Aug 22, 2011 8:23 am #16
OK, I see. The debate is about whether or not you can control contrast by development alone. I assume that the camera/film system in your three identical photographs are also identical, so the image capture - the negative latent image - is also identical, and the lighting is also identical.

In this case, I have to say that development alone will not change contrast in a hologram. If you made three identical holograms with exactly the same film, lighting and exposure and then developed them differently, say you underdeveloped one, developed one perfectly and overdeveloped one, then only difference would be brightness. You could make the argument that the overdeveloped hologram would be non-linear, that is the fringes were not sinusoidal. This would add noise and so create a loss in contrast. But, I assume that you want to control the contrast and not have spurious uncontrollable effects. In a hologram, you can control the contrast in the fringes by exactly the same methods you'd use to control the contrast in your three photographs, since a hologram is a photograph of the fringes anyway. . But, in a hologram, the change in contrast would result in a change in modulation and so a change in brightness. In a hologram, you're looking at an image created by diffraction from the fringes, not the fringes themselves. In a photograph, you're looking at the "fringes" themselves.

Ed Wesly Mon Aug 22, 2011 9:58 am #22
Zone system in a nutshell: Expose for the shadows, develop for the highlights.

There is a thorny problem in photography, that being the negative is a transmissive element, with light passing through it once, while the emulsion in a paper print does double duty, with light passing through it once, bouncing off the paper base, and then a second trip through the coating. The paper cannot contain the same tonal range as the negative, so some adjustment is necessary. Adams carried this technique to its ultimate refinement through calibration of all systems involved in the process.

The problem of a zone approach to holographic recording is that the holographic emulsions do not respond to a change in development time with a change in gamma, or contrast. They are like photographic papers, with differing development time’s plotted H & D curves parallel to each other. Speed is shifted, but contrast isn’t, which is the big tool that makes the zone system work for photographic negatives. Photographic papers are manufactured with different contrast built in to them.
