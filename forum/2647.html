I haven't done it as explicitly as you're showing here but I do have one hologram where I used this effect deliberately.

Rather than lighting my H2 evenly I created a hot spot in the center so that the hologram changes gradually from green in the center to red on the edges.
Kaveh Wed Aug 04, 2004 1:09 pm #3
Only problem with this is that your colors are related to the areas of the hologram, not parts of the object. So if you move your head, an object might change color as you are seeing it from a different part of the hologram. But if that is the effect you want, it's an interesting idea.
Dinesh Wed Aug 04, 2004 1:59 pm #4
To a limited extent, yes. It also depends on the medium and the processing scheme.

In a reflection hologram, the hologram consists of a set of planes within the emulsion. These planes are not uniform, but bend and twist depending on the object. Think of four people holding the corners of a large bedsheet in a strong wind. The reconstruction color of the hologram depends on the distance between the planes. Now, when you shoot a hologram, the planes are set at half the wavelength of your laser, more or less. Since the planes are bent and twisted, this half wavelength is approximate at different parts of the plate, but is an average figure. So if you shoot with a HeNe at 633nm, the planes are seperated by about 316nm.

When you process a hologram, or, more particularly, when you wet process a hologram, the various solutions in the development scheme will get into the gelatin. This will cause the gelatin thickness to alter and either create a greater seperation between the planes or a smaller one. This change in seperation will cause a color shift to a color of twice the new plane seperation. So if you start with a HeNe the plane seperation is 315nm, then, let's say, your processing shrinks the emulsion by 20% to 248nm. Now your reconstruction lambda is 2*248 or 496 - blue/green. Usually there is a downshift (from red to blue) due to collapse of the emulsion rather than an upshift into IR due to emulsion expansion. This is because if the processing involves fixing, the fixed-out Silver Halide leaves 'holes' behind which collapse thus collapsing the entire emulsion. In general, when the emulsion is wet processed, it undergoes various swelling and shrinking along the way and usually ends up thinner than when it started causing holograms shot in red to appear green. All of this is pretty dependant on the processing scheme you use. In fact, there is a large body of developer research on how to develop without swelling - since that's what more people are interested in.

So how does exposure affect all this? Partly by causing more grains to be developed and so creating more "holes" in the emulsion where there were Silver Halide grains and so shrinking more. Also, partly by stiffening the gelatin and so the gelatin does not swell as much but shrinks more during development. You have to overexpose by quite a bit and it is very dependant on various factors like the the type of developer, the temp of the developer, the pH of the developer, the type, temp and pH of bleach etc. All things being equal, overexposing a particular part of the plate will cause a color shift, but it's not very controllable. You won't get the nice RGB your picture shows. Also, as Kaveh says, the color will shift with angle of view if your image is deep.

There are various techniques of color control:

There is a technique, called pseudocolor, where you deliberately expand the emulsion before shooting with some chemical (usually tri-ethanolamine - TEA). Now the plane seperation is 316nm when shot, but since the emulsion is already expanded, when processed it collapses "more" than it should and you can get pretty good color control. If you use HeNe and process with pyrogallol or CWC2 then a 2% TEA wash for about a couple of minutes will take you to green. There is an artist from Portugal, I wish I could remember her name, who "paints with TEA and so causes different parts of the plate to come out different colors. She dips her finger in TEA and "finger paints" on the unexposed emulsion. Then when processed the hologram comes out in a different color where she "finger painted". Since her images are pretty shallow, the color maintains when you shift your angle of view.

In a single beam Denisyuk the color varies with the angle of the refenece beam. Because the of the very wide angle of view, you can twist the hologram a long way under the reconstruction source. After a while, the plane seperation increases relative to the reconstruction source because you're coming in at larger and larger angles. The reconstruction source "sees" a larger seperation and so the hologram shifts.

In DCG, the hardness of the emulsion is directly dependant on the exposure - much more so than in Silver Halide. So in DCG overexposure can easily take you into the UV.
