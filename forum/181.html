Dinesh Tue Feb 22, 2011 3:54 pm #3
You don't mention which JD, 2 or 4. Also, without pictures, it's not easy to make out the results. However:

> holomark: All plates have some milkiness

Possibly the plates are soft, possibly underexposed. You could try latensification, or longer intermediary wash (below)

> holomark: Development - all appeared to turn dark at approximately 15 sec (removed from solution 15 - 20 sec)

Generally, just "dark" is not enough. It needs to get to an OD of about 2 for reflection with VRP. The best way I can think of this is that when you look at your safelight through the plate, it should be barely visible, (OD of 2 means that it blocks 99% of light, leaving only 1%). It should begin to go dark at the 10 - 15 second point, after which you keep developing and occasionally looking at the safelight through the (developing) plate. There are two basic techniques: you either develop for a fixed time, usually 2 minutes, and check the OD; if it's too light, increase exposure, or you keep checking the OD by looking at the safelight and when it looks like about 1% of the safelight is all that's visible, you throw it into the wash.

> holomark: rinseall turned clear at approx 10 sec (removed from solution approx 15 sec)

By "rinse", you appear to mean "bleach". That is, you don't seem to be doing the intermediary wash. You need to wash it about 2 minutes in water before the bleach. Not doing this may be causing the milkiness. If the bleach took only 10 seconds to clear, it may be too light. Develop longer, it needs to get to an OD of 2 in about two and a half to three minutes. When you bleach, don't take it out of the water immediately. Leave it in there for about 30 more seconds.

> holomark: metal puzzles are kind of visable (object too dark?

More than likely, specular reflection. Metal objects are specular reflectors, that is, they reflect like mirrors. This mean that most of the light is reflected along a narrow range of angles and only a small part of the plate "sees" the object beam. However, almost all the light that reflects from the metal object is concentrated on the small part of the plate, which means you get a hu-u-uge relative ratio at that part of the plate and zilch everywhere else. The object beam should uniformly and diffusely illuminate the whole plate.

Hiya holomark!

Please bear in mind that I am still "but an egg" on matters holographic, but let's figure that for OK in this case; I've been there and recently enough to recall just how unnerving your first several plate developments will be and how easy it can be to misinterpret what you are seeing.

IMHO a darkening plate or film in developer is likely to look a lot darker than it actually is. Everything's trippy and surreal under safelight and it's difficult to tell exactly what you're seeing. Your best bet (as mentioned already) is to pull your plate from the developer when you reckon it's dark enough, give it a quick wash in your distilled water to avoid the risk of it continuing to develop in your hands, and hold it up to look at your safelight through the darkened film. I imagine that at this point you will discover that your film hasn't gone nearly as dark as it looks to have while in the developing tray. Film exposed heavily-enough and developed properly for reflection work should be nearly opaque. Transmission holos at this stage will be a nice dark gray. Feel free to return your film or plate to the developer if it hasn't satisfied your darkness demands. It may or may not darken further. If it doesn't, you're underexposed and will never darken fully. If you are able to eek out a little more developed silver, then try looking into some longer developing times and see if you can bump up that bleaching time a bit - it really should take longer for a plate to bleach clear than it sounds like it is there with your current regime.

Oh and - those lines perpendicular to the light source - sounds like maybe you're getting light entering the edge of your plate. Try carefully carding off the light in such a way that you are casting a shadow on the edge of the plate.

Good luck. Keep pluggin' away at it.

Dinesh Thu Mar 03, 2011 1:10 pm #7
> holomark: What happens if I leave in developer too long????

The whole thing goes black!

The way this works is that every grain of silver halide - one grain being a large collection of silver halide molecules - in the emulsion has a few so-called "specks" of black, native silver, that is, metallic silver which is black at these quantities. The exposed grains have a few more specks than the unexposed grains. The developer seeks out those grains with a few more black specks on them and converts the whole grain to black silver. A typical grain may have hundreds of thousands of silver atoms in it, so the three or four silver specks have converted to several hundred thousand silver specks. Once the developer has developed those grains, it continues to seek more grains with specks and starts to develop those. If the emulsion is still in the developer, then eventually every grain will develop because every grain has one or more silver specks. The entire emulsion will go black.

> holomark: I was guessing that becuase of the poor beam expansion the "bright" spot was overexposed and the remainder was almost properly exposed.

The ratio might also have something to do with it. If your ratio is too high, it's the object beam that's too hot, not the ref.
