This all depends on what kind of polymer we're using; not all of our holograms are made in gelatin, in fact, most of them aren't. We use a range of gels, including polyacrylamide, poly hema, and various others that are essentially non degradable. Some of them we've autoclaved, some we've fed to active microbial cultures, and they keep coming back. I've got some holograms that I've used in experiments over, and over, and over again and they're as good now as they were when I made them.

The complex part of this work lies getting a gel that gives a response to a specific analyte; if you want more information on that then a good place to start is with the website of the company through which this company is being exploited, Smart Holograms:

[http://www.smartholograms.com/site/sections/default.asp](http://www.smartholograms.com/site/sections/default.asp)

The gelatin ones will degrade if they're kept in a medium in which microbial action can take place; Rogers web site (the one linked to above) gives a good example of one in which that isn't an issue (in a whisky bottle). Gelatin is a marvellous material for making bright, pretty holograms, but it's perhaps more difficult to engineer into a smart gel for our type of work.

That depends on what it's a hologram sensitive to; typically, they're sensitive over a range of concentrations (say, a continual colour change over a couple of pH units or over a concentration range of an ion). We can certainly put an alcohol sensor in a methanol/water mix and follow the change as the alcohol evaporates out.

> This would be of immense benfit to DCG holographers who could easily determine the varying concentrations of an alcohol bath as the number of plates being processed increased, or the increase in concentration by evaporation of the alcohol when left overnight. I imagine it would be pretty useful for brewing as well. No more bulky glass tubes to determine specific gravity. When the Smart Hologram changes color, the beer's ready!

The alcohol sensor is a really simple one; the ones we've give a continual colour change over a range of pH units. We've got some embodiments of the technology that, regrettably, I can't talk about right now which do just what you're asking; what we're in the process of doing right now is taking that technology and getting it put into real instruments. It's my hope that you'll be able to stick one on a bit of glassware and it'll tell you all about what's happening in the liquid mix; we've got a range of sensor holograms that'll do that, and we're in negotiations to see who there is out there who can make best use of them. I might have mentioned already somewhere here that I've used one of our alcohol sensors to follow ethanol production by yeast, and we've done the same with lactate being produced by lactobacillus (you can monitor all manner of microbial and food processes) but they're only some of our potential applications. For more info, have a look at the Smart Holograms web site.

Getting the cross linking density right is a crucial part in the development of any of our sensors. Make it too soft and it goes shooting off out of the useful wavelength range, make it too hard and nothing happens; somewhere inbetween the two there's normally a range that gives us a linear response in the range of analyte concentrations we need.
