Dinesh Sun Jan 30, 2005 2:08 am #8
It's very difficult to buy all the equipment to make your own embossed holograms. Most of the process is not holography but printing techniques. First, you need to "master" the holographic image. This is usually the "H1", or several H1's of the original artwork. This process is called "origination". The H1 is then re-imaged (copied) onto a substance known as "photoresist". This is a liquid that is commercially availbale from, say, Microposit. This needs to be spin coated on to a glass substrate so as to create a layer of about 1 micron. For this you need a high speed spinner at about 1000 rpm. This glass substrate with resist is then left to dry and the hologram is exposed with a laser. You can buy ready coated plates form Towne Technologies ( [http://www.townetech.com/](http://www.townetech.com/) ). Since photoresist is only sensitive to extremely short wave light (roughly above 457nm - deep blue), you need a laser capable of these wavelengths. Most industrial holography companies use a He-Cd or Argon, although diode lasers in these wavelengths are now available. Once the hologram has been exposed on a photoresist plate, it is spray coated with a silver solution. This is known as the "Silver Mother". This silver mother is then placed in a bath of Nickel salts and electroformed in a Nickel solution. The current and voltage are carefully controlled so as to create an electroformed layer of a micron or so. This is known as the "Nickel Mother". The silver mother is destroyed in the process. At this point you can peel off the Nickel mother and use that as a shim in a printing press to press into plastic or paper. However, it will quickly wear out. To ensure that future generations can be made, the original Nickel mother is then redunked into the Nickel bath and re-electroformed. The new electroformed surface is the Nickel daughter. This is now used a shim in a printing press so that the Nickel Mother can be re-used to create new Nickel daughters when one wears out. Generally, holographers carry out the first two part of the process - the mastering and the recording of the hologram on a Photoresist plate - and then ship the plate out to another company for electroforming and creating the various Silver and Nickel generations. As you may know from the printing industry, this doesn't make a lot of sense unless you want to make thousands of holograms. Some companies will make "small" runs of a few thousand holograms, but usually, most companies are not interested unless you want about 10,000 or more. James River ( [http://www.jrpusa.com/en/holography.htm](http://www.jrpusa.com/en/holography.htm) ) is a good source for a small run. I believe Colin has a source also for small runs. These are some of the companies making mass production embossed holograms:

[http://www.abnh.com/](http://www.abnh.com/)

[http://www.aotgroup.com/](http://www.aotgroup.com/)

[http://www.cfcintl.com/productLine.asp?productLineID=3](http://www.cfcintl.com/productLine.asp?productLineID=3)

Small holographic studios can make master the hologram from your artwork and shop it out to various holographic printers. We, in fact, can originate the image here at our studios and then ship it out to holographic printers.

If you want to make holograms as a hobbyist, most people on this forum can probably make several recommendations. First, you need to decide what kind of medium you want to work in. Silver halide is usually a good way of starting since it's relatively easy and cheap. Integraf (link at top of page) makes a good beginner kit with everything you'll need. Integraf also provide all the film and processing chemicals along with a booklet explaining the process. After that, some people make a more complicated process called "DCG" or "dichromated gelatin". This is a more duifficult technique involving more process control. You need to make your own films. This can also become expensive. (I'd recopmmend starting with Silver Halide to get a feel for holograms first. DCG is "easy" to those who've spent a lot of time playing with all the parameters, but it's not an easy material to start if you've never made a hologram).

Dinesh Mon Jan 31, 2005 9:00 pm #12
"2D 3D" referes to a method invented/discovered by Steve McGrew. You can see the parent (along with others) here: [http://www.nli-ltd.com/patents/main.htm](http://www.nli-ltd.com/patents/main.htm) .

Basically the technique involves taking sets of 2D images and image planing them at different levels to create a set of planes of 2D imagery in a 3D volume. . So that one image layer, let's say a photograph, is image plane so as to be 1 in behind the plate and another flat image layer, let's say of a sheet of paper is imaged at 1/2 in. Now with relatively inexpensive artwork, you get the impression of someone reading a document. A further advantage is that the two or more layers can be different colours so that each layer stands out from the others.There's a bit of issue dealing with the whole patent problem. Apparently Steve Mcgrew still holds the patent. I'm not too clear on this aspect, but at ABNH, where worked, I believe we had to licence the technique from him. I could be wrong about this. I also seem to remember something about the patent going out of date here in the US, but still valid in Europe.

"Dot Matrx" is atechnique of transferring a computer generated image onto a resist plate. The technique reproduces the tonal and colour variations of the original image, but not very faithfully. It depends a lot on the reconstruction. Basically, a photoshop image is fed into the dot matrix machine. The one I worked on in the mid 90's captured the image pixel by pixel but it was one of the very first ones and I had the (extremely difficult!) task of beta testing it. Nowadays, I believe the entire image is captured. Anyway, the macjhine itself consists of a laser beam that goes down a tube. At the end of the tube is a small beamsplitter and a mirror. So at the end of the tube, the beam is sperated and part of it hits a mirror which re-directs it downwards so that both the original beam and the split beam meet at a resist plate. Of course, the position of the plate is very important because if it's too high or low, the beams will not superimpose. The beamsplitter and mirror are attached together and scan the plate side to side and down the length of the plate. The plate itself is placed on a travelling stage, so that the plate moves, not the tube. The trrick is that the tube rotates at an angle depending n the colour of the pixel from the Photoshop image. So a red pixel (255, 0, 0 in RGB) may cause the tube to be at zero degrees, while a pixel value 10, 50, 255 may cause the tube to be twisted by 10 degrees. The beamsplitter/mirror comination is determined by the RGHB value of the pixel. As the tube twists, so does the plane of the two beams, since the beamsplitter and mirror are in a fixed plane. Thus every pixel value in the photoshop image is translated into a set of fringes at a different orientaion to the reconstruction source and so will reconstruct at a different tonal and colour. A Dot matrixc is necessarily flat on the plate and the image has no depth. I understand that several groups have tried to make a "3D" dot matrix (I have ideas myself on how this can be accomplished) but so far no one has suceeded to the best of my knowledge. The great advantage of a dot matrix machine is that it's pretty much idiot proof. You simply create an image in photoshop, place a plate in the travelling base, connect machine to computer and press "Start"! This makes it an atrtrractive way to get holographic imagery without the problems of a lab and hololgraphers. Rob Mundy of Spatial Imaging makes and sells dot matrix machines ( [http://www.holograms.co.uk/technology/lightgatev4.html](http://www.holograms.co.uk/technology/lightgatev4.html) ), as does Ahead ( [http://www.findarticles.com/p/articles/ ... i_n7848813](http://www.findarticles.com/p/articles/mi_hb3163/is_200305/ai_n7848813) ). Either of these companies will be happy to install and train in it's use. (Rob Mundy is an old frioend of mine so go to Rob! )

As for making a mastering facility of your own. It's tricky. Basically you need a vibration isolation table, a laser in the deep blue, lots of optical equipment, lots of mechanical equipment, a clean room to coat plates (you can get them from Towne, but this could get expensive if you're going to be doing this on a regular basis) and an experienced holographer. I'd stress that the holographer needs to know what he's doing. Making resist plates for mastering iis very different from hobby holography. You need to "think backwards" right through the Nickel stage so that the recording is very, very clean, very linear and the fringes can't get to deep or the Nickel mother won't come off cleanly.

I thought I would add that the photo resist needs to be processed prior to the silverizing.

Not only the current and voltage need to be carefully controlled during the nickel plating but so does the agitation, concentration of nickel, ph, temperature and time in the bath. You also vary these parameters to obtain different tensile strengths for the Mother vs. the child shims.

Fixturing of the Silvered resist is also very important for conductivity and uniform nickel growth as is the fixturing of the mother shim.

The Mother shim is grown to much thicker then 1 micron.

Also, if you design your master so that the image is otho after the stamping with just a mother, it will be pseudo if you use child shims as the child is a mirror of the mother. This needs to be thought out in planning. You do not want your final stamped image to be bottom lit with letters backwards.
