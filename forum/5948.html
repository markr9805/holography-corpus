Ed Wesly Thu Dec 28, 2006 10:47 pm #4
I christened this step the "Reddeveloper" because of the color of the final holographic emulsion. This step promotes the formation of colloidal silver, which is not transparent like the silver bromide that it is made from.

Whenever I would ask the question of "why is the colloidal silver red" of Hans Bjelkhagen or Nick Phillips they would always reply that it was because of the size and shape of the developed grains, as opposed as the wild and wooly filamentary strands of the final product of grains developed conventionally into black silver.

the first I had heard of anything like this step was from our own Jeff Blyth, who wrote an article in the long defunct Wavefront magazine. He used Sodium Borohydride, a silver halide reducer (reducers act like developers in that they liberate the halogen from the silver halide crystal, except that they ain't smart enough to tell the difference between the ones that saw the light and those that didn't like developers do!)

The darkening of the background cut down the scatter so the signal to noise ratio gets bumped up. The grain sizes in the Agfa 8E and PFG-01 give a blue haze to the emulsion thanks to Rayleigh scatter, which also gives us the blue of the sky. The reddeveloper either lowers the refractive index so that there is not a huge modualtion difference and cuts down the scatter; or it simply absorbs light to mask the scatter. Or maybe it takes the silver halide grains and reduces them into tiny chunks, preserving the fringe pattern but at the same time reduces scatter by making the sizes of the particles much smaller than the wavelength of light in the Rayleigh scatter equation.

This doen's seem to be too far-fetched to me. I had lunch once with Denisyuk, TJ and Hans Bj and Yuri started talking about how he wanted to coat holographic emulsions with T-Max style grains, (T for Tablet, thin silver halide crystals like in Kodak photgraphic film), nanometers thick but microns wide, parallel to the substrate.

Everyone else didn't think that this was wise idea, as conventional wisdom dictates that once a crystal gathers enough photons to be developed, the whole crystal explodes into one big blackened grain. So the integrity of the fringe pattern would be messed up.

But Denisyuk's line of thought was that there would be a lot of surface area to gather light, but the whole grain wouldn't develop, just the parts of it that had the developable speck, thanks to the use of colloidal developers! Whether or not anyone ever ran experiments to prove the concept, I don't know, but it was kind of an eye-opener for me to hear that colloidal developers were site specific.

You can see this color of colloidal silver on Slavich PFG-03M plates when developed in their recommended developer. If you develop a plate in CWC2 (aka JD-2) with the room lights on you will also see this color appear as the developer first starts growing the grains, then it turns black.

Another example of the colloidal silver in use is the peculiar color of raw color photographic film. You will notice varying shades of orange on the light-sensitive emulsion side when you load 35 mm film stock, and this is caused not by the dyes but by the Carey Lea layer, named after the gentleman in the 19th century who did the fundamental research on the formation of colloidal silver.

This layer is under the blue sensitive layer, and it blocks blue light from exposing the green and red layers under it. (silver halides have natural sensitivity to blue, so it must be filtered from the green and red layers which naturally see blue but have sensitizing dyes to allow them to see their part of the spectrum.) It gets blixed out (color photographic process have a rehalogenating bleach followed by a fixer to clear all silver out of the emulsion) with the rest of the developed silver in color negative and positive films.

Why the Reddeveloper is so good is that it takes away that "Underwater" look in the hologram, where what should be a black shadow area is filled in with the milky haze of scatter. It's great for red holograms, but I have seen the brightness go away with green shifted ones.

Whatever is the mechanism, it certainly helps the contrast of the final image!

> Ed Wesly: ... or it simply absorbs light to mask the scatter.

Yes, I think it basically works like an absorption filter. Besides rivaling with Russian ("Soviet") methods, which involve colloidal development, the old Phillips paper (“Bridging the gap between Soviet and Western holography”) mentions the replacement of the nasty pyrogallol developer as one of its benefits.

So I guess the ascorbic acid post-treatment (by the way, a great many common photographic developers can be used equally, if sufficiently diluted) mainly acts on short wavelength absorption as would also:

- developer stain (from pyrogallol/pyrocatechol development),

- staining bleaches like PBU-amidol or ferric nitrate,

- adding certain dyes to the processed emulsion.

Martin Fri Dec 29, 2006 6:13 pm #7
> MichaelH: The question that's raised by the behavior of the alcohol treatment is if the ascorbic acid is actually changing the silver or if it's just staining the gelatin.

I don't think the colloidal layer is kind of stain that could be removed by alcohol.

I would have looked for an explanation like the one given by Tom (some residual trace of oxidizer within the emulsion).

> If it's the latter, then I can't see why the ascorbate treatment would have any affect to prevent print-out other than to make it more difficult to detect when print-out is occurring by pre-darkening the emulsion.

I think colloidal silver really is very stable compared to silver halide grains. The ascorbate at pH3 being an extremely weak developer, reduces a tiny part only of all the silver halide grains within the emulsion, forming some metal colloidal silver.

Print-out of an ascorbate (“post”) treated emulsion will take place on the silver halide grains and NOT on the colloidal silver particles.
