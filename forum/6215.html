Joe Farina Sun Apr 29, 2007 3:08 pm #1
I bought a fluorescent bulb the other day, and I found out a couple of interesting things. After seeing that Thornton (who is referenced by many writers on color holography) was part of the fluorescent lamp division of Westinghouse, I picked up a Westinghouse "full spectrum" bulb, and there was some interesting information on the label.

Surprisingly, they even included the spectral curve. Three peaks were shown, at about 435, 540, and 610nm. I did some web searching for this graph (so that I could post a link) but couldn't find one. There are two minor peaks, at about 410 and 360, but these appear to be insignificant, and I don't think they contribute to the color-rendering capacity of the light bulb.

It was interesting to see such a clear correspondence with Thornton's work in 1971 with regards to 540 and 610nm. There is a bit of variation with the blue, Thornton's "ideal" would be 450, while the bulb seems to have a slightly shorter peak, around 435. Maybe this is just a non-ideal approximation of 450, due to the available phosphors that are used. On the other hand, the peaks at 540 and 610 are so clear-cut and precise, it makes me wonder.

They also provide the color-rendering index of the bulb on the label: 94. This is done with with the three "spikes" mentioned above: 435, 540, and 610, the rest of the emission from the bulb is inconsequential in my opinion. A color-rendering index of 94 has got to be good enough for our purposes! Natural daylight is is 100, but even the quality of daylight can vary dramatically, being high in blue content from the blue sky on a clear day, or much less blue when passing through clouds. Direct sunlight would of course be different. Or, if the daylight is bouncing around on the "green" earth, it would have more green, etc.
