Dinesh Thu Mar 31, 2005 11:28 pm #14
First of all, yes he must be using a half waveplate. As wler says, a quarter wave plate makes no sense, since he's altering the polarisation vector angle, not the polarisation state from planar to ciurcular.

It also seems there's some confusion about how this system works. If you have a polarising waveplate, then such a waveplate takes the angle of polarisarion and splits the beam to two orthogonal polarisation states whose ratio is proportional to the angle of the input. Thus if the input beam was polarised at 45 degrees, then you'd get two beams at 1:1 with one polarised vertically and one horizontally. Now since orthogonal polarisation states don't interfere you need to get both state parallel. You can either use mirrors and planes or you can use another halfwave plate to rotate one or other polarisation state so that both states are now parallel. To vary the beam ratio, you simply rotate the input halfwave plate. The polarisation state of a polarised beam after a halfwave plate is rotated by twice the angle of rotation of the waveplate. Thus in the example above, if you wanted a 70:30 ratio instead of a 1:1, then you'd rotate the halfwave plate by about 20% (to decrease one beam by (70-50)/(2*50), hopefully the other beam would amplify by a similar amount, "hopefully" being the key word here!)).Since this needs a polarising cube beamsplitter (not cheap!) and two halfwaveplates (also not cheap!), this is an expensive and complex solution. There are non-polarising cube beamsplitters which work by FTIR and are a lot cheaper, but these have a constant ratio. There are also both linear and circular variable beamspliitters that are sometimes called "variable ndf's" but these have nothing to do with polarisation. Be careful that the "variable ndf" is not really a variable ndf!

Frustrated Total Internal Reflection. It works by the principle of evanescent waves. When the light inside a dense medium hits the surface at just the total internal reflection angle, it "runs" along the inside face of the medium. However, there is a small amount of "leak" of the e-field outside the dense medium. If another dense medium is brought into close contact, this "leaked" e-field produces light inside the second dense medium. You can think of it that the second dense medium "sucks up" the surface light. The light now propogates inside the second dense medium as well. The amount of light "sucked into" the second medium depends on factors like the seperation between media and the indices of the media. For an FTIR beamsplitter, you launch light into the face one medium shaped like a prism and arranging that it hits the opposite (slanted) face at j-u-u-st the total internal reflection angle. You then place another prism shaped medium, with the slanted faces parallel, vewy, vewy near the first. The evanescent waves from the first "leaks" into the second and propagates within, and eventually out of, the second medium. The proportion of the splitting intensity depends on how close the second medium is.

[quote:cf1069f42c="Colin Kaminski"][quote:cf1069f42c="Dinesh"]Be careful that the "variable ndf" is not really a variable ndf![/quote:cf1069f42c]

A beamsplitter requires that one beam goes through the optic and one reflects off it. A "true" beamsplitter therefore has a semi-reflective surface such that some light passes through and some reflects. However, an ndf (neutral density filter) only attenuates light. The lost light is not reflected, just absorbed. "Variable ndf's" are just materials with a varying amount of darkening so that the attentuation is variable along a face (if linear) or around the circle (if round). However, some so-called "variable ndf's" are actually true variable beamsplitters in that the filtering in the 'f' part is done by reflecting a proportion of the light rather than absorbing it. Some manufacturers don't even have "variable beamsplitters" but you'll find that their "variable ndf's" are really variable beamsplitters.

Dinesh Fri Apr 01, 2005 9:32 pm #22
Part of this confusion arises with the confusion over waveplates and polarisers. It seems pretty common since I've seen this confusion often.

Let's start at the basics. Light is an oscillating electric vector (I'm ignoring ther magnetic component now). This means you can associate an arrow of sorts with a light beam. This arrow grows and diminishes as the light travels forward, but it's always at right angles to the direction of travel of the light. This means that if the light travels from left to right then the electric vector, the polarisation, is either up-and-down or away-and-toward or at any angle between the two. If you imagine the direction of light travel as a rod, then imagine there's a dinner plate attached at right angles to the end of the rod. Any line drawn across the dinner plate is a possible polarisation direction. Now you have two possibilities: the dinner plate, with the line drawn across it is toddling along steadily and the line is alweays in the same direction, or the dinner plate is itself revolving. In the former case, with the line constant and fixed, you have plane polarisation, in the latter, with the dinner plate revolving, you have circular polarisation. If the plate is randomly rotating and suddenly altering it's direction of rotation at random times, it's called unpolarised. Note it isn't truly "unpolarised". "Randomly polarised" is more accurate.

In the former case, you can draw any line on the dinner plate. You can draw a vertical one, a horizontal one, one at 45 degrees, one at 38.4567 degrees if you wish - any old line. A polariser is a gadget that has a long slit in it (OK, on it). This means that if you orientate the polariser so that the slit is vertical, then any line drawn horizonatlly on the plate won't go through. The slit is vertical and the line is horizontal. Note, it doesn't alter the drawn line, it merely stops it from going through. Orientate the slit horizontally and now the line goes through. If the line is at some angle, then only part of it goes through - the part that fits "inside" the slit (if you're interested, this is known as Malus's Law). As Bob says, anything sent through this slit comes out linear because the slit itself is linear. Note also that if the dinner plate is revolving (circular polarisation) then the amount of light that goes through depends on whatever angle the line was at when it met the slit. If the beam is "unpolarised" then whatever direction the line was, that proportion comes through, but there's no predictability as there is in a well-behaved rotating plate. The next plate behind it may have a totally unpredictable direction of the line.

Now imagine a gadget that's programmned so that it looks at the line on the dinner plate and redraws it. This is a half wave plate. Why it works is the subject of crystal optics - where stuff goes funny - but effectively, the half wave plate (also called a retarder) redraws the line on the dinner plate according to a built-in axis. Since the axis on the retarder is built in, you can change the direction that this gadget redraws the line by merely rotating the gadget. This changes the axis on the waveplate and so the the waveplate redraws the line on the plate in a different direction. This is actually a half wave plate - half because of the mathematical thing called a phase relationship of 3.14159265358976/2 - Hey, the other kids in the class spent their time tossing around footballs, cricket balls and all manner of other balls. Me, I spent my time memorising pi

Now suppose you had a gadget that looked at the dinner plate's state, ie was it rotating or not. If it was not, this gadget would set it rotating, while if it was, this gadget would stop it roatating. That is, if the beam were circularly polarised, it would coinvert the light to plane polarisation while if th beam were plane polarised, it would change it to circular polarising. This is a quarter wave plate.

So, an unpolarised beam can be made poarised by passing through a polariser. The direction of the beam is now whatever direction the "slits" in the polariser happened to be. This "slit direction" is known as the pass axis. However, an unpolarised beam poassing through a halfwave plate would simply result in another unpolarised beam but the direction of the polarisation would alter. The gadget would simply look at the present line, figure out a new line based on it's own axis and redraw the new line. The new line has a fixed relationship to the old line. As the old line changed (poalrisation is altering) the new line would change accordingly (polarisation is still being changed). A polarised beam going through a half wave plate will have it's direction of polarisation altered by a half wave plate and converted to circular polarisation by a quarter wave plate.

[quote:1275978eea="JohnFlux"]

A couple of questions if you don't mind..

It seems to be that every birefringent material that grid structure (i.e. ignoring cockscrew etc molecules) is a wave plate for a particular wave length of light, and every wave plate is a birefringent material.

Not completely. Birefringent materials have specific axes, called the optic axes, depending on the anisotropy of the crystal structure. Not all crystal structures are optically anisotropic, the structure itself must be anisotropic. Thus, only only hexagonal, tetragonal and trigonal crystal structures are birefringent. Salt, a symmetric, cubic crystal, has no birefringence. Of the ones that are, some are uniaxial and some are biaxial. The uniaxial ones have only one optic axis and the biaxial ones have two. These optic axes give rise to differences in refractive index and also splits the polarisation of the wwaves. Thus a uniaxial, birefringent material has two indices of refraction, the so-called ordinary and extraordinary, also known as o and e. Thus any wave travelling at any direction except along the optic axis, splits into two waves - the e-wave and the o-wave - with polarisations at right angles to each other. The directions of the e-wave and the o-wave are in general different however, the e-wave tends to revolve around the o-wave. Thus if you look at some text through a piece of calcite, you'll see the text split. As you revolve the crystal, one image will revoilve while the other stays still.

If the wave travels perpendicular to the optic axis, then the o- and e-waves travel in the same direction with orthogonal polarisations, however there is a variation in the wave velocity. Thus the phases of the two waves are seperated in space. On leaving the crystal, at the other face, the two phases combine together to give a resultant phase.

I'm a litle wary of dealing with the physics of optical phenomena here, since this forum is more interesting in the "nuts and bolts" aspect of holography. If you're interested in birefringence and polartisation you can always email me.
