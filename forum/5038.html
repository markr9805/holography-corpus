Now that I've got your attention, I was wondering if any on here could shed some light on the likely reasons for the following sludging of a hologram I made last December.

Last week I decided to take a hologram that I'd left un-backed (so one could see through the hologram and glass frame) and treat it with ascorbic acid to increase the contrast. The hologram has been sitting in a well-lit room open to the elements for slightly over eight months and has shown no ill-effects at all. No darkening of the film and no dimming of the image.

After dropping the film in the ascorbic (10% solution) I turned on the lamp overhead and left the room. Several minutes later I came in to check on it and saw that the hologram had developed a filmy look. I pulled it out of the solution and as I did so several spots floated away. The emulsion had turned into a sludgy film.

I tried it again with a different hologram from the same period and got the same results.

Any thoughts?

Yes I think this is a subject that needs airing again. It is a problem we need to be aware off.

My guess is that the effect would have also occurred with just water alone and that it was not to do with the ascorbic acid. (although I have not understood what the ascorbic acid was to do with regard to contrast).

I have been also been shocked to have this happen to me.---

A nice bright Denisyuk 10” x 8” HIG I made over 23 years ago and had given to a special friend , had been on a brightly lit wall for about 4 years in a glass covered picture frame . It was originally a real image golden Tutankhamen mask. It was made on rehalogenated bleached Agfa film and had not darkened by “print-out” because I had left it (I think) in an acid final rinse with a bit of dichromate included (dichromate was not used in the initial bleach though). After about 3 years I noticed it had become a bright green color and with that image in particular it had to be gold colored.

So I offered to re-treat it to get it to expand back to its metallic gold . “Now you won’t spoil it will you?”

she said. “Of course not” said I confidentally.

I took it out of its frame and put it into a dish of just water and was staggered to see it at once break into a mass of miniflakes in the dish so I was left with just a sheet of clean polyester with.-- and this is very revealing—a firm coherent outer border of the original gelatin which had been protected from the ambient light by the frame and when dry this border showed the outer edges of the golden mask .

So it seems that there are probably many holograms out there that have been absorbing light for years (no doubt it is the violet end that matters)

and I think it causes random micro cracks of the gelatin structure which does nothing to reveal its damaged state until it is put in water. I expect it is not just silver halide film that can suffer. The picture frame glass no doubt helps to protect but it is just a matter of more time before that actinic light gets through..... Actually the Tut was from necessity made with the emulsion facing out because it was of a pseudoscopic object, so I did not have the added protection of the polyester layer.

Abyway, have any of you venerable DCGers found that a long-time sun-blasted example fell to bits when you tried to reprocess it?

Dinesh Tue Sep 06, 2005 12:51 pm #11
[quote:0840eeadc6="jeff-blyth"]Abyway, have any of you venerable DCGers found that a long-time sun-blasted example fell to bits when you tried to reprocess it? [/quote:0840eeadc6]

With DCG this testing is not possible, usually. DCG holograms are sealed within a few days at most which makes them impossible to reprocess. However, I have DCG's that are 15 years old kept under normal lighting conditions (indoors under halogen lighting, but not in direct sunlight) with no apparent degradation.

I have also seen this damage to old emulsions in acetone. In 1990 or so, I was visiting Brown University, where the first copy of "The Meeting" was kept. The Optics Prof asked one of his students, Mitch Henrion, to re-bleach the hologram as it was getting discoloured. However, the hologram was blacked so she asked me how to remove the black backing safely. I suggested acetone, but since this was a historic piece, I cut one of my own holograms in half and soaked in acetone. Comparing the two halves, the blacking was gone with no apprent damage to the image. So we put "The Meeting" into a bath of acetone and sloshed it around a bit. What came out was a clean piece of glass!

I don't know what conditions the hologram was put in, but I suspected at the time the emulsion dried and lifted off the base, allowing the acetone to penetrate under it and lift it.

My feeling now is that gelatin in an emulsion continues to cross link, albeit incredibly slowly. This cross linking rate will depend on the amount of sunlight (specifically UV) that it receives. This cross linking tends to harden the gelatin, make it brittle and seperate it from the base. Any immersion in a liquid will get underneath, between emulsion and base and effectively break up the now-brittle emulsion. This effect is obviously enhanced if unexposed gelatin is exposed to UV and also is obviously affected by the type of gelatin used and the type of environment is placed in. This probably has a bearing on John's comment of pollutants, since the cross-linking rate is dependant on pH. If the emulsion is protected by light and the elements, either by glass for DCG or a black paint for silver, the rate of cross linking will reduce and the hologram will last a longer time.

Holodisc Mon Sep 19, 2005 1:00 pm #15
In hot and humid climates, preservation of the film without air conditioning is impossible. Even cellulose acetate base degrades, becoming sticky and distorted. This is known as the "Vinegar Syndrome" because of the acetic acid, with its characteristic vinegar smell, formed during the decomposition. The emulsion layers can separate from the base during this process and render the film unusable.

Another danger with high humidities above about 60% is growth of bacteria and mold. But humidity is not the only environment that bacteria can effect emulsion in, and not all bacteria thrive in oxygen.

Some bacteria, are photodissassociative whereby they use photons to pump hydrogen across their membranes. The hydrogen from trapped air is collected in sealed pockets, areas in the emulsion volume, and precipitate as an 'acid', the hydrogen based acid breaks down the collagen bonds, and so are anti-cross linking in action, thereby destroying the integrity of the layer(s).

UV and other blue light, is not especially destructive to bacteria such as Escherichia coli. Very little UV gets through most glass, but in the open, UV exposure will mutate the bacteria in an emulsion and in trying to repair itself, will create much acid which is trapped in the emulsion. Over long exposures and time, even low dosaes of UV and blue light may still activate these mechanisms. Also the emulsion may have carbohydrates in it, or other chemicals which also induce growth. Experiments with some 'crystalized bacteria', show, that it will still continue to pump hydrogen. The hydrogen based acid, cuts bonds. If the emulsion is stuck to glass, tension will build as the internal bonds collapse. This tension, when released, will crack and shatter the matrix!

 

Aerobes = Require oxygen to grow

Anaerobes = Do not require oxygen for growth

Obligate aerobes = Must have free O<sub>2 for respiration.

Obligate or strict anaerobes = killed by free O2.

Microaerophiles = grow best in presence of small amounts of free O2.

Facultative anaerobes = carry out aerobic respiration when O2 is present but shifts to anaerobic respiration in its absence.

Aerotolerant anaerobes = Survive in the presence of O2 but do not use it in their metabolism.

Bacteriological Process Residues:

ACIDOPHILES & NEUTROPHILES USE PROTON PUMPS & POTASSIUM TRANSPORT SYSTEMS TO MAINTAIN pH. Superoxide radical, hydrogen peroxide & hydroxyl radicals are extremely toxic. Hydroxyl radicals are most toxic With half life of micro-seconds They react with most organic molecules resulting in their destruction.

This sort of mechanism, where organic corruption of the emulsion destroys the bonds of the emulsion over time with light exposure ...may not be the cause of the present discussion's challenge, but it is a failure mode that some of you may experience and need to think about.

Microfiltering fluids and emulsions etc, not sneezing in your photolab, wearing gloves and a mask, sealing your hologram in a germ-free environment, and essentially ...treating the gel of your hologram as if it were the epidermis of your arm, is how to get past this nonarchival mechanism!

The general process I'm thinking of is as follows (rough notes).

Processing of the holograms

· Pre-rinse with heated, filtered water. Tub of 1/2 gallon water. Record temperature.

· Development in JD-2, diluted 2 parts distilled water to 1 part developer.

· Acetic/vinegar distilled water rinse for 1 minute. Record dilution and temperature.

· Filtered heated water rinse in 1/2 gal water for 1 minute with agitation.

· EDTA bleach until clear + 10 seconds. Record bleach time and temperature.

Save samples of the tub and faucet water.

Two minute final rinse in 1/2 gal filtered water

· Heated.

· Un-heated.

· With acetic acid.

· Without acetic acid.

Storage

· Cold laminated with Oracal.

· Not laminated with Oracal.

· Left open to the air

· Left in a closed container.

· Left in a closed container filled with nitrogen.

· Left in a cool environment.

· Left in a hot environment.

· Left in a container with nitrogen in a cool environment

· Left in a well-lit environment

· Left in a dark environment

The tests will result in 40 different samples to track for at least six months and possibly up to a year.
