Joe Farina Tue Nov 16, 2010 1:50 pm #1
After doing some tests of paints for use in 2-color holography (532nm & 633nm) I noticed some things which might be useful for other holographers planning 2 or 3-color holograms. The main thing is that I under-estimated the importance of the final reconstructing source on the final result. It's been said frequently that the appearance of colored objects under laser light (the combination of lasers, that is) will provide a preview of what the final hologram could look like. That sounds logical, but of course that's only part of the picture, since the final hologram will need to be illuminated by a reconstructing source that could be highly variable, unless the source is known and planned in advance.

When the same group of colors is observed with different white-light sources, the appearance can vary greatly. I bought about 20 tubes of different gouache colors, and smeared them on a piece of card, and mixed part of each rub-out with white on one end, so that I would know what the lightened version of each color would look like. I looked at them under a mixture of 532nm and 633nm, and selected the colors which looked best.

Then I looked at the same card under the white LED which I'm planning to use for reconstruction. This produced a different set of results (as to what looks best).

So, if anyone is doing something similar, I would suggest doing both tests at the same time (under laser light and under the planned reconstruction source). Then I would AVERAGE the results and select the colors accordingly. By the way, prices for LEDs have come down considerably. I just bought 50 white LEDs which are excellent for 4 X 5 inch holograms, for $26.

Gooood morning Jo Jo,

This color mixing and holography has facinated me for quite some time and when I was doing testing with DCG in multi-color I did tons of research. I think what I like about it is the limitations of using two or 3 pure wavelength to come up with multiple other wavelengths. I would like to throw my opion out there.

What you say is true for sure, but we need to take into consideration the medium we are using. Picking paint color by what they look like in the reconstruction light is not really the way to go simply because we are not creating the hologram with all the wavelengths of the reconstruction light.

When making a hologram we want to match the paint that has the hightest reflectivity of the wavelength of light we are using. So for 532 we need a paint that reflects the most 532 and the least 633. Then for the 663 of course we need a paint that reflects the most 633 and the least 532. We want a pure pigment that has the minimal amount of white in it as this will reflect the other wavelength.

Now to "paint" you object with the colors that you want in the final hologram considering the illuminating light. We take these two paints and observe them in the illuminating light, in this case the white LED. We can see how these colors behave. Adding white paint to change the color of one of the "pure paints" will not result in a correct final color as the laser will only be adding the other pure wavelength whereas when we are adding white paint under the white light we are adding may different wavelength.

The correct way, in my opinion, is to observer the two "pure" paints that was detemined in our tests to have the best reflectivity under each laser wavelength under the white LED light as mentioned above but to change the final color we can only work with those two paints. So lets call our two "pure" paints Red1 and Green2. To get our full color pallete we can only mix those two paints in various ratios to one another because that is all we can do wit the lasers. So pure Red1 for exposure by 633 should look, if no shrinkage or swelling, exactly like the Red1 that is observed under the white LED. Green2 for exposure toe 532 should look in the final hologram like what that paint looks like under the white LED. And a (these are not exact numbers) 25% Red1 and 75% Green2 should be close to (633 - 532 = ~100 so 25% of 100 = 25 and 633 minus 25 is 608 - Yellow with a little orange) like wise 75% Red1 and 25% Green 2 should be close to (633 - 532 = ~100 so 75% of 100 = 75 and 633 minus 75 is 558 - very limey green).

If your hologram truely has only two wavelengths then all the other wavelength that are not between 633 and 532 can be elliminated even if the LED puts those wavelength out. And if you look at the color triangle (the one that does not look like a true triangle but the rounded corners triangle) you will only be able to get the colors that are on the very edge of the triange beteen those two wavelengths.

Oh, this viewing and testing process (not the actuall math part) which again coinsides with what you stated also takes into account the different sensitivity the eye has to different wavelengths. I put the math up there to try to explain what is happening but in actuallity, looking and combining those two paints uder that white LED to visually see what color is desired should result in the closest color match under that LED.

Hope I explained it well and it makes sense.

John, thanks for the clarification. I agree with much of what you said.

There is an important practical aspect to pigments however. They are highly variable and irregular in the wavelengths they reflect. Of course, there is no paint which reflects just 532 or 633, or even begins to come close.

If you go to the artist's paint section of a store, you will of course see more than 3 colors. If every color could be reproduced by mixing 3 colors of paint, then the color-makers would probably have to go out of business. A lot of colors can be reproduced by using 5 colors (the three primaries plus black and white) but most painters will rely on more.

It's also a question of skill and knowledge. While a very skilled painter might be able to produce a wide range of colors with 5 basic colors (maybe pretty close to all that's needed), it takes a huge amount of knoweledge and experience to do this. It's very difficult to imagine the color needed, and then try to mix it from the primaries. If you could devote a great deal of time to this, it could be done. But imagine how easier (and in the long run, more effective) it would be to have those colors already in front of you, ready to go. You can see them, and know exactly how they will behave.

With regards to DCG, I can see only one reason to be concerned with fluorescence. If the color effect you want is partially due to any fluorescence of the object under laser light, then it should be realized that the fluorescence won't be visible in the final hologram, because it's incoherent light. Some colors might have a little fluorescence "mixed in" with the non-fluorescent color effect, and that's what I'm curious about. Any fluorescence during recording of DCG would probably just contribute to the bias hardening. But it would probably be undesirable, because it seems likely that the hardening would be irregular across the surface of the plate.

With AgX I assume things would be somewhat similar, but due to the greater sensitivity, the fogging effect could get more serious, I guess.
