One of the most interesting and promising areas of holography was brought to my attention while at a conference in Baltimore while I was working for Lenox Laser years ago. The paper outlined a technique that was amazing. Here is how it worked. As a robot roamed down a hall, it would stop and take a photo of the hall from a particular position. Then it continued foot or so and take another picture, and so on down the hall, around a corner and down another hall. Each hall has doors and openings to other halls. Then back at the lab each of these photos (50 or so) were recorded in a 1 cm square LiNb crystal (I don't remember what it was doped with) but the reference beam angle was altered for each of the recordings of the photos. This crystal was then placed back into the robot and a camera focused the image of the hall onto the crystal. Obviously replaying the hologram with the object beam would cause the reference beam with a particular angle to be projected onto a photo detector with a particular angle from the crystal. As the image of the real time hall more closely matched one of the recordings in the crystal, that original reference beam was stronger then any of the others thus when a particular image of the hall was focused the robot knew when to turn as that photo detector detected the most light. The advantage of this method over other optical recognition systems was the low about of data processing. One image from a camera would "instantly" be recognized instead of having to process thousands (millions) of bits representing pixels for each scene. It would recognize the scene as a whole instead of searching a huge database of images for a near match.

I guess the field is waiting for a laser diode, possibly pulsed and with enought power of the correct wavelength that can record the the images directly on the fly. I would imagine they would need to image the scene, feed it to a LCD panel, then record it changing the angle of reference beam for each recording. I thought it was a very interesting application.
