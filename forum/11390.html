It depends on the H2 geometry. Was it a reflection H2, or transmission? Did the image straddle the plane of the H2, ie did you put the H2 plate into the middle of the H1 image, or was there some distance between H1 image and H2 plate? Where was the flashlight with respect to the H2 plate and image.

Assuming it was a reflection H2, with the image straddling the H2 plate, and the flashlight was on the back of the H2. In other words, you were reconstructing a reflection H2 in a transmission geometry, standing on the "wrong" side of the H2, then you'd get a black and white image. The reason is that on reconstructing the reflection hologram in transmission mode, the image would be "thrown" to the other side of the plate, where a viewer viewing in reflection mode would be. Since you're on the "wrong" side, behind the plate rather than in front of it, you'd see a sort of reverse image. The colours of the image on this reverse image would be all the wavelengths in the white light source (the flashlight) except for the colours that went into the reflection image. Since most reflection holograms are narrow-band (only a small range of colours), the reverse image colour is almost white because only a small proportion of the flashlight wavelengths went into the (correct) reflection image. By excluding only a small range of wavelengths the rest of the image appears white. Assuming this is what happened, I've drawn a rough sketch.

If what you did was different, it would be useful to see a diagram of the recording, the position of the reconstructing source and your position.

BobH Sun Nov 24, 2024 10:46 pm #8
You're supposed to see an achromatic (B&W) image from that geometry, when illuminated with the congugate beam for the H2. That is, when you play the H2 back as a transmission hologram so the image of the H1 is on the viewer's side.

A transmission geometry essentially records a prism function with the image. When played with white light, image points get more color-smeared as they get farther from the plate. The H1/H2 geometry essentially records a lens function in the H2 with the image as well as a prism. That keeps image point colors together much farther away from the plate so you see a B&W image over a reasonable depth. Steve Provence used that technique a lot for his embossed holography.

You can get an image when replaying the H2 as a reflection hologram because the Fresnel reflection of the reference beam off the back surface of the plate interferes with the image light to make a reflection hologram at the same time as the main image is recorded. If that's the case, the reflection image should be green.

Din Mon Nov 25, 2024 9:49 am #10
Ignore earlier explanation, I assumed you were looking at a reflection H2 in transmission, since you seemed surprised to see an achromat image.. What you have in the H2 is called an 'achromat', or 'open aperture', there are various names.

When you make a transmission hologram the image is subject to dispersion. This means that different colour components start to separate out, and you see the image as a rainbow - like looking at something through a prism. This is because, in a transmission geometry, the hologram is known as a 'thin' hologram. The essential difference between a reflection ('thick') hologram and a thin hologram is that a thick, reflection hologram only diffracts a narrow range of wavelengths, at a narrow range of angles and you see the image in a narrow range of colour and direction - this is known as 'Bragg selectivity', if you're interested. A transmission ('thin') hologram will diffract all wavelengths at all angles; with each colour being diffracted through a different angle. So, when you reconstruct with a white light (your flashlight) all the colours will diffract at different angles. However, this differentiation of colour with angle starts at the plane of the plate and progresses through larger angles, the further is the image from the plate; the 'colour blur' , if you will, gets progressively worse the further the image is from the plane of the plate.

So, if the image is at the plane of the plate as yours is, there is no (or very little) colour blur and the image appears black and white. As parts of the image are further from the plane of the plate, both front and back, the colour blur gets progressively worse. In a traditional achromat hologram (so-called because there is no disperion at the plane and the image is achromatic), it's advisable to have an object that's only about an inch in front and about half an inch behind the image plane. However, if the object is several inches from the plane of the plate, then dispersion becomes worse, and, at about 3 - 4 inches (7.5 - 10 cm) all you see is a colour blur and all object information is lost. So, if you look at your H1 in transmission mode with a flashlight, it'll just look like a colour blur. However, if the recon source has a narrow band (a restricted range of wavelengths), then there is less dispersion. So if you look at your H1 in transmission mode with an led, you'll see some semblance of the original object.

By the by, this dispersion effect is helpful in determining the efficacy of your safelight. If you look at the safelight through an H1, the dispersion will tell you how "pure" your safelight is.

Din Mon Nov 25, 2024 1:34 pm #13
> BobHwrote:↑Mon Nov 25, 2024 11:42 am: There will be Bragg selectivity as well as dispersion.

You cannot have both Bragg selectivity and dispersion, the two are contradictory. Dispersion - the variation of the angular deviation of wavelength components as a function of angle of incidence - does not occur in Bragg selectivity. In Bragg selectivity, a variation of +/- 5 degrees will cause the diffraction to drop to zero (where the +/- 5 degrees is given by the dephasing parameter in the Kogelnik, coupled-wave, equations) whereas in a dispersive hologram, diffraction occurs occurs through a wide variation of angles. The more exact definition would be to differentiate between 'thin' and 'thick' is to determine whether the hologram follows a Bragg model or a Raman-Nath model. In a Bragg model, small changes in the reconstruction parameters will cause the diffraction efficiency to fall to almost zero (the image disappears in a display hologram), whereas in the Raman Nath model, there can be considerable variation in the reconstruction parameters without significant loss in efficiency. Below, a plot of Raman-Nath vs Bragg as a function of angle of incidence, the difference in the reconstruction modality of the two kinds of diffraction can be seen.

> BobHwrote:↑Mon Nov 25, 2024 11:42 am: Transmission and reflection refer to geometry, thin and thick refer to recording material. The two sets of words are not related.

The difference between 'thin' and 'thick' depends on the value of the Q parameter:

Q = 2πλd/Λ²

where d is the material thickness and Λ is the 'fringe' width.

So, as you see, the criterion between the two is not just the recording material, it's also the spatial frequency (1/Λ) of the recorded 'fringe' structure. Generally speaking, if the Q<~10, we have a 'thin' hologram. In Alan's case, judging by his diagram, I'd approximate his reference angle (on which at Λ is based) about 30 deg, and Joy tells me that standard Bayer polymer is about 15 microns. This give me a Q ~ 6.5, and so well within the 'thin' range.

 "Unified Approach to Ultrasonic Light Diffraction" Klein and Cook, IEEE Transactions on Sonics and Ultrasonics. Also, eqn 4.53 in Hariharan "Optical Holography"

EDIT: There is always, of course, the spatial coherence of the reconstruction source. The sun subtends about half a degree to the earth, and therefore is probably the highest white light space-coherent source existing. Lasers are, of course, far more spatially coherent. The reconstruction properties of any hologram are based on the similarities between the recording geometry and the reconstructing geometry in terms of both space and time coherence. The Bragg structure mitigates against any deviation from the time coherence of the reconstruction source, but there is no mitigation against a deviation from the space coherence requirements. This deviation may cause dispersion.
