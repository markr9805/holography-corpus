doug694 Sat May 19, 2007 8:53 pm #10
Some perhaps useful notes regarding dye lasers...

The most efficient way to pump them is to use a UV laser. The most common (i.e. cheap, simple and effective) implementation is a nitrogen laser.

Nitrogen lasers ([T]ransversely [E]xcited [A]ir or [T]ransversely [E]xcited [R]educed [P]ressure, using either air or a nitrogen mix) are easy to build and produce kilowatt -> megawatt pulses in the 1ns pulse length category.

Such high peak power and short pulse duration is good for exciting dye, and doesn't lead to problems with triplet forming by the dye molecules (this stops lasing, and takes time to recover). Total energy delivered however is small, so a dye laser pumped in this way would require more than one pulse to expose a hologram (probably many).

Obviously, dye excited by a 1ns pump will produce a very clean pulse and with a duration much shorter than most q-switches can manage, although the pulse may be a bit longer than the pump pulse (it depends on the dye concentration).

Dye concentration needs to be very high for this to work, as the pump beam is focussed via cylindrical lens to a thin line inside the dye cell and with a short cavity, gain must be high. The dye cell walls, assuming they are parallel, are usually enough to give feedback for laser threshold.

Obviously the lens must be made of a UV-transmitting material such as fused silica or quartz (good borosilicate will work, but loses power).

The radical alternative to this scheme is to use a very long dye cell, a very weak dye concentration, and pump it longditudinally like a solid-state laser with a xenon lamp.

Note that you will never be able to produce a pump 'flash' short enough to beat the fluorescence upper-state lifetime of the dye (<100ns), but you _can_ saturate the dye with sheer power.

From the xenon lamp's point of view, this is 'peak power' in the form of a single flash, but from the point of view of the dye, this represents high average power (CW) pump source. Sufficiently high average power during a xenon pulse will threshold the dye regardless of fluorescence lifetime. The dye will stop lasing very shortly afterwards (long before the lamp is finished). This is a very inefficient way to pump a dye laser, because most of the energy is lost as either heat, much of which is absorbed by dye which has stopped lasing and already in a triplet state.

However just because it is inefficient, doesn't mean the laser pulse can't be powerful. Very long dye cells are possible, and dye gain is high. Long dye lasers can produce very powerful pulses with very short durations.

Perhaps the last critical thing which affects pumping is dye choice. Each dye has its own idea of a pump wavelength. Some absorb UV very well (such as Stilbene and Coumarin 460), others don't (like Fluorescein), prefering visible wavelengths. You can use tricks, like mixing Coumarin with Fluorescein to act as a transfer buffer, which converts the UV into more useful pump light from the primary dye.

I had a great link showing absorption and emission graphs for nearly all of the dyes, but it's not handy. I'll have to dig for it. Sorry!

Its worth trying to build a small experimental nitrogen/dye laser to get a feel for whats possible.

There are some extra issues such as dominant wavelength selection, (which requires a diffraction grating or prism, or special mirrors), mode control and coherence length which are all critical for holography. But considering the dye is cheap, reconfiguration is generally an engineering problem rather than a laser-rod-sourcing problem!

I have been spending some time with dyes doped into plastic rods, using xenon lamps, mercury lamps and nitrogen lasers as pump sources. Some problems persist but fluorescence response from the rods is excellent. Getting optical grade finish on the rods is the main challenge right now. Looking into the possibility of using a fiber polishing puck to solve this. Lots of other stuff keeping me busy first though :-/

jonsinger Sat Sep 29, 2007 1:35 am #11
Hi.

I am very interested in the possibility of using a flashlamp-pumped dye laser for pulsed holography, and in fact I am in the middle of rebuilding my big dye laser right now.

Before I get into that, however, let me say a few things about some of what I have read in this topic.

A) Concerning Q-switching: Under ordinary circumstances, it is not possible to Q-switch a dye laser. The fluorescence lifetime of Rhodamine 6G, for example, is just over 4 nsec. In order to Q-switch it you would have to have a Q-switch that could respond significantly faster than the lifetime of the dye. If you wanted a single Q-switched pulse rather than a series, you would also need a pump pulse that was only about 4 nsec long or even a bit less. These things are both possible, at least in principle (a TEA nitrogen laser would be a reasonable pump), but what would it get you? I have never heard of anybody who even bothered to try it.

B) "In fact, dye lasers have a big problem with singlet-triplet conversion..."

This is not quite correct. Some dyes have problems with singlet-triplet conversion, but some don't. Fluorescein, Rhodamine 6G, 7-Diethylamino-Coumarin, and 4-Methyl-Umbelliferone, for example, seem to work quite well without any special effort or worry about this issue. I have operated all of them in my flashlamp-pumped dye laser, and they were all easy.

"...so they do need much shorter pump flash than solid-state ones."

With the dyes that most of us can get, the short upper-level lifetime is what causes them to need short pump pulses, not the intersystem crossing issue. The lifetime of R6G is just over 4 nsec. The lifetime of Nd+++ in YAG is 230 microsec, more than 50X as long. You can calculate the peak electrical power you need to jam into your flashlamp in order to bring a dye laser to threshold, do the same calculation for Nd+++, and compare the numbers. (The longer fluorescence wavelength of Nd+++ makes it even easier.)

If you don't want to bother calculating, I can tell you that my big dye laser can threshold R6G with something like 20 joules stored energy. The capacitor in that laser is only 0.1 microfarad, and the resulting lamp pulse is about 500 nsec long; this is ~40 MW average electrical power during the pulse, roughly 56 MW peak. (I am charging the cap to ~20 kV.) This should tell you why photoflash capacitors are not a good bet. It is sometimes possible to build a working dye laser with large capacitors running at relatively low voltage, ...but it isn't easy.

"This also causes problems with q-switching."

As mentioned before, it is not feasible to Q-switch a dye laser; but the S-T intersystem crossing has nothing to do with it.

If I may quote Doug694 for a few lines:

From the xenon lamp's point of view, this is 'peak power' in the form of a single flash, but from the point of view of the dye, this represents high average power (CW) pump source.

I guess that's true in the sense that the lamp pulse is a lot longer than the fluorescence lifetime of the material.

Sufficiently high average power during a xenon pulse will threshold the dye regardless of fluorescence lifetime.

Assuming you are using a dye that doesn't have a triplet problem, this is true. The real issue here is what "sufficiently high power" means. (As I mention above, for R6G in my laser it's on the order of 50 million watts.)

The dye will stop lasing very shortly afterwards (long before the lamp is finished). This is a very inefficient way to pump a dye laser, because most of the energy is lost as either heat, much of which is absorbed by dye which has stopped lasing and already in a triplet state.

Well, I have run ordinary dye lasers with lamp pulses down to about half a microsecond long, and I have run them with lamp pulses up to about 2 microseconds long. The dye did not stop lasing long before the lamp was finished. What I haven't tried, though, is lamp pulses on the order of 2 dozen microseconds long; you have to work pretty hard to maintain 50 MW for 25 microseconds!

A flashlamp generally isn't the most efficient way to pump a dye laser, but I don't think it's really quite as terrible as you make it out to be.

Something else Doug694 has mentioned: nitrogen lasers. While a nitrogen laser is a nice easy pump, it has one killer drawback for what we want to do here: the output energy is too low. A really huge nitrogen laser puts out maybe as much as 10 or 15 millijoules. If we get fairly good conversion efficiency in the dye laser, we can expect up to about 5 millijoules output. That's enough to expose a 6cm x 6cm area on a BB-640 plate, which seems like it might be okay...

...Except that it's an "absolute best case" calculation. The biggest nitrogen laser I ever built puts out only 1.75 millijoules or so, and if I used it to run a dye laser, even a relatively good one, I'd get about enough energy to expose a 1.5cm x 1.5 cm area. That just isn't going to cut it.

You could always pump a dye laser with a doubled Nd+++ laser; but if you already have green light, why bother with a dye at all? A lot of the professionals seem to be using doubled Nd+++, in fact.

This leaves two main possibilities: flashlamp (or spark) pumping, and excimer-laser pumping. I already know how to build lamp-pumped dye lasers, and I don't really want chlorine or fluorine in my workspace, so I'm concentrating on flashlamps for now.

Next problem: Coherence Length.

According to the nice folks at Exciton, Rhodamine 6G can be tuned from 553 nm to 615 nm. That's a range of 62 nm in wavelength, which turns out to be just under 55 THz in frequency.

If you run R6G without any tuning it will lase near 590 nm, and you could possibly get about 10 nm bandwidth out of it, which is about 8.9 Thz. (It is quite difficult to find information about this on the Web; about the best I can say is that a p-Terphenyl dye laser, pumped by a KrF excimer laser, had an untuned bandwidth of about 2.5 nm at roughly 339 nm.) In any case, the coherence length is much too short to be useful for holography.

Clearly, it will be necessary to tune the laser. A grating, unfortunately, isn't going to be enough, at least not by itself.

Light travels about 300 million meters per second. This means that a wave with a frequency of 300 MHz has a wavelength of about 1 meter. It also means (depending on how you define it) that a wave with a bandwidth of 300 MHz has coherence length of about 1 meter. (I'm not going to get into the details here; different people get different numbers, and you can look up the various arguments and definitions on the Web; I think the Wikipedia page on coherence length is pretty good.)

In order to get 25 cm coherence length (1/4 of a meter), we need bandwidth of about 1200 MHz (4 x 300 MHz). Given that 10 nm bandwidth near 590 nm is about 8.9 THz, 0.010 nm would be 8.9 GHz, and 0.001 nm would be 890 MHz, which is close enough for folk music. Got that? We need a bandwidth of a little over one PICOMETER if we want to have a good chance of actually making holograms. (My calculation is probably a bit conservative; I think people have used lasers with bandwidths of a few pm to make passable holograms of small objects. Even that, however, just isn't what you expect to get from a regular pulsed dye laser.) It will be necessary to use some sort of advanced technique or techniques here.

If I get anywhere with this, I'll post about what it took to do it. I'm not holding my breath, though, as I have serious doubts about whether I will actually succeed.

Cheers --
