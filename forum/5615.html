In the drawing above is the cross section of two wires that are tightly wound across a bar of metal.

- The surface area of the bounding box of one wire with diameter x would be S=xx.

- The surface area of one wire in the cross section of wound wire would be: A=(0.5x)^2pi

- The area that is of the bounding box that is not occupied by the wound wire is then S-A = xx-0.25xxpi

- Only half of that area is available for coating (because the wire is tightly wound), so Q= 0.5(S-A) = 0.5(x^2-0.25x^2pi) = 0.1073x^2

- Because after the bar moves over the emulsion, the emulsion will try to flow into evenness, the thickness of the final emulsion will be: t = Q/x = 0.1073x

- In [http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewt ... sc&start=0](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4458&postdays=0&postorder=asc&start=0) John suggests that when a g% coating dries, the volume of the gelatin that is left is g%. So the final thickness of a dried emulsion should be: t=g%0.1073x

- So, for a coating with a thickness of 8um, the desired wire thickness would be: x=t/(g%0.1073)*=5E-4m = 0.5mm (for 15% gelatin). This is about the same as 24SWG wire.

This 0.5mm is consistant with the side note in Saxby's Practical Holography (page 320).

However, I also found one reference where it is suggested to use SWG20 wire, not tightly wound, but at about 7 turns per cm. This confuses me because at 7 turns per cm, the wire is not tightly wound and resembles more to a doctor's blade with a clearance of 1mm for SWG20. That would leave an emulsion thickness of 150um with a 15% concentration.

The idea of using SWG20 with 7 turns per cm is appealing because it is much more easy to make that than a smoothly tightly wound bar with SWG24 wire. But I cannot make the math work for me in the case of the loosely wound bar. How can that work?

Joe Farina Sun Jun 11, 2006 11:57 am #8
Personally, I wouldn't delve too deeply into the "science" of the Mayer bar. By the way, "Meyer" bar seems to be a misnomer, see: [http://www.rdspecialties.com/Page.asp?Script=2](http://www.rdspecialties.com/Page.asp?Script=2)

When a coating is made with a Mayer bar, it's not really the "Mayer bar method" of making a coating. Essentially, it is the "gravity settling" method. The Mayer bar simply acts as an effective spreader for the gelatin. There are many other ways to spread gelatin across a plate. For example, a simple rod, with adhesive tape placed along the edges of the glass (to act as "risers").

However, I think Mayer bars are the best way to spread gelatin, since all you need to do is change the wire gauge on the bar, and the thickness of the final coating will change accordingly. There is no need to change any other parameter, and it will be reliable and repeatable.

When using the gravity settling method with a Mayer bar, I think the following points are significant:

1. The plate should be warm, so that gravity has a chance to act, making an even coating. If the gelatin gells too quickly, this will interfere with the gravity process, and there may also be "Mayer bar marks."

2. The coating should be made in a dust-free area. I think a laminar flow enclosure is best (these can homemade).

3. If you want to know the final (dry) thickness of the emulsion, a measurement method is needed. If you don't have a reliable measurement technique, there is no way to assess the results. Practice the "craft" of making coatings, not the science. In other words, keep track of what you do, change one variable at a time, and have a way to assess your final results. Do this often, and don't ask too many questions.
