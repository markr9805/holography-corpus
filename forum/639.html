Dinesh Mon Oct 29, 2012 8:58 am #7
This is speculative, since I haven't seen the gratings.

In the first case ("cheap grating" - assuming it was holographic ), there is a low modulation surface profile. In this case, light transmitted through the grating will pass through the surface and there will be a phase change due to the longer path length of the light through the "hills" of the grating as opposed to the "valleys" of the grating. This phase change causes diffraction. However, light reflected from the surface profile will also interfere due to the undulations on the surface. There is a path difference again between the light hitting the hills and light hitting the valleys. So, the grating diffracts in reflection also. This is basically how resist works; it's a transmission hologram - you record in transmission geometry - but it's viewed in reflection mode. In the second case ("reflection hologram") all that I can come up with is that there must have been interference between the emulsion and the substrate (glass?). The reflections from the front and the back of the glass ("Newton's fringes") is very wavelength dependent, especially since the glass is thousands of wavelengths deep. The fact that it's thousands of wavelengths deep makes it very sensitive to angle for Newton's fringes to be seen. At the correct angle and/or at the correct thickness, glass will produce fringes because for that specific angle and thickness the light path difference between the front reflection and the back reflection is a multiple of (lambda)/2. The emulsion, being thinner, is not as angularly sensitive and so may create fringes (or you simply may have been hitting it at the right angle for the emulsion thickness). The fact that some parts of the glass showed fringes and some not, irrespective of the image, may be explained by a variability of the emulsion thickness. The thickness of the fringes is probably a function of the flatness of your emulsion.The flatter the emulsion, the more identical is the direction between the two interfering light paths and a flat surface would reflect light from front of the emulsion and back of the emulsion - the glass substrate surface - along the same direction. If the emulsion flatness varied, then light reflected of the slant side of the emulsion would interfere with the light light from the uniform base and cause a higher spatial frequency.

> Joe Farina: I have another question, if anyone cares to consider it. Say we are doing a split-beam reflection master using a real-time recording material. I wonder what the light is doing which is being reflected back off the newly-forming fringe-structure (say for example the reflected reference beam). Will there be fringes similar to interferometer fringes?

Yes, depending on such factors as ratio, angle of incidence, polarisation etc. You have two (actually, quite a few more than two) competing processes to transfer actinic energy - the direct interference between the object beam and the reference beam and the retro-reflected reference beam. You also have the retro-reflected object beam, the internally scattered beam from density variations inside the emulsion, the various parts of the object interfering with itself ("intermodulation noise") and a few more. These various interactions will cause a variety of fringes to form and the actual modulation of these fringes will determine the diffraction strength and direction. Usually, most of these spurious fringes are low frequency and low modulation, so that these spurious fringe structures usually show up as a low-angle weak diffraction which shows up as a glow around the image - noise.

> Joe Farina: A film grating from a different supplier (Edmund) was also specified as 1,000 l/mm, but produced no fringes in the same orientation.

It's possible that the film surface was coated with a protective coating. This would hide the undulations of the surface grating under the protective coating. The light, not seeing any surface variations, will not interfere.

> Joe Farina: Saran Wrap plastic produces no fringes.

It could be that the thickness was not a factor of lambda/2 for the wavelength you were hitting it with; or it could be an effect of surface non-uniformity causing scattering.

> Joe Farina: A microscope slide produces ~2mm circular fringes.

Well, it's rigid, fairly flat and, probably, a factor of lambda/2

> Joe Farina: A glass plate ~3mm thick produces no fringes.

Probably non-uniform surface.

> Joe Farina: A DCG reflection hologram hologram produces some partial ~2cm wide straight fringes.

As I said above, this could be caused by the Newton's fringes off the emulsion.

At the end of the day, to cause fringes, the surface must be flat to within about lambda/5,ie the rms height must be ~lambda/5, must be rigid and must have a front to back thickness of lambda/2 (actually lambda/(2n) ). The thicker is the material, the more angle sensitive it becomes.

Dinesh Wed Oct 31, 2012 8:04 am #9
Not having read the paper or Kaveh's article, let me see if I understand how the systems at work. Let's say you have two beams hitting the plate from the same side, let's call them B(1) and B(2); you also have a real time holographic medium on the other side of the plate (the side opposite the plate from the beam's origin). Now, going through the plate, you have the diffracted beam from B(1), call it B''(1) and the zero order B(2), call it B'(2), going co-linearly. Then, if B(2) were shifted due to some small motion of the optics, B''(1) and B'(2) no longer collinear. This small angular difference would then presumably form a moire pattern. Would this be right? If so, then yes, you can, in theory, make this work in a reflection geometry. However, there are a number of factors to consider. In a transmission geometry, the fringes are primarily surface fringes and so would form fairly fast. This would result in a fairly fast creation of B''(1). In a reflection geometry, you're creating a volume structure which is slower. In a reflection geometry, the transmitted zero order, B'(2), is dependent on the reflectivity of the medium which in turn is dependent on the polarisation and angle, so it's probably a little more angle sensitive than a transmission geometry. The "real time effect" of dcg that you're referring to is presumably the latent image formation. In this case, the efficiency of the latent image formation, dependent on the concentration of dichromate, is also a factor.

BobH Wed Oct 31, 2012 4:27 pm #11
In practice, using real time fringes as the input to a fringe locker is very difficult. Especially with a diffuse 3D object. Even with a HOE, the alignment of the fringes with the detectors of the fringe locker will be different for each shot. To acquire any very weak signal initially, you'd need very good stability so the forming, very low contrast fringes could be captured. Then there's the issue of a grating in the plateholder with the recording material causing parasitic gratings in the hologram being recorded. Recording materials shrink during recording, causing real time fringes to vary in frequency during the exposure. That further complicates using such fringes to lock the system.

This is a rather complex process, and I'm in no way certain of the correct (or even possible) geometry for recording a split-beam reflection hologram while locking onto a moire pattern formed by the interaction of the reference and object beams with a real-time (newly forming) fringe structure.

There may be some confusion here, because there would be no "grating" in the process, other than the real-time fringes in the hologram. MacQuigg used a secondary hologram which could be considered a grating, but the Frejlich system doesn't use one.

My idea of using a mirror for a specular object beam would involve cementing (or very securely attaching) the mirror to a support directly cemented to the object. Say a rod of glass, cemented and inserted into the object itself, with black velvet covering it, so it won't be seen. Then the end of the glass rod would extend off to the edge of the plate, with a small adjustable mirror at the end. This mirror would reflect a portion of the object beam (picked off from the object beam source) back through the emulsion at the edge of the plate, and back towards the incoming reference beam. The idea is to make the mirror and object as close to "one object" as possible.

The incoming reference beam would be diffracted backwards (I assume) due to the weak real-time fringes being formed. I think I may be missing something, but, the only way to have the mirror on the object reflecting in the same direction as the reference and to be colinear with it is to have the mirror placed so the light from the object, or rather from the attached mirror, hits the plate at the same angle as the reference beam. This would mean that if the object were at a distance d from the plate and the reference beam were coming in at theta, then the mirror height above the centre of the plate would be dtan(theta). Putting in real numbers, if the object were 2 inches away from the plate and the reference were coming in at 60, then the mirror would have to be placed at 2tan(60) = 3.46 inches. Wouldn't this be a little tall and wouldn't this require a fairly large object beam to encompass the mirror? If the mirror were placed near the top of the object (but not at dtan(60) above the plate centre), then there would be diffraction between the reference and the light reflected from the mirror. The diffracted light would then follow the zero order of the reference regardless of the angle at which the mirrored light hits the plate. So, if you had an object that 2 inches tall and one inch from the plate across the centre of the plate, with a mirror on top of the object, then light from the mirror would hit the plate at 45 degrees. This would then interfere with the reference (let's say at 60). Real time diffraction would cause difraction from the mirrored light (at 45 degres) to diffract at 60 degrees and be inline with the reference. I also think that measuring the phase difference between the mirrored light from the object and the reference light would give you a relative phase difference, but not an absolute one.

> BobH: Two things cause them; changes in the optical paths of the system, and changes in the material during recording. The former is what you want to correct for. The latter, caused by shrinkage...

Do real time recordings caues shrinkage? In a transmission geometry the intensity at the plate is:

I(tot) = I(1) + I(2) + sq rt[I(1)I(2)]cos<phase difference>

thus, there is a bulk change because there is still light and an actinic reaction,due to the I(1) + I(2) term, at points where the phase difference becomes pi/2 ("destructive interference"). But, in a reflection system, the standing waves are given bty

E(tot) = 2Esin(kx)cos<phase difference>

and so give a zero intensity at the anti-nodes (at least, according to the Lippmannn model). So, where does the bulk index change come from with no wet processing?
