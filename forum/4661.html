I'm sorry, I'm joking. Your request took me back to the earliest days of Applied Holographics (I was employee #20 or so). Hamish Shearer ran a stall in a street market in London selling hoklograms ca 1981, I believe. When he found how well they were selling, he though to cash in on this and so created Applied Holographics. However, one problem was the intricate method of making a hologram and the length of time it too. I wasn't around this early on, but I was told he considered scanning. Eventually he invented the holocopier. This was a machine that had an inbuilt pulse laser and sheet film fed by two rollers. The object or H1 was put in a cavity, the rollers fed the film into position and the pulse ruby zapped it. They claimed they could do 10's of thousand in a day this way.

Anyway, about scanning large format holograms from an H1. I think you may face problems. The scan has got to have a point of origin. thus at the extreme ends there will be an angular change of the scanning beam from the middle. This will cause the scanning reconstruction beam to be off-Bragg at the ends. I suspect you'll get vignetting.

It may be different, depending on the parameters. if you matched the beam divergence of the original reference beam to the scan angle exactly it may get an image. Dave wanted to arbitrarily scan a large hologram from what I understand. Also, if the original reference was collimated you wouldn't be able to scan it. You could always use an x-y scan, again with the proviso that you match the original reference. I assume you mean by that a plotter with a laser instead of a pen. Remember, however, that the wavefront and beam profile of an expanded beam, diverging or collimated, used to record any small point on the H1 is not the same as the wavefront and intensity profile of the scanning raw beam used to reconstruct it at that same point. In essence, the scanning raw beam woould have to simulate the portion of the wavefront that any given point on the H1 saw at the time of recording. If it didn't, every point reconstructed by the raw beam would suffer aberrations due to mismatch between the (spherical) wavefront of the raw beam and the (roughly planar, but possibly "tilted") wavefront of the expanded beam at any point on the H1. Also, due to the gaussian nature of the raw beam, every point on the H1 would be reconstructed by an individual gaussian (due to the raw beam). The mismatch may be small if the H1 is small, but since Dave specified a large H1, the mismatch may be non-trivial..

If the line is thin, this may work. As the line gets thicker, any given line on the H1 is reconstructed by a cylindrical wavefront while being recorded with a planar wavefront (as an approximation, it holds over a line whether the original ref was collimated or diverging). The phase mismatch will again create aberrations.
