Dinesh Tue Aug 17, 2004 1:50 pm #2
Unless I see what you mean by "fuzzy", I'm not sure. It could be several reasons. Possibilities are:

Broad source size/image too far in front. You may be looking at it with a large source and also, it's in front of the image plane more than behind it. The larger the apparent source of the reconstruction the fuzzier the image seems. The "fuzz factor" increases with the depth of the hologram and seems to be worse for images in front of the image plane than behind it. I'm not certain why this is. My belief is that the eye has no "reference boundary" to define the scene and so can't resolve it. I've tried to see images in front of the image plane with a broadish light source but in a mirror, the idea being that the edges of a mirror provide this reference boundary. It seems that the image clears up when you do this. You may also be too close to the light source. The closer you are the larger the filament seems to the hologram, even for a halogen.

Insufficent washing. You may have carried over a solution into another solution because the hologram wasn't washed well enough. Fixer may causes an overall white/yellow haze due to the fixer releasing sulphur compounds in acidic solution, such as a bleach.

Noise and scatter/corrupted debveloping solutions. Something in one of the developing solutions that shouldn't have been there oozed into the emulsion and is causing a lot more scatter than usual. This could also occur if you mixed solutions without meaning to, ie you didn't wash out a tray holding fix well enough but then used that tray for the developer. It's also possible that the water you used may have a different pH than usual. You can test this by looking at the emulsion through the other side. If the emulsion is scattering more than usual, then looking at it through the glass will cause the reconstruction beam to travel less into the hologram before image light comes out. Is there a sort of glow to the image? Loss of contrast?

I'm assuming you're talking Silver. If you're talking DCG, the DCG could be too soft, due to too early use or too little fixing.

Reticulation. For some reason (too exuberant use of hair dryer?) the emulsion has minute cracks all over it. This usually occurs when you overstress the emuslion by too aggressively drying it. Putting it in alcohol or water and trying to reconstruct may help diagnose this. Sometimes, the water or alcohol "fills in" into the cracks and the holograms becomes less fuzzy.

Bad plates?

Dinesh Tue Aug 17, 2004 5:09 pm #4
It seems to me that you have a mismatch between the shooting geometry of the H1 and the reconstruction geometry. Assuming that you used the same wavelength to both shoot and reconstruct, it sounds like either your original reference or your reconstruction is skewed. If you're using a collimating mirror, then it's possible that the pinhole or lens were off-kilter and the beam hit the mirror off-center. The beam may have looked collimated but may have been twisted.This would be true of both the reference or reconstruction. That is, one or the other was twisted. If the ref or recon were not collimated, there could be mismatch between the point of origin and twist of the shooting and reconstruction of the H1. It's also possible that the plate (film) was twisted while being shot and is straight while being reconstructed. This is sort of unlikely, though. It's more likely a mismatch between H1 reference and H1 reconstruction.

The coefficant of astigmatism is given by (assuming the object and reconstruction are in the x-z plane) :

A = ((x_p^2)/z_p^3)) - ((mu(x_o^2/z_o^3)) + ((mu(x_r^2/z_r^3)) - ((x_i^2/z_i^3))

where:

x_p, z_p = x and z co-ord of reconstruction source

x_r, z_r = x and z coord of reference point (infinity, if collimated)

x_o, z_o = x and z co-ord of object point

x_i, z_i = x and z coord of image point

mu = shooting lambda/reconstruction lambda (ratio of shooting to reconstructing wavelengths. 1 if the same)

Assuming that mu = 1, there's a square law dependance on the x positions of the shooting reconstruction and an inverse cube law dependance on the z co-ordinate. this means a small mismatch in horizontal distance can have a pretty dramatic effect.
Dinesh Tue Aug 17, 2004 6:52 pm #5
One thing I forgot to mention, if the H1 is astigmatic, which it sounds like it is, then the H2 image focuses on two planes. Dinesh Wed Aug 18, 2004 1:17 pm #7
The x and z directions are the backward and forward (z) and side to side (x), by convention. Holographic systems are analysed by placing a co-ordinate system so that the x and y axes ar on the surface of the plate, with the (0,0) origin at the center of the plate. Thus anything on the plate itself can be described by an x coordinate and a y coordinate. Anything outside the plate needs all three coordinates. To make life simpler, one of the three coords is sometimes ignored. So, if on the plate, the y coord goes up and the x coord goes side to side, then all the pertinent points, source of reference point, image point, and reconstruction point are all assumed to have only x and z coords. In other words, all the various sources are on the same level. This way, since nothing is above or below this level, we ignore the y coord. In the equation, the reference is assumed to be coming from a point source placed at coords (x_r, z_r), ie a distance z_r away from the plate and x_r to one side. Similarly for all the other coords. For no astigmatism, the entire expression

A = ((x_p^2)/z_p^3)) - ((mu(x_o^2/z_o^3)) + ((mu(x_r^2/z_r^3)) - ((x_i^2/z_i^3)) = 0

If A is not 0, then there is astigmatism coming from the system.

However, there's no need to dig into the formula if you already know you have astigmatism. You just need to find it's source. When you hit a lens or mirror with an expanding beam so as to collimate it, or just focus it somewhere else, the lens or mirror has to be orientated at right angles to the incoming beam. In this way, the axis of the lens, the object point and the focused point (or the axis of the collimated beam) are on the same straight line. If the lens or mirror is twisted, the incoming light to it will hit it at an angle and the focus point will not lie on the center line but will focus off it. The direction of the twist determines where the focus point wil be. If there is twist in one direction only, you get coma. If the twist is in both directions, you get coma and astigmatism. Thus, if you have a collimating mirror orientated off-axis so that the spatial filter is expanding the beam to the mirror, but the mirror is twisted off to an angle so as to hit the plate, you'll get coma and/or astigmatism. The greater the twist, the greater the coma/astigmatism. There are off-axis paraboloid mirrors available that are designed to counteract this coma/astigmatism, but they're bloody expensive! However, usually a twist of about 20 degrees or less is not noticeable on most display holograms. Some of the more experienced holographers may disagree, but usually you can get away with a less than 20 degree twist. Any more than that and you'll get astigmatism. However, if the axis of the spatial filter is not on the same line as the center of the collimator, there'll definitely be pretty noticeable astigmatism. So if the raw beam were hitting the mirror off-center, but it doesn't seem so, because the spatial filter is not along the raw beam but is also slightly twisted, the H1 ends up astigmatic.

You can use a technique of Tom B's to put a lens into the collimated beam so as to focus on a white plate in the H1 plateholder. If you can't get a focused spot then you're probably getting astigmatism or coma. I'd fiirst check that the collimator were not set at too high an angle. Then I take the spatial filter off and check the raw beam is hitting the center of the collimator. Then I'd re-align spatial filter and pinhole.

Dinesh Wed Aug 18, 2004 3:13 pm #9
If you were using a flat mirror for the H1 ref, presumably the beam was diverging. Presumably to reconstruct the H1 pseudoscopic, you must be converging it somehow. Perhaps it's in the optics used to converge the reconstruction beam. Aberrations come from a mismatch between the reference and the reconstruction. There's no inherent aberration in the hologram, it's in the making and/or replay. If the reconstruction beam is also uncollimated and diverging, then there are going to be other aberrations, such as barrel distortions. The reconstruction beam needs to be a conjugate of the original reference. If the original reference is diverging the recon should converge through the same angle that the original reference was diverging. If the expanding lens is slightly off, then any aberation due to lens twisting may be exaggerated by using a diverging beam for both reference and reconstruction.
