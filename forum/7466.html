As i mentined earlier, the stumbng block to UV sensitivity for silver halide mateials is not the crystals, whihc see UV just fine, but the attenuation of the UV by the binder, gelatin. It may take more exposure to get usable densitites, however all the exposure may be at the surface of the layer and not much lower down.

The other problem, of maintaining reasonable DE, is that the scatter will go sky high, as it is proportional to the inverse sixth power of wavelength, and 360 although not too far from the visible, will scatter quite nastily, lowering fringe contrast liek crazy during recording.

> Glenn: Why is it that holographic film is wavelength-specific right down to the nm?Howis it?It's almost as if the film is some kind of freaky interference filter or something, even though it's mostly silver halide xtals that, under ordinary circumstances, would see service as Kodak 35mm slide film.Weird.

Glenn I think you have not got some fundamentals straight. The film is only crudely wavelength specific. A film intended for use at say 633nm will also usually manage to record at 532nm given plenty of exposure. (However the reverse situation may not be true ----a dye used to give sensitivity to AgBr grains at 532nm may have no absorption at all at 633 nm.) There is intrinsic photosensitivity in nano AgBr crystals to the far blue or UV but this can actually be stopped by the dyes used to give sensitivity to the longwavelength region. I just use my own home made silver halide stuff WITHOUT sensitizing dyes to record at 355nm.
