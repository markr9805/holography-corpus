Ed Wesly Wed Apr 11, 2007 2:28 pm #28
This is an amazing paper! Hats off to this guy!

Believe it or not, I was thinking of ferric ammonium citrate being used as a holographic recording material, but as an amplitude material. As part of the photo-history class I teach, I demonstrate two of the processes that are mentioned in Bjoengaard's paper, cyanotype and gum printing, or gum bichromate as it was called in the 19th century.

When I was making the cyanotype exposure, I was thinking that it would be interesting to coat a plate with the cyanotype chemistry and expose it. The bright fringes would be represented by potassium ferrocyanide, Prussian Blue, the end result of the cyanotype reaction, and the dim fringes by clear glass, if the material had the right kind of resolution. And there would be no reason not to think so. But it would still be an amplitude hologram, so it might not be all that bright. And if it had been exposed to a blue laser, the blue in the recorded fringe system might not be so absorptive so efficiency would be even lower. So I put that on the back burner.

But a couple classes later we made the gum bichromate prints, and as usual you feel a little guilty carting around dichromate. The though occured to me while working with that system would be to coat a plate with the gum arabic and dichromate and hold the pigment, and it might make a dandy surface relief grating in the transmission mode, a poor man's substitute for photo-resist!

For exposing both of these od processes, I used an overhead projector as the light source. Objects and negatives were placed on the platen, and the coated paper over it. Typical times for the cyanotype were 1/2 hour, and about half that for the gum! There probably isn't that much blue and UV coming out of the bulb, even though it is a 300 Watter!

The formulation I used for the cyanotype is the same as the Photographers Formulary kit, and is great fun to play with. The bichromate formula I used was from William Crawford's The Keepers of Light, a great repository of workable alternative photographic processes.

This looks like a promising avenue of research!

I have done an after-dinner -experiment following up Sergio's suggestion.

The plan being to take an uncrosslinked gelatin coating , then

1) treat it with CHILLED 4% ferric chloride solution,

[Ferric chloride tetrahydrate from Sigma Aldrich is reasonably cheap and

unnasty]

2) I then blowed it with cold air till touch dry, then put it in an oven at

about 90-100C for half an hour

3) Then gave it a good rinse in cold {should be chilled } DI. to remove any unreacted iron salt..

(I noted that the crosslinking power of Fe(III) is pretty low compared to say Cr(III) or even alum. This was judged by the fact that water rinsing

at only 28C was inclined to make it break up).

However keeping it chilled at ~10C I then proceeded to the next step of the plan.

This was to soak it in a chilled solution made of

50ml 25% TMG acetate at pH 7. and 25ml 0.4% methylene blue in water.

for a minute then to blow it in cold air till touch dry. At this point I realized that the plate was too sticky and that I had too much TMG acetate.

Nevertheless I continued on to see whether the principal would work.

I knew it was too sticky probably to record stable holofringes so I exposed it through a mask of some lettering to a 20 mw 633 HeNe beam

spread to about 2.5 inch diameter. I was impressed by the fact that it was completely bleached by 1 min exposure and noticeably bleached

in only 15 seconds exposure.

Now the question was did this bleach area reduce the Felll to Fe II and

thereby uncrosslink the gelatin !?.

The answer was ...........

YES!

I know this because when I put the plate in chilled water

the bleached areas strangely initially went blue and disappeared but with continued rinsing

the blue was all washed out and I was left with the light struck areas much more swollen than the dark unexposed areas. So what this was in effect was a potential "positive" working system! which softens with light as opposed to a negative working system like DCG which hardens with light.

I anticipate with this system that TEA could be substituted for TMG

as in effect TEA acetate, allbeit with lower photosensitivity.

The feeble crosslinking of the iron is improved by dipping it into 5% hydrogen peroxide after exposure (see the CHIBA paper referencein this thread.) However it was getting too late to continue and I retired for some shut-eye. All the same it was an interesting experiment and I may

follow it up. I thought if some of you took the idea up too we could all evolve

a novel "holographyforum" paper---- on a "dichromate alternative" maybe.

Jeff

Thank you Jeff to put your hands on.

I have the felling that the mechanism is a superb gelatin decrosslinking. I would suggest primary use a very hardened gelatin layer that indeed still hydrophilic will absorb the water solution.

This is a positive system but you may after all develop as DCG system because the end product is the same crosslinked gelatin with one fringe phase.

I would suggest keep the system simple as did with Jeff experiment, because we could use the oxygen in our favour.

It would not be necessary use H2O2 as development system because, with the use of methylene blue (MB) you are dealing with a recycling synergistic mechanism:
