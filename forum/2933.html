Tom B. Fri Dec 03, 2004 6:56 am #2
The problem is that each point on the hologram requires light from the entire object. You could either continuously illuminate the entire object while scanning the reference beam over the plate or vice versa, but it would not be an improvement over the traditional method.

As far as I know, scanning is really only useful for contact hologram copying. Saxby briefly describes it in "Practical Holography". Usually the beam is expanded into a line with a cylinder lens and scanned in one direction over the master/copy sandwich. This greatly reduces stability requirements and problems with film reciprocity failure.

> There is a nice paper, "Scanning holography and its applications", by Qu, Feng, Wesly, Jeong, SPIE 1600. Maybe Ed Wesly could elaborate on that...

Dr. Zhimin Qu was inspired by Larry Lieberman's talk on contact copy replication via scanning, so he tried to make holograms off of objects by scanning a slit and even an undiverged beam over the holo plate with the object underneath, Denisyuk style.

Yes it did work, but the viewing angle was strange. The millimeter or so wide beam would illuminate the part of the object directly underneath it, so it was like looking through a slit at the object, as the object didn't light up all at once.

But you could even make the exposures hand holding the plate and scanning the laser by hand! And you could even make a hologram of a real hand!

So don't be afraid to try it!
Dinesh Fri Dec 03, 2004 4:36 pm #6
Scanning works for small objects very close to the plate as a single beam Denisyuk. If you hit an large-ish object with a small point of light, the light will illuminate (reference) a small part of the plate but will go on to illuminate a large part of the object. So, now this small part of the plate, a circle of approx 1/2 mm, will image a large part of the object, say a circle of about 2mm on the object. If you now move on to the next small part of the plate the same thing happens. However, the "next small part" will illuminate a part of the object that the previous scan spot also illuminated. Now you have two scanned spots with each scan spot imaging an overlapped part of the object. This overlap of the object will go on for every scanned spot. If the object were very close to the plate so that the scan spot only illumnates a spot on the object as large as the scan spot on the plate, it'll work.

[quote:95e367682e="Ed Wesly"] Yes it did work, but the viewing angle was strange[/quote:95e367682e]

It would be. Consider each scan spot as it's own hgologram and you have a sort of "bee's eye" view of the total hologram with a viewing aperture equal to the spot size for each facet of the bee's eye. That's assuming that the scan was linear. If the scan was angular so that the scanner was trotated as it scanned the plate, you'd have all the usual distortions of replaying a hologram made with a diverging beam using collimated light - the rolling effect - multiplied on each hologram of the bees eye view.
