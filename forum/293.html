rzeheb Thu Aug 04, 2011 6:08 pm #1
So I had this idea to shoot 4 essentially identical reflection holograms (using 4" X 5" VRP-M plates, 532 nm light, Kodak D19 developer, & ferric EDTA bleach) and then artificially shifting the playback colors so that I had 1 pane, each, in blue, green, yellow and red. The four would be mounted together as a group for display. I have no problem shifting to longer wavelengths which I do by soaking the finished (developed and bleached) hologram in solutions of citric acid whose concentrations I empirically determined would give a pleasing yellow color and a somewhat less pleasing red color to the finished, dried hologram. However, now I am faced with shifting to shorter wavelength playback to achieve blue. The problem is that I have already shot, developed, bleached and dried the plate so pre-swelling prior to exposure is no longer possible (unless I want to shoot a new plate which is a possible plan B). Does anyone on this board have any suggestions for a solution that I can use on my already developed plate that might gently remove some material allowing shrinkage which might shift me to blue? I have several other green holograms that I would be able to sacrifice for the cause of testing and fine tuning the concentration of this magic solution. TIA, Ron.
ron olson Sun Aug 07, 2011 9:51 am #2
My answer comes more in the way of a question. On a recent 15" x 15" silver halide plate I observed the following: following colloidal developing (which I always use with Agfa materials) and Blythe colour-tuning (in this case 70 grams of citric acid in 1 liter water) I arrived from green at my desired amber (~590nm) but alas, I had a color blemish at the border. I allowed the plate to dry overnight and then resoaked the plate in water before making a second attempt at red-shifting. To my surprise (actually delight) I noticed the plate had turned a very attractive (almost achromatic) blue.

It seemed to be remaining stable (following several days) so I painted it as per usual and now have a nice blue image which I would estimate to be at ~490nm. Maybe Jeff or Dinesh can explain the why.

Well, if you look up the work of Northrop and Kunitz :

THE SWELLING OF GELATIN AND THE VOLUME OF

SURROUNDING SOLUTION. BY JOHN H. NORTHROP AND M. KUNITZ.

(From the Laboratories of The Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research,

Princeton, N. J.)

And also Krishnamurti's Nature paper:

"Mechanism of the Swelling of Gels"

K. KRISHNAMURTI nature 123, 242-243 (16 February 1929)

You'll see that the swelling of gelatin is an osmotic process, depending on the concentration and pH of the acid and the concentration of the gelatin (and the pH, according to the Rockefeller paper).

If you use colloidal development, you reduce the density of the gelatin. When you washed it you further reduced the density of the gelatin (by washing it) and then you put it back in the citric acid, an acidic solution with a relatively high pH. I suspect osmotic pressure then caused the gelatin to shrink.

Ed Wesly Tue Aug 09, 2011 7:10 am #10
Sodium Sulfite definitely is a silver halide solvent. See Bjelkhagen, Silver Halide Materials for Holography, p.147, et seq. I couldn't find mention of the solution I know he used to shrink reflection holograms to shorter wavelengths in the book, but I am pretty sure that it was 10% or so, with a long time in the solution.

Sulfite is a pretty amazing additive to photo processes. It acts a preservative by capping oxygen dissolved in water by changing to the sulfate state. It is a clearing bath after reversal bleaches in B&W photography, hardly ever see it used after the Pyrochrome type bleaches because of its silver solvent capabilities. It inhibits the tanning effects of Pyrogallol and Pyrocatechol. It dissolves the anti-halation backing of many films. It provides the activator or accelerator for phenidone in the POTA developer used on the above-mentioned Tech Pan film, which also keeps grain size down as it does in other high acutance fine-grain photographic developers. There's a ton of it in B&W paper developers to keep fog down and control contrast.

Buy it by the pound!

You're suggesting two different spikes at different frequencies, as opposed to a bandwidth? If that was so, one of the two wavelengths must be blue, since Ron claims there's a component that he estimates at 490.

> ron olson: I noticed the plate had turned a very attractive (almost achromatic) blue.It seemed to be remaining stable (following several days) so I painted it as per usual and now have a nice blue image which I would estimate to be at ~490nm. Maybe Jeff or Dinesh can explain the why.

Also, the blue component must be quite strong, since the eye is fairly insensitive to blue. This would indicate a shrinkage. I suppose it all revolves around the use of the word "achromat". If the bandwidth were 490 +/- 15, then there would be a green-ish component which, when mixed with the 475 component, would give an "achromatic" appearance. However, if two complementary colours such as red and blue-green were diffracted at narrow bandwidths, this also would give an achromatic appearance. However, I'd think one of the two complementary colours would have to be blue or blue green, ie below the 532 he shot with. In either situation, it appears there must have been a shrinkage.

Ed's conjecture that the silver halide was dissolved by sodium sulphite would indicate that about 8% of material were dissolved, assuming that the bandwidth of the post-processed hologram were equal to the bandwidth after initial processing. However, it seems to me that it would depend on the kind of bleach Ron used. If Ron used a rehalogenating bleach, would not the sulphite dissolve both "image" halide and "non-image " simultaneously? This would result in a loss of efficiency. However, if he used a reversal bleach, then the halide in the non-actinic areas - those areas where the halide still exists after bleaching - would be dissolved. But, would this not destroy the entire image, since this is all there is left to carry the image?
