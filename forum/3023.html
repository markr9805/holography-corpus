Dinesh Tue Jan 11, 2005 9:59 pm #6
Lots of possibilities.

First, you need a 1:1 ratio. Problem with single beam Denisyuks is that you can't control ratio. Whatever bounces off the object is what the ratio is. You can, however, help things along a little. The two beams have to interact. It's no good if the object beam and the reference beam don't "se" each other. So, I tend not to like shiny objects because the specular reflection bonces off the object and not into the reference beam. Imagine a coin parallel to the plate with a reference coming in at 60 degrees. The specular reflection off the coin goes downward and misses the incoming reference. If you do use a specular object, orientate it so that the specular reflection reflects back into the reference. If you use a diffuse object, the scattered light does scatter back into the reference. However, again, check that most of the reflection is back onto the reference. Another possibility is to have a mirror off the plate (or film) holder pointing toward the object and widen the reference beam so it hits the mirror. The mirror will catch the spillover from the reference and redirect more light onto the object. For this to work you need the coherence length, which I believe the Integraf laser has. Again, check that the "spillover" reflection does not glance off the sides of the plate but reflects onto the reference.

You need to process very, very dark. You may not be taking it dark enough. If you use a safelight, you should process it so that the safelight is almost invisible. Note: "almost". You can overdevelop and go too far the other way. If you're familiar with the terminology, you need an OD of about 2.0.

Your best bet is to illuminate the setu and look at it from the "other" side, ie the reference side. What you see is what the refreence sees. If you see zilch, the refrence also sees zilch.

Exposure. Do exposure tests. Make a mask so that you can expose different parts of the film with different exposures. This will give you a good idea.

[quote:d0200d5b7a]I've been using red subjects[/quote:d0200d5b7a]

Don't. Use white objects. The reason is that the "red" of your objects may not have any significant value of "red" at your laser wavelength.

I too am a beginner and I have found out the following:

Aside from beam ratio and reflectivity of the subject, I have found out that the proper angle of illumination of the film (I am using pfg-01 film, held together by two pieces of glass), has a great effect on clarity of the hologram.

I now use the Brewster's angle (56 degrees angle with respect to the normal of the film holder). An easy way to make sure you have the correct angle is as follows: The ratio is y/x=1.48

This means: If the laser points down from a height of 15cm, the plate needs to be 10cm away from the place exactly downwards from the laser. You can use a piece of glass to check the reflection back onto the ground surface.

Another thing that can help clarity, I have found is to block any licht hitting the ground surface BEFORE the plate. This is light that is of no use to the hologram and is just illuminating your plate unnecessarily.

It made a great difference to my holograms. Hope it will do the same for you.
MichaelH Wed Aug 31, 2005 5:50 pm #8
Another method to find the BA is this from Frank D at holoworld.com

> You will need a polarized laser and make sure that the polarization is properly oriented to your plateholder. You will also need to bring your reference beam (or single beam) in at "exactly" brewsters angle. One way to determine this is to set up your plateholder at brewsters and place a single piece of glass in it. Hit the glass with your spread beam. The glass is going to reflect some of the light hitting it, so place a white card in this reflected light path (in order to view it). If you rotate your laser head, you will notice that this reflected light becomes brighter and dimmer. Find the spot within the rotation where the reflected light is at it's dimmest on your white card, and you've got it. There should be two spots for this with every 360-degree of rotation. With my HeNe, it is at the 3:00 and 9:00 position(s) for side-reference (parallel to table). With the diode, it is at 12:00 and 6:00. With a HeNe, you'll always have a "little" bit of light reflected. With a properly-running diode, the reflection will go completely out on the card (100% -- or VERY close to 100% -- transmission through the glass).For side referencing, also make sure that your plateholder is not angled toward or away from the incoming laser light, too. It should be straight up and down in relation to the reference beam. This also means making sure that your incoming laser light stays parallel to the surface of the table . . . and is not directed upwards or downwards in any way toward the plateholder during it's travel. If you're using an overhead reference, then it should not be angled in any way from either SIDE. In other words, the ONLY "angle" present should be the reference angle -- no matter what table geometry or set-up you're using.
