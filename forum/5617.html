Ron Michael Sat Jun 10, 2006 1:07 am #2
Several sources refer to the use of a telescope in the resonator to increase mode size in the active medium. Additionally published in "Proceedings of the IEEE" Apr 1965 and again in April 1972 both papers by P. W. Smith indicated the use of a cat's eye resonator which is two flat mirrors with a convex lens whose focal length is 1/2 the distance between the mirrors and is placed at the half way point with an aperture at one mirror and the laser medium at the other mirror. According to Li and Smith they reported 2.5 times the output power than just from an aperture alone. This arrangement basically forms a confocal resonator with large mode volume in the medium. Back in 1965 this was done with a HENE laser. Of course in both approaches you must consider the thermal lensing that can happen from a solid rod. During q switch operation this lens can be negative during this inital pumping. Depending on pump levels this lensing can become pronounced. So the use of telescopes or convex lenses must be calculated with the lensing of the rod. Try sending a hene beam down your amplifier rod and measure the beam spread before and after to calculate your negative lensing at the power levels you want to use.

Ron Michael Sat Jun 10, 2006 1:26 pm #6
Colin is right, you do need to utilize the amplifier volume as much as you can without causing too much diffraction due to small rod diameter to beam diameter. In the early stages try and keep 2x larger aperture of the rod than gaussian mode cross-section of your beam since even small diffraction ripple will get magnified by later amplifiers. In the last amplifier you can allow it to get closer to 1x and only pickup some spatial modulation. Your low power is a combination of several factors. You have less output power from your oscillator. your energy extraction from your amplifier is somewhat less due to the smaller beam size. the KTP is also losing efficiency due to the lower beam density because the beam energy is now reduced. But the energy is now more concentrated within the smaller beam size so it's not as much loss as you think. Still for best efficiency you need to get up to 200MW/cm2 power density for KTP without going over since that reduces your conversion efficiency and raises possible damage to the crystal. So back up and measure your oscillator output. Work out arrangements like telescopes if needed for your amplifiers to utilitze the maximum volume staying within the general aperture rule and finally you may need to reduce your beam size before going into the ktp to get your power density where you want. Of course in my designs I have used these techniques for testing and gathering data, I have found that the right combination of amplifers, distance, negative thermal lensing, can all be used to your advantage and reduce your component count. Flat top and image relaying are techniques that can be used but again add components and in the far field add problems as well in holography. So in the end a good TEM00 gaussian propagation generally gives you the best intensity distribution at the plate. In the lab where I've produced holographic gratings made from UV, we just expand it beyond what we need to get a more even illumination at the plate. If you want good quality holograms with higher fringe contrast (very important with HOE like the above gratings) you want a single polarized illumination field which TEM00 mode and a single axial mode provide.

Dear Holographers,

I found that dirty optics can force your laser out of TEM00 mode into higher order modes, which is unwanted. The central portion of the rod is most important to keep clean so that TEM00 is not suppressed since it maximizes in the center of the Gaussian beam.

I ordered some solution, tissue, and stuff from Edmund Optical and Ebay. I learned about the Drop and Drag technique of cleaning optics. You place the flat optic on a clean tissue. Lay another on the optic and place a drop of cleaning solution (methanol was suggested in one source) to wet the tissue and underlying optic, and drag the paper laterally across the optic bringing new fresh tissue surfaces across the optic as it dries and picks up the dirt. You may need to hold the optic from sliding.

I can see some spots or dust in the SSY-1 lasers I have been using to make the holograms, by shining strong white light through the cavity. I will see if cleaning the optics improves the beam quality.

Sincerely,

Bill Jensen

JohnFP Thu Jun 15, 2006 12:39 pm #10
I have never been a fan of that type of cleaning method. The reason is, let say you have a piece of dirt, sand etc that is on the optic at the furthest locaton from the direction you will be pulling the tissue. Then in essence you will be pulling the potentially abrasive piece of dirt across the entire surface thus scratching it.

I always preferred the roll method with a Q-tip for small optics. I get the good Q-tips as they are wound better and don't have loose threads coming off. I then put the Q-tip in the cleaning solution of choice and give it a gentle shake once. I then place the Q-tip at one edge of the object and very lightly pull it across the optic while, and this is the most importand part, rolling the Q-tip. In essence what I am trying to do is if there is a piece of grit the rolling technique lifts that piece of grit off the surface and does not drag it across the surface. It is important to only roll the Q-tip less then 360 degrees or you will be back to a potentially dirty spot.
JohnFP Thu Jun 15, 2006 12:47 pm #11
I want to stress that the way you roll the Q-tip is up in the front with relation to the direction of movement. In other words, the leading edge is being rotated up.
