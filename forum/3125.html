Colin Kaminski Sun Feb 13, 2005 4:28 pm #2
You are looking for the best image (nice contrast, low noise, etc.) not the brightest image in an H1. A bright H1 will make the transfer easier with a shorter exposure time but you can adjust the beam ratio anyway. So if you have a great image on a very low efficiency plate you need to have more Object beam and less reference beam and a longer exposure durring copy. This makes it possible to make great H2s from some rather difficult H1 objects.

BobH Sun Feb 13, 2005 5:39 pm #4
I would suggest:

a) if the only purpose for the hologram is as an H1, decrease the beam ratio (more ref) so that the plate gets proper development with a 40s exposure. The downside is that the H2 may be a bit noisier.

b) expose for 40s and develop for more time.

c) expose for 40s and develop at a higher temperature.

d) expose for 40s and develop in a more aggressive developer.

e) be certain all sources of audio noise is minimized; especially that caused by fans, plumbing, or anything else of a regular nature. The object will not move unless something causes it to move.

f) eliminate any source of air currents within the system by total enclosure. Load the plate, close the box and wait at least 5 minutes before opening the shutter.

g) plastic models are glued together at a certain room temperature. Make the exposure room temperature the same so that the plastic parts are not under stress during the exposure.

h) if the model is mounted on the table vertically (pointing up), it may be under stress because it was assembled horizontally.

Dinesh Sun Feb 13, 2005 6:05 pm #6
I also suspect beam ratio and development time. Increase the beam ratio (for a crazy moment I thought that Bob had been taken over by aliens !) You say that as you increased exposure various parts ended up brighter. Were these the same parts or did different parts "light up"? If it were motion then the parts that were dark at 80 secs would be darker still at 300 secs. If different parts were brighter at the various exposures, it may mean that the increased exposure is increasing the parts that had a lower object reflectivity. Also, you don't say how dense you developed it. Did you take it to the same density for all the exposures, or the same time of development. How long did it take for the film to go dark? It needs to start to go dark at about 20 secs and develop to about 50% dark in about 2.5 - 3 mins. If, at 300 secs, it went instantly dark you're probably overexposing. On the other hand, if it took a long time (>40/50 secs) to go dark at 40 secs, then you were probably underexposing.

I personally would not suspect motion. If you shot it 15 hrs after mounting, either you have a constantly moving table, in which no hologram will come out (do a Michaelson), or you're object never stopped moving (attach a mirror on the object and do a Michaelson), or you're using a laser powerful enough to heat the object/glue as you shoot. Unless you're using a high power Ar or Kr, I think this is unlikely. Bob's point of good baffling is important. The higher you're level of optics (plate center, mirror center etc) the greater is the effect of air currents and the greater is the need for good baffling. If you do have a high optical axis, you may want to get it a bit lower.

It sounds like you don't have a light meter. If you can, buy a solar cell and cheap volt (and amp) meter from your local electronics shop. It won't be calibrated for light readings but you can calibrate it yourself and end up with something usable to help you determine your exposure times.

Dinesh Sat Feb 19, 2005 4:34 pm #11
[quote:6ebca94f31="HoloM"]So what I don't understand is, if it is motion, why is the motion one week later the same?[/quote:6ebca94f31]

Motion does not always "become quiet" after a certain amount of time. This depends on the kind of motion. Remember that it needs only move by about 0.5 microns to go dark. For example a large, flat,rectangular, metallic object is mounted by gluing on one edge. The other edge is unsupported. Now normal thermal variations in the room will cause the unsupported end to constantly sway around the glued edge. The sway may be very, very small - maybe 200 - 300nm - but it will be constant and enough to cause a dark image. An object may not be mounted at it's center of gravity. Now there is a moment of force (I don't think "moment" is the right word but I can't think of it,. When there are two equal forces acting at different points causing a twisting action). Anyway, gravity is trying to twist the object down while the restoring force at the mounted point is trying to twist it up. There's a constant battle between gravity pulling down and the hinge point pulling up. One example is a car monted by a rod attached to the front. The center of gravity of the car is in the middle so there's a <moment> between the hinge point of attachment and the weight of the car. This will cause the car to constantly go up and then down by again a few nm. Another problem may arise with rubber or plastic . Rubber or plastic is in a constant state of tension and never completely stops. If any of these is the case, attach the object at a different point or attach a nice solid, rigid support (like a piece of glass) at the back.

Joy has just suggested another possibility. The object may be depolarising at the dark areas. To test this, look at the object through a poariser and rotate the polariser. If the parts that are dark are also dark in the holographic image, this may be the problem.

If you posted a picture of the object and it's mounting, it may be possible to see what your problem may be.
