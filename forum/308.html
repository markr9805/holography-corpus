> rzeheb: For the last couple of years, I have been focused almost exclusively on making image plane holograms.

> rzeheb: So recently, as a change of pace, I started making split beam reflection holograms.

So, the flat holograms were holograms of flat objects? In a single beam Denisyuk geometry? Now you're using an H1/H2 set and your images have some volume to them?

It sounds like just that any image blurs as you go away from the image plane (The "plate"), depending on the size of the reconstruction source.

When the image has a significant volume, consider the image to be made up of a very large number of single point sources. When you make the hologram, there is a 1:1 relationship between the position of the point on the object and of the image (assuming no aberrations). Thus, every object point is precisely positioned at a point in space. When you reconstruct the hologram with a laser, every image point will be reconstructed at exactly the same position. The reason it does this, is that the laser is considered a true point source, the laser light is emitted from an aperture of zero dimension (This is not actually true, but you're hologram would have to bloody deep to show blurring in a laser).

Now, when you reconstruct with a non-coherent source - led, halogen, etc - the light source has a dimension whether it's the size of the filament or the size of the led. So, every point on the source will reproduce it's own set of image points. The image points will cluster around each other, so the image point becomes a small circle, rather than a single point. The size of this "circle" will depend on the distance away from the image plane (the "plate"). At the plate itself, there will be no separation of image points, every image point is reconstructed as a point no matter what the source size. As you move away from the plate, the size of the "image circle" gets larger and larger, causing more and more blurring, depending on the size of the reconstructing source. The larger the reconstruction source, the closer to the plate are these circles noticed and the more blurry the image. The flat holograms show no blurring because the image points are all on the image plane so the "image circles" have zero radius.

So, try to reconstruct the image in various sources of different sizes - a halogen,the sun, an led, a fluorescent bulb, etc - and note how blurred the image is. The deeper the image, the smaller must be the source size of the reconstruction source.

Another source of blurring is "noise". This is created when the plate "sees" any light that is not just object light and reference light. Any spurious reflections from posts, metallic objects on the table, edges of mis\rrors etc. Every one of these spurious reflections causes a grating on the plate and these multiple gratings will cause tiny deviations surrounding the image points.

> rzeheb: I further split my object beam so that the subject is illuminated from both the left front and right front sides. Could I be making two holographic images of my subject very close together?

No, not if you use the same reference beam for both lightings.

Jeffrey Weil Mon Sep 05, 2011 9:45 am #5
It sounds like Dinesh is right about your blurriness. If you want to do reflections and get around this problem do some image plane images. That'll get things closer to the surface.

You'll still need a good replay source but it'll be much better. If you have an object that has 4 inches of z depth you'll be, at best, 4 inches away from the surface with the back of the object. Image plane that and the back is now 2 inches from the surface. This is of course only true if you put the image plane right in the middle of the object. You don't have to do it that way but I needed an example.

BTW, image plane reflection holograms are usually considered the Cadillac of display holography. If your all about display holograms you might want to head in that direction even forgetting about the blur problem.

Yes, you need a big master to make them look great, it's harder, you need more optics, it's not nearly as efficient with the laser light. etc......But if you can beat those problems it's worth it.

Jeff Weil

O.K., I ran a couple of tests last night. The results were interesting. First of all, the holograms looked pretty good under laser light, not bad under sunlight, not very good under halogen spotlighting or single LED illumination. This is consistent with Dinesh and Jeffrey's observations about image depth and distance from the plate. BTW, in case anyone was about to ask, I did use collimated light in my reference beam. However, with respect to some of the other factors that were suggested, I shot a second hologram with the following adjustments: (1) I did find, and block, some stray light that was hitting my plate directly. It wasn't very much and I doubt if it had a significant effect but it certainly didn't do anything good either, (2) I shifted the light significantly towards the reference beam and away from the object beams, I think this helped quite a bit and my second hologram was better than the one I had been complaining about, but still not really great. At the end of the day I think the REAL problem was with my choice of subject matter. I was shooting a nice piece of bleached white coral. I thought it would make a good subject. There were arms of coral going out in every direction and, further more, each arm was itself covered with small projections and depressions that made it spatially very interesting. However, when hit with laser light it diffused and dispersed the light so strongly that it almost seemed to be lit from the inside. As a result, a lot of the surface detail (those small projections and pits) seemed to wash out (even when just looking at the object directly rather than at the resulting hologram. I think I was disappointed in my hologram because of my choice of subject matter. Maybe with a lot of fine tuning and tweaking of my illumination I can make it look the way I want, but in the end I don't think the problem was as much with my set-up as I had originally thought.
holorefugee Sun Sep 11, 2011 7:50 pm #8
Don't forget that the reference beam of a reflection hologram needs to match its display light. If you have a pint source for a display light then you need a spatial filter for the reference beam. Collimation is important in H1 work because you need to flip the plate and it keeps you from needing a converging reference for reconstruction.
