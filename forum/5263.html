Sorry, I cannot help myself. I hope I don't bore you all with my questions.

1] Why have most commercial films a thickness of around 7um? I figure that, with reflection recordings, an increased number of planes will replay more in a single wavelength due to destructive interference of the other wavelengths. So would a very thick emulsion be very dim due to replaying in a smaller part of the reconstruting light spectrum?

2] However if that is the case, why do commercial films that are designed for shorter wavelengths not have thinner emulsions in order to keep the number of bragg planes at their optimum?

3] If the replay bandwidth would turn out to be not so important, would it not be benificial to have a special reflection film with a very thick emulsion? That way one could record more planes and have higher diffraction efficiency. Also I could imagine it could then hold more information because of the increased number of AgBr chrystals.

4] I have bought a meyer bar for 8um. It looks like a metal bar with a super fine thread turned into it. I would like to make some for other emulsion thicknesses. I read somewhere you can make them at home with a metal bar and a thin wire wound around it (7 turns per cm). Is it the amount of turns per cm that determines the emulsion thickness or the gauge of the wire, or a combination of both?
JohnFP Tue Dec 13, 2005 9:08 pm #2
You could always run two strips of 1/2 mil tape (clear wrapping tape) down both edges of the plate where the myer bar would rest on the glass. This would raise the windings by 1/2 mil (12.5 microns). Together this would give you about 20 micron thickness. You could get different thickness tapes to increase the thickness of your emulsion.

Just a suggestion. It would allow you to go thicker without purchasing another Myer bar to start.

Interesting questions. The principal issues involved with layer thickness seem to be DE (index modulation), signal/noise ratio and bandwidth.

According to Bjelkhagen:

> Hariharan has shown the existence of an optimal value for the emulsion thickness achieved as a result of a compromise between the ration of peak diffraction efficiency and bandwidth at hologram reconstruction in white light. A thickness between 5 and 7 um seems to be a good compromise according to the paper. Another fact mentioned is that an increase in the emulsion thickness will also cause an increase in the scattering within the emulsion which is due to the finite size of the grains. (Silver-halide recording materials, p.71).

Yes, all those commercial SH emulsions have similar values (Kodak used to sell their 649-F plates in a 17um version though). For reasons I don't quite understand, Slavich sells their DCG plates PFG-04 with a 20um layer.

Oops, I've just had a look at Slavich's Russian site ([http://www.slavich.ru](http://www.slavich.ru)) which provides much more detailed information. I gathered that PFG-03c had a 10.5um layer.

I also wanted to point to the charms of home-coated layers (SH, DCG, photopolymer) when experimenting with different kinds of effects related to layer thickness. This subject might become particularly important in areas like edge lit holography etc.

> Hans: ...............................1] Why have most commercial films a thickness of around 7um? I figure that, with reflection recordings, an increased number of planes will replay more in a single wavelength due to destructive interference of the other wavelengths. So would a very thick emulsion be very dim due to replaying in a smaller part of the reconstruting light spectrum?Yes your intuition is quite right about this, it will look very dim to our eyes if it is extremely narrow band. See below my answer to your question 3.2] However if that is the case, why do commercial films that are designed for shorter wavelengths not have thinner emulsions in order to keep the number of bragg planes at their optimum?Actually on an entirely practical restriction level, there are 2 problems with silver halide emulsions that are several times thicker than “normal” . They are more difficult to process optimally with normal chemistry because the developer and bleaches take different times to get through to the glass side of the layer. The problem seems to be that the reflection hologram can look great on the gelatin side but on the glass side the hologram can look very milky . The reason I can only guess at.--- Possibly the developer carried on developing at the “back” end while the stop bath only worked OK at the front end. Also the bleach did not stop at the back end and encouraged grain growth while it was effectively removed from the front end.The other more subtle problem is that with Denisyuk holograms you need to limit the amount of photosensitising dye or you kill too much light going to and coming back from the object. So an optimum amount of dye for 6-7 micron films may need to be cut down by a factor of (say) 6 for a 50micron film. This reduces photosensitivity. (Normal dye levels can be maintained for transmission holos though.) Some of these problems can be got round by adapting the processing chem.3] If the replay bandwidth would turn out to be not so important, would it not be benificial to have a special reflection film with a very thick emulsion? That way one could record more planes and have higher diffraction efficiency. Also I could imagine it could then hold more information because of the increased number of AgBr chrystals.Yes. The more Bragg planes you have the greater the selectivity and the narrower the bandwidth. Graham Saxby has pointed out to me the similarity of the situation withUHF TV signals and roof aerials. The more the number of those subsidiary bars parallel to the first main bar , the greater the station selectivity. Perhaps one could equate those bars with Bragg planes for radio frequencies. In the 1920’s Indian physicist CV Raman invented a new very useful form of spectroscopy. Now much more practical since the laser was invented.An intense visible laser beam is passed through a liquid chemical in a glass container and at right angles to the beam path buried in the usual Rayleigh scatter there is some very subtle information. The normal twisting, rotating and vibrating goings on of molecules at room temperatures make tiny modifications of the fundamental frequency of the laser beam just likeVHF radio signals are a modulation of the main carrier beam , giving an identical small + and – frequency shift either side of the main carrier beam. The tiny modulation of the main light beam is so close to the main beam that special filters are needed to separate it off , these are in effect very thick reflection holograms with loads of Bragg planes and are known as “notch filters”.I think the US company Physical Optics Corp have for a long time specialized in making these in DCG.
