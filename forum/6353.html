A few months ago, I ran across an old pulsed laser for sale, and couldn't let it pass on by. It's a 1962 Hughes Aircraft Company Model 200 pulsed ruby laser system, in excellent condition and fully operational, and one of the first hundred or so lasers sold by them. I already had a Perkin-Elmer/Spectra-Physics Model 111 Helium-Neon laser head in my collection, one of the first 75 He-Ne lasers sold by Spectra-Physics, but I never thought about trying to make it lase. This old Hughes ruby still works, through 45 year old caps and coatings and with the original helical flashlamp, in essentially the same configuration as Maiman's group originally made. If it were an airplane, this would be a Wright Flyer.

Anyway, I've officially joined the pulsed laser club, at long last but with a twist. I've already made a few transmission hologram gratings with it, and will post here when I get more interesting results. Today, however, I thought I'd poke a hole in a razor blade. Typical thing to do I know, but I had to get it out of my system.

I put the beam into a large aperture 20x microscope objective lens, and focused it onto the blade (Wilkinson Sword, not Gillette, but I was lucky to find one here at all). Charged the caps to 1.3Kv and POP!!! Looking through my laser goggles, I saw white sparks fly both directions away from the blade. I figure it's about 150 microns wide, and offset from the center of the burn pattern quite a bit, but still pretty good for a Kennedy era laser.

Firing this laser is like running an antique car or riding a high-wheeler bike. Not much you can do with it these days. It's kind of sad ... all of its generation did their jobs in basic, exciting research into all kinds of applications for the new kind of light they gave. Most were probably busted in use. Cracked crystals, burned coatings, exploding flashlamps, shorted components in the power supply, blown caps, whatever.

They were the first. People used these and thought, "we gotta make this a better beam" (or something like that), so immediately external cavities with Q-switches and much higher energies were available. These little guys, with their delicate coatings right on the ends of the rod, were probably tossed or otherwise set aside.

Doesn't look like much, about a 6"long by 3" diameter metal cylinder, a power supply about the size of a breadbox and a couple of cables. This one apparently was used in the midwest for laser trimming of resistors. It was bought at a flea market by a collector of ham radio equipment, who had it until he died. I bought it from the one who rescued it from the estate sale.

So I'm going to make some holograms with it, keep it clean, exercise the caps, and hopefully find an appropriate home for it some day. 8)
