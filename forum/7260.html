BobH Sat Apr 05, 2008 3:26 pm #2
That's why I always recommend a much larger pinhole than the calculations would suggest. It takes a very ugly beam indeed to make the plate look bad. The reference beam can be pretty dirty, but the beam ratio variation caused by the nonuniformity of the beam isn't enough to show up in the hologram. Object lighting beams need to be more uniform to avoid seeing the patterns on the object after the hologram is made, because those beams are dimmer than the reference and therefore the variation in beam ratio is greater.

I never had much luck with 100x objectives. The light diverges too much to clear the pinhole mounts on all the spatial filters I've used. Even a 60x is difficult. I recommend a 40x and nice clean AR coated negative lens if the beam needs more divergence.

Tom B. Sun Apr 06, 2008 6:30 am #7
> Dutchelm05: Any reason as to why the hologram actually worked? Why is it I did not see the rings in the results?

Fortunately, display holography is very forgiving about exposure times and beam ratios - you have to be WAY off before any degradation becomes apparent. The rings and such (if not too bad) only cause a slight local variation in beam ratio at the plate, and a complicated 3D object also helps to hide any uneven lighting. If you are shooting an object with smooth contours, (an egg for example) then lighting becomes more critical as it is easier to see beam imperfections which will be faithfully recorded. A diffuser in the object beam can help to reduce these.

JohnFP Tue Apr 08, 2008 7:29 pm #19
> Yes I do John, (8" diameter) but the FL on it is too fast believe. So if I expand my beam say 7 inches, it will focus my beam rather than collimate it. I could move the collimation mirror in of coarse until it collimated but that would result in a small beam diameter.

If your collimator is focusing the beam at a spatial filter (or lens) distance that makes the light hitting the collimator 7" then you need to bring the spatial filter in until the beam after the collimator is not converging or diverging. Once you find this distance, record it (I actually write it on the side of my collimator). This will be the focal length of the collimator and will never change. Always keep your spatial filter at this distance. Then simply go up in power with your objectives until the beam is slightly larger then the collimator. 40x? 100X? The distance from the collimator to the plate/object can be changed without changing the collimated beam diamter.

Since the collimator is so fast you may find the F.L. to be quite short. Maybe 16" or so. This would be geat for your table . Mount the laser up off the table with two cinder blocks at a distance of about 24' from the mirror. Then place two cinder block on each side of the table away from each other with a shelf on top of them about 16" on center from the mirror which is also mounted at the height of the laser beam. The shelf will provide a place to put the spatial filter that expands the beam as the correct FL and then the collimator directs the beam down to the table in between the blocks where the plate and object rests.

Unfortunately I think you have it wrong. As I stated, once you have your spatial filter at the correct FL distance for a collimated reflected beam, you don't change that distance. If the SF distance is left at the FL then changing the objective will only change the diameter of the beam that hits the collimator.
