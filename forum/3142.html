[quote:72a006d264="DannyBruza"] what should the max gel thickness on the plates should be, and if its 10-20 microns what would be problem arise in the gel being that thick

[/quote:72a006d264]

For most of the common materials, emulsion thickness has little to do with material mechanisms. So long as your material can take the spatial frequencies linearly, the effect of the thickness will determine only the bandwidth of the reconstructed image, assuming you're shooting a volume hologram. One effect that may have to do with material properties is the amount of surface profile that arises and the resulting dispersion.

When you shoot in any (volume) geometry, you effectively form two gratings, a volume (Bragg) grating that affects the interior of the emulxion, and a surface profile that alters the surface of the medium. The surface profile creates a dispersive effect that adds colour blur while the volume structure affects the bandwidth (sharpness of colour). If the emulsion is relatively thin (<5m microns) then the surface effect will predominate and you'll get a lot of colour blur. If the emulsion is relatively thick (>20 microns) then the volume effect will predominate and you'll get a "monochromatic" image with little colour blur. However, the statistical distribution of the Bragg planes is wide enough so that a lot of colours will diffract and it can easily be seen in white light. It's not true "monochromatic". As you increase the emulsion depth the spread of the plane seperation narrows down and the reconstruction bandwidth gets narrower and narrower, ie it gets more and more monochromatic. At around 40 microns, the bandwidth gets down to about 30nm, which the eye can just see, at 80-100nm the bandwidth is probably too low to be see-able. Sergio has also brought up the point of intermodulation in another post. If the emulsion is very thick and if the efficency is high enough, as the planes start to form, the planes start diffracting within the structure creating "new" reference beams within the emulsion.

A courtesy from Jeff.

Add this in the paper of DIY plates

"..."Panchromatic"Plates making.

If you want to make plates sensitive to both red and green you cannot do it by having both dyes in the same bath. Instead you need to repeat the process twice and use the dyes in the "right" order. I only found what was right by trial and error. So in the case of the dyes : 1,1 -diethyl -2,2 cyanine iodide for exposure at 532nm and Pinacyanol Chloride for exposure around ~640nm. ----

I first make the emulsion sensitive to the 532 nm light following the steps above EXCEPT for step "6" . So for the first Ag Br loading operation to get sensitivity to 532nm the sensitizing bath step "6" should not be done at this first stage. The plate should then be given a VERY thorough rinse , this can first be in tap water to get rid of all the soluble bromide ions and then it should be rinsed well in distilled or de-ionized water to get rid of any chloride ions from the tap water.

The plate (wiped free of droplets ) is then treated with silver nitrate solution again but as it is already swollen, 1 minute is long enough this time. The plate is then squeegeed free of droplets, given a cold air blow and immersed in an agitated bromide bath containing this time the pinacyanol dye for the 640nm region , however to restrict grain growth the time in bromide bath may need to be only about 1 minute or less. Then finally the sensitizing bath can be used (step 6).

A dimmed down blue computer monitor screen works fine for a safelight by the way...."
