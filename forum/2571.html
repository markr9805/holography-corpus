Dinesh Tue Jun 29, 2004 4:54 am #5
There was a paper a (long!) while back where some MITer used a liquid gate for pseudocolor. The idea was to place the film in a liquid gate (a closed enclosure filled with a particular liquid) with plain water. Then she made an exposure, drained the liquid gate, filled it with TEA and made another exposure. She continued this process for two or three exposures with different concentrations of TEA to get a range of colors. If you're interested, I'll try and find the paper, but I believe it was sometime in 87 or 88. I've also seen this technique around about 82. Some researher at CalTech was using liquid gates to identify the bones of small animals. He immersed the film in water and shot the bone, then changed the liquid in the gate to alcohol and shot the same bone again. The change in the index of the film surround gave enough of a phase difference to cause interference lines on the bone. The interference lines gave information on the small details that differentiated the bones of one small animal from another.

Dinesh Tue Jun 29, 2004 1:10 pm #8
The underwater holograms are absolutely fascinating. He gave a paper on it two years ago. The idea was to count plankton in the North Sea. They take a submersible with two arm-like appendages. one "arm" is the laser and on the other is the film. In between the two is about a cubic meter of plankton-filled water. They then shoot the hologram, develop and reconstruct it back at the lab and use a microscope to count plankton in the hologram. I have the paper if anyone is interested.

Just a point of personal interest: My own interest in pseudocolor DCG came about as a result of the Walker talk. Someone in the audience asked her if it was possible to use this technique for DCG. She replied that pseudocolor in DCG was an impossibility. Of course, the moment anyone tells me something is impossible .....

I would have to find my notes but I remember when I first started playing with VRP-M and 488nm my single beam reflection holgorams were coming out deep blue to violet. I performed some testing and found I could take the completely processed and bleached hologram and soak it in some table sugar (about 2 tablespoons) and water (1 cup) for about 5 minutes then redry and it would shift the color into the green to light green. I tried different soak times and different sugar concentration and could only get the emulsion to increase in wavelength of about a max of 50nm - 60nm. I got the idea from Dr Joeng of Integraph but he stated to use some chemical (I don't remember now) to swell the emulsion post processing and redry. He explained the idea is to leave some foreign material in the gelatin, thus when it dried it had more volume thus increasing the fringe size. I am not sure where those test holograms are right now so I am not sure how durable the emulsion is after that.

Martin Wed Sep 22, 2004 5:48 am #16
With PFG-03 which used to be an extremely soft emulsion (I learnt Slavich

may have increased emulsion hardness since), you would have to use a much higher alcohol concentration (the softer the layer, the higher the degree of swelling), maybe 80% or so. Otherwise, when replaying the dry emulsion, the reconstruction wavelength will be greatly UV shifted.

One important point to consider when doing that kind of alcohol/water

swelling is: things may significantly change when you carry out a 100% water pre-treatment prior to inserting the emulsion into the alcohol/water

solution. That way the emulsion will reach an equilibrum state much quicker. On the other hand, using but alcohol/water may take the emulsion hours to stabilize.

Instead of alcohol/water as the immersion liquid you may also consider salt solutions - as long as they are sufficiently inert with regards to the

silver halides. Sainov/Sainov/Bjelkhagen (SPIE 615, 1986) then used sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) with some success with a Bulgarian silver halide emulsion similar to PFG-03.
