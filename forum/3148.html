Is there a difference between holographic film and holographic plates?
Dinesh Wed Feb 23, 2005 2:09 am #2
Well a holographic film could be a movie with Tom Cruise playing the reference beam and Julia Roberts playing the object beam. Holographic plates is a kind of excercise where you think you've lost weight, but you haven't. Oh, sorry! That's Holographic Pilates.

No, holographic material comes in two varieties, sometimes. The base is either on glass, in which case they're known as holographic plates, or they're on some sort of flexible plastic film like tri-acetate, in which case they're known as holographic film. Sometimes, however, the emulsion itself, whether coated on glass or flexible plastic, is also called "film", which could lead to confusion. Sometimes you see statements like, "The film takes a resolution of 3000 l/mm" which obviously is talking about the emulsion itself. Glass is easier to work with because it's easier to place in a plateholder while film needs to be supported. I sandwich my film between two sheets of plain glass. There are other optical differences. Plastic is usually birefringent, which causes problems, while glass is not. Glass is easier to process while film flops and curls in the developing tray. However, glass is expensive compared to film. Slavich usually comes in two flavours: glass and film, while Colourholographics BB only comes in glass, as far as I'm aware.

Being a film lover as well (not just the movies), I've to add a few points. Compared to glass plates film may have some upsides too: it can be cut very easily. You already mentioned the price issue - that's probably the most significant advantage over glass. That quality naturally favors all kinds of experiments (who wants to put at risk hundreds of $$ paid for a few glass plates!).

Film may be applied on curved surfaces.

With regards to processing, film may be developed/bleach in automatic photo processing machines. Or at least, processing drums may be used, which require an incredibly small amount of liquid: e.g. as little as 125ml developer solution will be sufficient to develop a 30 x 40 film sheet.

By the way, it's been a while since film was made of birefringent plastic material. 1985 or so Agfa switched from polyester to triacetate. All subsequent film producers (Slavich, Ilford, Ultimate, Filmotec) are/were based on TAC. The manufacturing process of triacetate involves casting, therefore no optically active material will form.

From the manufacturer's perspective a real problem with films seems to be - unless large quantities can be produced (and sold!) - the effort required for coating basically remains the same as for glass plates. On the other hand, large scale film production facilities might be grossly under-utilized. According to Richard Birenheide (HRT) even then monopolist Agfa used to coat their annual holographic film production in just one day...
