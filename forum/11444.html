I am using silver halide film, with a TAC base, laminated onto glass.

The reference beam angle is 65deg to the plate normal, linearly polarized, p-polarized orientation (ie. to minimize surface reflections).

My issue is that I am getting much better holograms when the emulsion is facing the object beam but I'd like it ot face the other way.

Can anyone tell me why it is so (internal reflections reducing the contrast perhaps?) and if there are ways to avoid this?

My initial thoughts are that TAC is birefringent. Thus, assuming the hologram is a Denisyuk with the emulsion towards the object, the reference beam first hits glass, then TAC. Inside the TAC, the polarisation will twist due to birefringence. If this twisted polarisation hits the object and reflects, the polarisation the object recieves (the twisted polarisation due to birefringence) is reflected with the same twisted polarisation, assuming the object does not de-polarise too much. Therefore the (twisted) polarisation of the ref, and the (twisted) polarisation of the returned object light are more-or-less parallel, and you get high contrast.

If the emulsion is facing the ref, the emulsion "sees" p polarisation. Then the beam goes through the emulsion to the TAC. Here, the polarisation is twisted, hits the object, and is returned twisted. So now, the p polarisation of the ref and the twisted polarisation of the object will not be parallel and the contrast is reduced.

Also, the high angle of the ref may be exacerbating the de-polarisation, because the e wave depends on the incident angle.

What to do? Well, if you have glass plates (no TAC), this will give you a reference point, because there should be no difference - I've done it both ways on DCG, and found little difference which way the emulsion is pointing. The point about which way the emulsion is facing is based on which side is blackened, you need to blacken the emulsion side. However, with DCG, you laminate a cover plate of glass, and it doesn't matter which side you laminate.

Without knowing the geometry, it's difficult to say whether internal reflections could be the cause. If there are internal reflections, it would decrease the ratio, but the hologram would be noisy. Noise in a hologram is usually caused by low frequency gratings superimposed on the object beam, creating a halo effect around the image.

Din Sat Oct 11, 2025 8:49 am #10
If you're getting woodgrain, it suggests that you're at S polarisation, not P. One way to find out is to use a plain glass plate, and have the ref reflect off the glass plate. Start by orienting the glass plate so reflection is at normal incidence, then slowly rotate the glass plate in the plane of incidence (horizontal, if you're side referencing, vertical if you're top referencing). At about 60 deg, the reflected beam should to vanish at P.
