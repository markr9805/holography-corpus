Make test objects before you spend lots of time on the big project. Soap stone is much slower to work with than the other products you have mentioned. I have had good results with sculpey. That looks like a good start, and the tools should serve you for a long time. I've never used the sculpey, but the soapstone at least sounds interesting. One problem with stone is that once you carve it off, you can't put it back on.

Long ago I made a couple of things from plain old ceramic clay, the kind you put in a kiln. It was fairly thin, and once dry I applied silver spray paint and one of the things (a face-in-a-flower) made the display hologram I show off so people can see what I'm talking about. Even with the paint holding the unfired clay together I'm quite sure that it wouldn't ship well.

We sell this two part epoxy putty, it's silica filled and turns into something a lot like stone (and it's pretty much non-toxic), but I haven't made a holo-model with it. The woman I work with sculpts horses with it, and of course, no matter how physically realistic they are, the "Art" community doesn't accept them as "Fine Art" - lumpy bronze castings are "Art", but not ultra-realistic resin castings with oil paint jobs(?)

I designed some carving/sculpting tools that work pretty well on the putty, resin, and other plastics, carbide tips and a collet handle that have become a must-have in the model horse hobby. They're not cheap, but you couldn't have them made for the price.

I'm having a sample of the epoxy putty sent to Dinesh today, if he tries it and finds that it moves around too much once it's cured I'll take his word for it. On the other hand, if Dinesh likes it, that should make for sufficient testimonial as to it's suitability. It's far less brittle than things like JB Weld and quite a bit easier to work with. Good for fixturing too (yes, I know, shameless self-promotion, but it's my job).

Regarding shipping, I mail expensive and somewhat delicate items all over the world and am now renowned for the quality of my packaging and the 99.9%+ no-damage rate.

-Use a good sturdy box that is at least six inches larger than the item in all directions (i.e., a 4 inch item requires a ten inch box) - USPS priority mail boxes work well and they're free.

-Wrap the item in bubble wrap, snugly but not too tightly - if it's too tight the constant tension will probably deform something made with polymer clay. Don't wrap with newspaper, it offers no cushioning and will get ink on your model. Mummifying it with soft toilet paper is an option, required if the item is painted.

-Packing peanuts to fill in around the item in the box are preferable, loosely wadded single sheets of newspaper will work if absolutely necessary. Poke the stuffing down around the sides of the item to eliminate settling in transit. Soft foam rubber works if it's installed correctly.

-This is important - when you close the flaps on the box, it should require a small amount of pressure, a little for the peanuts, a bit more for wadded paper depending on how tight the wads are. This way, just enough pressure is applied to the stuffing to keep the item secured in the center of the packing material.

-Don't skimp on tape in sealing up the package, it's cheap insurance. In theory, the package should be able to fall out of an airplane in flight, in a rainstorm, and the item inside should be retrievable undamaged from the soggy ball of tape and cardboard (and the package label still readable).

-Once the package is sealed, you should be able to shake it vigorously without detecting any movement inside - if it moves, add stuffing. You want the item/package to be able to withstand a drop of at least fifteen feet without worry.

-Insure the package, and delivery confirmation is good too. This way the package can be traced to some extent, and you can check online for delivery. If the USPS site says it was delivered and the recipient hasn't got it, you can start on it immediately instead of having to wait a month. UPS automatically insures to $100 or so, and tracks online.

Pack it right and the trip to Dinesh shouldn't be a problem.

-Gary

tpark Fri Jul 15, 2005 5:32 pm #5
I've had good luck with air drying clays and sculpey. I've always waited a few days before taking shots of the items in case there's some residual shrinkage, plus I usually let things settle for a day before taking a shot. This was for 30 second or so exposures.

Some air drying modeling materials are a bit of a pain because it's not easy to add material to a piece, but if you choose the starting size carefully and work fast you can get pretty good results. Certainly it's easier than materials such as marble
