> rja4096: I think my objects could be better illuminated, do I really need a front-surfaced mirror, won’t a normal mirror do. – I’m not looking for an absolute perfect hologram. After all there will be many reflections between the different objects in the scene, surely all these don’t hit the plate at the same phase?

You can certainly use a plain mirror for object illumination. You'll get better results with a front-surface mirror but you should start by using what you have available. The plain mirror will create extra reflections that may interfere with each other to some extent but it all depends on the thickness of the mirror and angle of reflection.

> rja4096: - No matter how I view the holo, I can’t get it to appear in front (‘real’ I think) of the plate. Is the only way to do this to create a H2 reflection? With a H2 reflection, would I then get a hologram viewable in white light with the depth of the transmission hologram.

Depending on your creation and reconstruction geometry, you may need to get waaay back from the hologram plate to see the real image. At the last PCG Gathering, Dinesh told a story of someone who had the same problem. Turns out that he was standing inside the image and that's why he couldn't see it.

If you've flipped the plate correctly (top to bottom if the reconstruction beam is coming from above) prop the plate up and stand back from the plate.

> rja4096: When I exposed, the distance from laser to plate and objects was about 12 inches. When I view the hologram, I can either have the laser a long way away and the image is dim, or have the laser close and only see one part of the image at once. – any adjustments I can make to my setup to solve this, or do I need a more powerful laser.

When you expose the plate, does the laser light cover the entire plate? How far from the plate is the lens you're using?

This sounds like you might be using a laser diode with no lens. Is that correct?

> rja4096: Should the objects be 4 times brighter than plate or other way around?

When measured at the plate, the reference beam should (by popular formula) be 4x brighter than the light coming from the objects. With PFG and some other films, you can easily reduce this to 2x.

Can you post some pictures of your work? We're always interested in seeing other people's holograms.

That was me. My objects(2 Matchbox trucks) were 6" away from the plate when the H1 was shot, but they were at least 4 feet! wide when I reconstructed the real image.

If you follow Dr. Jeongs directions in the little pamphlet he supplies about shooting a single beam transmission hologram such that a) you shoot the H1 with a diverging reference beam and b) you reconstruct with that same diverging ref. beam, then you will get a hologram that reconstructs correctly when viewing the virtual image, but when you view the real image it is greatly magnified/distorted. Dr. Jeong does state elsewhere in his supplied documentation that you need the "conjugate" reference beam. ie: if you shoot with a diverging ref, then reconstruct with a converging beam or vice-versa.

I have since followed Dinesh's advice which was to use a collimated reference beam. The conjugate is still a collimated beam, so I just pop the plate back in the holder to view it (provided there is something there to view )
Tony Tue Oct 19, 2004 1:34 pm #5
I have used plain rear surface mirror on occasions. Specificly, I sometimes use a small one on the 'far' side of the object to redirect some additional light on the object. This works pretty well, despite obvious coherence length issues.

The light from the rear surface mirror has fringes in it. If the mirror is pretty flat, the fringes are evenly spaced. It looks like woodgrain when it hits the object - an interesting and sometimes useful effect. Sort of makes a contour map of the object high and low spots.

> rja4096: I’m not really sure I understand LarryD’s advice on this

When you spread the beam out from a point it is "diverging." The conjugate(opposite) is when you are "converging" the beam, focusing the previously expanded beam back down to a spot.

I think if you want to view the holo you already have with a converging beam, you need to first spread (diverge) the beam, then use a lens to focus that spread beam through the hologram back down to a point, with the point of focus being located approximately where your laser was when you shot the holo in the first place. The goal would be to have the same rate of contraction of the beam as you had when expanding the beam. Hope that didn't add to your confusion

I use a parabolic mirror to "collimate" the light, that is the beam is neither expanding or contracting, just like a solid column.
