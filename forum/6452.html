> Jur7: Well to start of ill introduce my selfe first. Im a 17yr old boy from holland.

Welcome!

> Jur7: First of all i dont really untherstand how its possible that you can see a transmission hologram by lighting it from the forside (read it on some page).

When you record a transmission hologram the film has a bunch of very fine lines. (About 700+ per/mm for a transmision hologram). The lines work as a difraction grating to bend the light from the reference beam to the observer in exactly the patern of the original object beam.

> Jur7: Next to that i dont untherstand why a transmission hologram does need to be viewed with a laser, and why a reflection hologram can just be viewed with white light.

The lines are perpendicular to the plate in a transmission hologram. This makes them bend light but not to be selective to wavelength. So, if you used white light it would work as a prism and spread out the light into a colorful smear.

A reflection hologram has the lines parallel to the plate. Using bragg difraction (look on wikipedia) it will select only one wavelength to reflect. Effectivly making the hologram viewable in only color regardless of the light source.

> Jur7: And last, i dont untherstand how its possible that when you cut a hologram in 2 pieces, the whole hologram is visible on one piece.

OK, this is the biggest problem in holography. I one day I will research the orgin of this question and send hate mail to them. Here is the best explaination I can give without math. A hologram is a window with a memory. If you break the window you can still see all of the hologram that was visible in that part. For a transmission hologram with the object far from the film, you will see the whole object from different points of view. This is what someone was trying to explain and it got misinterpreted to you can see the whole image with more noise. That is wholy untrue! You can only see the image from the original point of view. This is the result of every point on the object reflecting light to the entire plate.

In a reflection hologram with the object very close to the plate it is much closer to how a photograph works.

Colin Kaminski Wed Sep 26, 2007 2:35 pm #5
A transmission hologram requires less resolution from the film (hundreds of lines per mm) than a reflection hologram (a few thousand lines per mm) so you can use different film. However, it is not a requirement. There are films that do both pretty good.

Processing can be different as well depending on the requirements of the final hologram.

A reflection hologram is limited in depth by the bandwith of the reflected color. If the reflected color is narrow then the scene can be a few inches deep but the hologram is not as bright as a wideband reflection. A wide band reflection hologram can be very bright but you may only have an inch or so in focus.

A transminssion hologram can have objects feet away from the plate in perfect focus. This can be used to advantage when you try to make a reflection copy of a transmission hologram. You can place the reflection plate inside the real image from the transmission hologram! The resulting hologram has some of the image in front of the plate and some behind.

Hey there, dude!

Here's a mental image that I formed fer myself a while back in an effort to understand just how a bunch of fine lines could ever create an image. I have not yet confirmed the accuracy of this explaination with those who certainly know more than I, but perhaps future posters on this topic can either shoot it down or call it reasonably valid...... We'll see.

In an effort to envision (on a submicroscopic scale), imagine baffles cut through the thickness of the emulsion. Under an electron microscope, these fringes will look like rain falling on the surface of a pond - thousands if not millions of concentric rings and circles - a supercomplex fresnel lens, maybe? In a reflection hologram, imagine these baffles angled and spaced in such a way that the light reflecting from the baffle below interferes with light reflecting from the baffle above, and the baffle above that, etc, to reinforce just one color of the reflected light. Constructive interference. You can now imagine how heat and cold will change the spacing of these baffles, resulting in slightly altered constructive interference - different colors!

A transmission holo also occurs in this complex fresnel configuration, though with the baffles open to allow the light to transmit through, like slits. The light (laser) that squeezes through these slits/baffles is, of course, diffracted (as light is when passing through slits and/or past edges) so as that it will interfere into the image. Squeezed through.....

JohnFP Fri Nov 09, 2007 12:49 pm #11
Ok, I had to throw in the one-off of DCG. That statment is true as far a silver goes because all the Bragg planes are of equal size and spacing. The rest of the light simply passes through, gets diffracted through or gets obsorbed. This is also true for a narrow band DCG.

But for a broad band reflections DCG, there are muliple sets of Bragg planes increasing in thickness from the surface of the emulsion down through the emulsion. Thus the planes close to the top of the emusion are reflecting shorter wavlengths and the ones deeper in the emulsion are reflecting longer wavelengths. Thus you get more light that is being reflected and thus a brigher and more efficient hologram and a broadband hologram. In essence you are using more of the available light used to illuminate the hologram to create the image.
Colin Kaminski Sun Nov 11, 2007 3:12 am #12
So now we venture out of beginning holography.

In most reflection holograms, the frequency of the bragg gratings is a spread of D (the spacing between fringes). So more than one frequency is formed. Even an a narrow band (by art hologram standards) hologram will have a 30nm spread of frequencies. It is posible to broaden this spread by shrinking the outer layers more than the inner layers (close to the glass) and make the hologram broadband. This can replay in a white or gold color. It is still pretty narrow band when compared to light sources. The unused light simply passes through the plate.
