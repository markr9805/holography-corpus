Dinesh Mon Oct 25, 2004 3:37 am #21
Feynmann was pretty uncompromising in the light-is-a-photon view. I suspect that at the level he was working, light is a photon!

Basically, QM deals in what are called "ensembles." QM is basically a statistical theory, with no real analogy to any "common sense", intuitive understanding or theory. Take a typical classical experiment: throw a ball up in the air. After a certain time it returns to the ground. If you threw it up at an angle, it returns to the ground some distance away. Now you can do this over and over and over again. So long as you threw it at the same angle with the same speed, it'll always come back at the same time to the same place. Not so with QM. QM basically says that if you do any experiment, the result is a probability. Note that the probability is not due to uncertainty in information, it's inherent in the laws of nature. Classically, you may not be able to say exactly where the ball landed down to say a micron, but this information is in principle available. In QM, the information is not available even in principle. It cannot be given, except in a statistical sense. So, what is the "result of an experiment"? Well, the idea is that you take an ensemble of exact same situations. Iin other words, you do the same experiment in exactly the same conditions over and over and over again. In each experiment you'll get a different result. The "result of the experiment" is now the statistical average of all resultsof all the experiments in the ensemble. You cannot get any better than this. The result cannot be known better than the staistical average over the ensemble. There is also the Copennahgen view that any question of the track of a particle is meaningless. If you throw a real ball in the classical world, you know pretty much where it is at any time. You may have seen those "Physics tricks" where you launch a ball from a slide and drop one simultaneously and they both collide in midair. This is only possible because at any instant of time you can point to some determinate position in space and say, "the ball is there". Not so in the Copenahgen interpretation. "Particle" loses all meaning of the word because you cannot have a trajectory of a particle. The moment you do, quantum effects disappear (hence the famous "one photon interference") . The essence of classical physics is the calculation of the trajectories of particles in a system, all, or almost all, problems have to do with particle trajectories and their evolution along well know paths as determined by the so-called "equations of motion". In Qm, the particle leaves <here> and it gets <there> what it is, or even if it exists, in the intervening space is a meaningless question. So a photon leaves a quasar, it arrives on earth. Did it "go" along any path? How long was this path? Meaningless! It went along every path or it went along no path at all, or maybe a bit of both. The path had a length of so much. The path had no length. Who knows? All we know is that it left and it arrived.

However, these effects are only noticeable in systems where quantum theory predominates. Roughly speaking, quantum effects predominate at levels comparable to Planck's constant (h=6.6 x 10^(-34) ). So if you're talking atomic levels or sub-atomic levels, then it makes sense to talk of photons, but if you're talking about interference on a 4 x 5 plate, it makes no sense. Since Feynmann's work was at the sub-atomic, particle level, he probably never thought of classical Maxwell's theory of em waves.

How do you know it's "one" photon? Well, it isn't. However, if you had an ensemble of one-photon experiments, then the satistical probablity of the energy of the photon would be equal to the statistical probability of the energy of the electron. We usually "classicise" these problems by writing down energy and momentum equations as if they were classical particles, just to make some sense of them. But QM , and it's big brother QED, is the most misunderstood, un-understood and beyond-understood concept ever. However, it is also the most succesful theory ever. It gives predictions for experiments down to 6 figures accurately. So we say, "one electron hits an atom of a phosphorescent substance, one photon is ejected and so you can 'count' photons by means of a phosphor" This simplifies at the expense of understanding, but it's a well stretched metaphor! You'd lose half the class if you started saying, "Well, according to the Copenhagen Interpretation, you need to do millions of experiments, take the average of each result of these millions of experiments, all alike, mnd you, exactly alike! and then say 'This happened' or 'That happened' But don't ask how or where any one electron or photon actually travelled, because well you see it didn't travel. However, it didn't get there instantly either because then you violate relativity!"

The point of Schrodinger's Cat that most popular and semipopular presentations miss out is that the act of observation kills, or doesn't kill, the cat. Looking at it kills it, or not. Whether or not the cat is 'really' alive or not is meaningless. The act of observing it sends it into alive or dead status. Inside the box unobserved it simply doesn't exist in any classical sense. This is known as the "collapse of the wavefunction".
