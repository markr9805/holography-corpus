Ed Wesly Tue Mar 08, 2005 8:43 pm #1
I was cleaning up my storage space of holographic incunubula and happened upon a box of Electric Umbrella (Rallison) dichromate pendants, vintage 1979 or so. There was a ring of about 2 mm of dead area on the circumference of the 38 mm pendants.

Remembering having seen Ansel Adams, the late photographer being happy to dry his photographic prints in a microwave oven, (real fast!) I thought that maybe the microwaves would dry out the encapsulated DCG.

So I nuked one for a minute, and almost everything else disappeared! So what I thought would be a helpful post, reviving disapppearing dichromates, is a warning post, like don't put them in a microwave oven!

What I figured happened was whatever moisture was in there turned to steam and being trapped, traveled through the rest of the remaining emulsion, and turned the Bragg Planes into mush.

Oh well!

Dinesh Wed Mar 09, 2005 12:19 am #3
Remeber that gelatin has a lot of hydrogen in it and microwaves have just the right energy to shake up hydrogen. Personally I doubt there'd be any water left in there after so many years. If so, the entire image would probably have disappeared. I have DCG holograms from 1985 that are still good right up to the edge, and one DCG that I think is fading at the edges. I think water may have once been there (The "Mars" effect?) but has long since gone. Rehydration of the Bragg fringes may reduce delta(n), but once reduced, even if the water dissipates, the delta(n) is still too low for reconstruction, unless you reprocess. By zapping it in the microwave, you may well have shaken up the entire molecular structure making it more homogenous. This would even out the density profile and lose delta(n) throughout the emulsion.

However, don't feel too bad. I was visiting Brown University many moons ago , ca 1988 or so to give a lecture to RISDI (on rainbows, if anyone's interested) . Anyway, I was in the optics lab when the prof asked one of the holographers to remove the backing of The Original, Very First "The Meeting" (you probably know the one - with the wine or champagne glass re-appearing). Apparently it was going dark and the prof thought it needed re-bleaching. The holographer, I won't mention her name because some of you know her, was understandably nervous about meddling with such an important hologram and asked me about it. I thought benzene would do the trick but I thought we should test it first. So we went back to the lab and made a hologram, which we painted black. After two days to let it dry, we cut it in half and put one half in benzene. The half cut was to ensure that the benzene would not degrade the image, ie we'd have something to compare before and after. The test went well. The black backing came clean off with no apparent damage to the hologram. So, nervously, we placed The Original "The Meeting" into a tray of benzene, sloshed it around a bit and lifted it off. Unfortunately the benzene lifted the emulsion off as welll, the hologram had disappeared. The Original, The Very First "The Meeting"! You can imagine how I felt. A year later I was in London and took in a holography gallery and what was the first hologram to greet the visitors? "The Meeting"!
