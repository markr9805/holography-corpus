Din Tue Jan 07, 2025 1:38 pm #4
If I understand you correctly, you are generating a fringe structure in software. You then print this fringe structure onto Kodak film, and then transfer the fringe structure onto Ultimate holographic film using laser light.

I don't think polarisation is an issue, since you're not exposing either the Kodak film or the Ultimate film with two beams. If I'm right, you're generating the fringe structure in a computer, either just as a set of lines or by using FT software. Polarisation only becomes an issue when recording with two beams, where the polarisation of one beam is π/2 rotated with respect to the other.

My thoughts are:

What is the spatial frequency of your computer-generated fringe structure? You say that your printer has an 8K resolution, but without the size of the print, it's not possible to determine spatial frequency in terms of lines/mm - the usual way of stating the holographic spatial frequency of a grating. The spatial frequency is given by sin(θ)/λ l/mm(assuming one beam is normal), or, in your case, sin(θ)/0.000633 l/mm. So, what is the sin(θ) you have simulated? As an example, if I recorded a grating with one normal beam and one beam at 30 deg with a HeNe laser, the spatial frequency would be ~790 l/mm.

The actual fringe structure, recorded by two beams in the conventional manner, is sinusoidal. If the printer cannot reproduce the sinusoidal nature of the fringes, and instead prints them as a square(-ish) variation, you'll lose efficiency. The reason is that the diffracted light is based on the sinusoidal components of the printed fringe structure, ie on the Fourier transform of the printed fringe structure.

What is the modulation, ie what is the intensity variation between fringe and not-fringe? The most important parameter in holographic efficiency is the modulation. If the modulation is low, the efficiency will be low. Generally, the modulation is given by:

M = {I(high) - I(low)}/{I(high) + I(low)}

where I(high) is the intensity of darkest part of the fringe, and I(low) is the intensity of the non-fringe.

I am curious, though, why you feel you have to transfer to a holographic film. If the print can take the spatial frequency, you can simply hit the transparency with laser light, you don't need to transfer to holographic film. In fact, it may be a cause of the low efficiency, since there is a loss of information due to the transfer gamma; you may be losing some of the higher frequencies.
BobH Tue Jan 07, 2025 2:03 pm #5
I wouldn't use tape to diffuse the beam. That's probably why your DE is low. I'd recommend using a lens to spread the beam.

I'd also recommend using index-matching oil between the emulsions, and a smoked glass backer also index-matched to the back, all to prevent Fresnel reflections.

Polarization may be an issue as Jody mentioned. The tape messes with polarization. The Kodak base may as well, but you are emulsion-to-emulsion, so I don't think that's it.

I would not tape the Kodak film to the backer. I'd make a plateholder that clamps the plate uniformly over the film, possibly by using Kodak-film spacers around the edges where the clamping force is applied.

Well, one thing that occurs to me is that when you copy a set of fringes from Kodak to Ultimate, you may be getting diffraction off the edges of the fringes. This may "fatten" the dark part of the fringe structure (make them larger than the original). This will change the diffraction profile.. Also, my recollection of using Ultimate is that it's a very, very fine-grained material. Therefore, it's also quite insensitive and may require more than 200 uJ/cm². Another possibility is that you're "contact copying" the hologram, as well as direct copying of the fringes. If you hit the Kodak with diffuse light, some component of the diffuse light is at the right angle to not only copy, but reconstruct the diffracted beam. In this case, the rest of the diffuse beam acts as a sort of reference beam to the reconstructed diffraction. If it is contact copying, the object/reference ratio is very low, giving you low efficiency.

> christianpytelwrote:↑Tue Jan 07, 2025 3:06 pm: For my fringes, if I want a good sinusoidal structure, I need to make the distance between slits much higher due to my film's poor resolution. A sinusoidal structure involves continuous change in the intensity (following a sin wave). Clearly any printing process cannot follow a continuous sin wave intensity variation, so the printed structure is not sinusoidal. In order to answer your question, I'd suggest a binary grating with small separation. This way, the primary Fourier component will be the spatial frequency of the binary grating.

lobaz Sat Jan 18, 2025 4:22 am #11
Hi Christian,

I guess it's impossible to solve your problem without knowing your intent and details of the complete process.

In the first step, you create a synthetic fringe structure using some software; for instance a simple diffraction grating or an interference pattern between an object wave and a reference wave (i.e., a conventional hologram). Right now it is not clear whether the result is a binary pattern or a grayscale pattern, but I think it is not important now. Anyway, showing an example would not hurt.

In the second step, you print the pattern onto a Kodak film and develop it. Are you using a standard black and white film and conventional development process for photography? You get an amplitude diffractive optical element (DOE) in this step?

You have mentioned that you get 80% diffraction efficiency (DE) with the Kodak film, but you also complain that it has poor transparency. This does not make sense to me, perhaps you use other definitions than those I'm used to. 80% DE is hardly possible with amplitude DOE. 80% DE is possible with some phase DOEs, but I doubt this is the case.

So, it is critical to understand what is the result of the second step, what is the problem there and what do you expect from the transfer to the Ultimate plate. The world of diffractive optical elements is quite broad and many people here are focused to a very specific part of it; they try to help you as good as they can, but often from their perspective without assuring you are working on the same class of problem.
