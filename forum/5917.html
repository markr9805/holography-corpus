JohnFP Wed Dec 13, 2006 11:02 am #1
We all know that the reference beam and object beam need to have polarization in the same direction for interference to occur. And that diffusing objects randomize the polarization and only a fraction of the light coming off the object is actually useful in creation of the fringes and thus the hologram. So here is my discussion topic.

How many of you actually look your illuminated scene and reference beam through a polarizer when adjusting light ratios in creating an H1 or Denisyuk hologram? Now for you meter users, how many of you actually take a polarizer and find the maximum throughput of the reference beam with the meter and then measure the light coming off the object and through the correctly oriented polarizer before hitting the meter at the plate plane?

I believe, in using a polarizer in this manner a more accurate and thus a more repeatable ratio could be used when changing object scenes.

Furthermore, I believe if a filtering polarizer was placed between the diffusing object or scene and the plate with the polarization oriented correctly to the reference beam then the hologram would come out cleaner as there would be less fogging and other undesirables resulting from the incorrect polarized object light hitting the plate.

So I guess the question is, do some objects or scenes actually polarize the light differently? That is, does the texture of the object and or the paint used polarize the light more or less depending on that variable? I believe so.

Two thoughts:

First, I think the surface of the object has a lot to do with the result. I think silver paint retains more of the polarization and gives a brighter result for a given ratio (measured in unpolarized way). I have had varying results from different sufaces which can be explained by the polarization variations. For example, a dull glossy finish from a statue is much brighter than a flat white surface.

Second, I agree that looking at the scene, or measuring ratios through a polarizer would be beneficial. I have a set of polarized glasses from a 3D movie, but I haven't used them in a while - it's worth a try.

Third - I agree that using a polarizer in front of the plate might allow better optimization of the exposure within the dynamic range of the emulsion - but every additional layer of optics adds it's own artifacts, which might not be worth the benefit.

BobH Wed Dec 13, 2006 5:57 pm #6
I've always used a polarizer at the recording plane to see the object as the recording will see it. It's important to be able to look at the object, through the polarizer, from all angles possible from the plateholder location, keeping the orientation of the polarizer fixed. My technique for low noise recording with Polaroid HN-CP material, copied into the Holo-Wiki by Colin, describes what you're proposing.

I don't measure through a polarizer for beam ratio purposes, though, because beam ratio generally needs to be "walked in" for best results with a final image and its lighting geometry. Exposing a hologram index-matched to a sheet polarizer will certainly result in higher signal to noise, and a brighter hologram because the beam ratio can then be set lower. 8)

Ed Wesly Wed Dec 13, 2006 8:01 pm #8
There is no doubt that the finish and the paint can affect polarization. My default paint for single beam reflection objects has been Krylon #1401 Bright Silver, as its pigments are tiny aluminum flakes. So it's somewhat of a diffuse reflector, but polarizing preserving, as you would observe by rotating a polarizing filter in front of your eye while viewing an object so painted. TJ and Nick Phillips were quite surprised by this demonstration.

Although the light from a truly diffusingly refecting object becomes randomly polarized, it is only when the two waves' polarization vectors are orthogonal that no interference results. There will still be interference when a vertically polarized beam meets up with one that is polarized at 45 degrees from the vertical! It would not be as strong of an extinction, but nevertheless bright and dim fringes are created!

Something oriented at 45 degrees can be broken down into two component vectors, horizontal and vertical. The energy in the wave that is not interfering fogs the plate, so as was noted above, it is best to tweak the ratio etc to get the brightest image.

Although I have heard so may times in the past about holographing through a properly oriented polarizing sheet, I have never seen it done. but as also noted above, there is the risk of more dirt from two more surfaces in the way of the polarizer, so that is why it has never been adopted as standard laboratory practice.

Try this mind-blowing demo if you have three pieces of polarizer: orient two of them to acheive extinction, then slip the third inbetween these two are 45 degrees to them. Light will pass through the triple stack!
