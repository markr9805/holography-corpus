Ed Wesly Tue Mar 06, 2012 2:20 pm #2
It didn’t necessarily make it sensitive to green, it just made it more sensitive, enough to fog under your safelights.

Michael Wenyon showed me something that I had long suspected, that there is a special safelight for the Agfa products. If you look at the Spectral Sensitivity Curve for 8E75HD, (for instance go to [http://nlutie.com/ewesly/GP61.html](http://nlutie.com/ewesly/GP61.html) and click on Agfa Gevaert Technical Information Bulletin 21.7271(480)) you will see that there is considerable green sensitivity, with a dip at approximately 500 nm. Michael had a safelight filter, he called it V502, a narrow band green filter for that Agfa’s notch.

My own experience has proven to me that a lot of the so-called red holographic emulsions, Agfa, current Harman, do have green sensitivity. In fact, Agfa 10E75 is just about as sensitive at 532 as at 633 nm! And you can see that on the chart.

Traditionally in photography the Kodak No. 3 Safelight Filter was used to look at panchromatic films mid-development. (A Wratten No. 3 is a different animal.) It too, has a notch close to the dip of the Agfa red-sesitizing dye. [http://www.kodak.com/global/en/professi ... ti0845.pdf](http://www.kodak.com/global/en/professional/support/techPubs/ti0845/ti0845.pdf)

Its green color was adopted not because of the film’s sensitivity, but the human eye’s, whose peak sensitivity is in the green.

So your safelights may have tested out fine in the past, but in light of the fact that the TEA does make the film 2 to 4 times more sensitive, so you may have gotten over a threshold of exposure with the doping. Try going darker.

Another possibility is that the film was fogged before you started with the TEA. Although I am sure you have checked that also.

Since TEA is colorless, it should have no effect as a color sensitizer, as they need to be color complementary to the wavelength pursued; viz. the cyan color of the dye in the red sensitive emulsions. TEA is just as an overall sensitizer.

Recently Hans Bjelkhagen confirmed something about the old Ilford holographic plates (as compared to the current production of Harman Technology Ilford) that I had also suspected; that they bought from Agfa the cyan dye that makes the holographic film sensitive to the NIR, especially for the edge of human eye sensitivity 694 nm wavelength of the Ruby laser. It might have been part of the deal to keep the prices of both the products almost exactly the same.

The usual panchromatic dyes for photographic film take a nose dive before 700 nm, so Agfa came out with a dye that extended the response out to 750 nm (which explains the 75 in the film’s nomenclature) and I had found that it even worked further in the IR with a 780 nm diode laser. It must be a difficult chemical engineering problem to solve, as there are only a few photographic emulsions that cross the visible spectrum barrier, such as this one, which I suspect use this dye. [http://www.freestylephoto.biz/pdf/IlfordSFX200.pdf](http://www.freestylephoto.biz/pdf/IlfordSFX200.pdf) And I also suspect that the dye might be produced by Agfa, itself a word which doesn’t trip too easily off the tip of the tongue, but is a merciful abbreviation for Aktiengesellschaft für Anilinfabrikation, Corporation for Aniline (Dye) production, which is what they had started producing in the 19th century.

Maybe it’s time to invest in some Infrared Emitting Diodes and night vision goggles!
