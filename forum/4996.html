One possibility is the Benton "Holovideo". The MIT team generated sonic waves which they inserted into an acousto-optic crystal. They then diffracted a beam from the resulting index variations inside the crystal ( [http://alumni.media.mit.edu/~lucente/ho ... video.html](http://alumni.media.mit.edu/~lucente/holo/holovideo.html) ). I believe they used Crays, so it might be a bit difficult to do in a basement. On the other hand, the last time I saw this was the late 80's, so the computing power necesary may be a little more compact by now.

It'd actually be very difficult not to produce a volume hologram! Viewing a hologram has nothing to do with whether or not it's avolume or a surface.

When you produce a set of interference fringes, these fringes exist in space as a three dimensional volume. They're effectively a pair of nested hyperboloids for the "standard geometry" ( [http://www.math.okstate.edu/~wrightd/IN ... node3.html](http://www.math.okstate.edu/~wrightd/INDRA/conics/node3.html) ). By nested, I mean that the hyperboloids are like those Russian dolls, a slightly smaller one exists inside each one with a small gap between them. Now, if you place a holoographic film such that it cuts through the hyperboloids, then the intersection of the plane of the film with the hyperboloid creates a set of (sllightly curved) planes within the volume of the film. How the film reacts with the planes determines whether or not the hologram is a "volume" or a "surface" and this, in turn, determines the form of diffraction which then determines how you see the image.

If the internal volume of the film can be accessed by some processing scheme, then that processing scheme creates a "hard copy" of the planes within the emulsion. There is a material difference of some physical parameter inside the film. Generally, this physical parameter is density, and so refractive index. Thus, in general, after processing, the film consists of a set of planes within the emulsion of varying refractive index. If the film is thick enough, there are a lot of these planes, usually about 50 or so for most display holograms. The light comes in and sees a stack of planes of differing index, like a stack of glasses of a few different types - say crown glass- flint glass-window glass-crown glass-flint glass-window glass etc. Light interacts with such a stratified medium by partial reflections at each interface between two planes. Thus with the right conditions, you have each reflection off a plane re-inforcing the reflections from all the other planes and creating a strong reflection. The form of the reflection depends on the orientation and twist of the planes. In holography, the orientation and twist of the planes are created by the light field off a real object, and so the reflection from these planes creates a light field that's an exact replica of the original light field that created those planes in the first place. Most commercial film is like this, in other words, most commercial holographic film is volume.

If the processing scheme cannot get "into" the film, but only distorts the outer surface, then while the planes still exist within the emulsion, only the disturbance caused by the planes at the surface is used to generate the hologram. This disturbance exists because the planes are never absolutly parallel to the surface, but are usually twisted and slanted, so the "ends" of the planes "jut" out from the volume onto the surface. Examples of these kinds of film are photoresist, used in mass replication for the printing and security industry, and thermoplastics.

Note that whether or not the emulsion is a volume or surface has nothing to do with whether or not the geometry of the exposure is "transmission" or "reflection" (a popular misconception from the early days of holography where it was true for other reasons). Having said that, if the film is very thin and the planes are well seperated, then the part that 'juts out" of the surface may have a greater efect that the parts of the planes within the volume. In general, unless you make you own film, this is not a consideration. Most modern commercial emulsions are thick enough to be volume for most display geometries.

Since you asked about volume, I assume you know the difference in terms of viewing. Briefly, a pure volume hologram has angle and wavelength sensitivity. It only shows up at a narrow set of angles around the reconstruction angle at a particular colour. Depending on how you shoot it, it either winks out or changes colour as you rotate it. A pure surface hologram has no angle sensitivity. it diffracts at pretty much all angles. Most holograms are a mixture of the two with a heavy emphasis on volume.

In the MIT case, a computer generated the same waveforms as the optical waveforms of a real object using fourier transforms, except the waveforms were acoustic. In other words, the variation of pressure copied (sort of) the variations of the electric field. Of course, the E-field is a vector field and the acoustic pressure field is scalar but you can ignore the vector nature of the E field by maintaing polarisation. The pressure variations caused index variations inside an acousto-optic crystal, exactly as a conventionally shot holographic film would have done. A seperate reconstruction source was then used to diffract off the acoustically created index variations.
