Colin Kaminski Mon Dec 18, 2006 3:42 pm #9
This banding was likely caused by mode hopping, the rapid shifting between one frequency and another. Try mounting it into a water bath. take a copper plate about 1/4" thick and drill a hole that will fit the diode. Drop the plate vertically into a bucket of water and leave the diode just above the water. You will want at least 1 L of water.

Put a microscope slide in front of the beam and steal the reflection and aim it at a photodiode. Hook this photodiode up to a amplifier and speaker or better an oscilliscope. When the laser is in a single mode it will be quiet.

Change the power and temperature of the water until it is stable. It is very easy to change the temperature of the water by adding hot water with a seringe. This is very controlable.

Thank you to Jonathan Head for perfecting this idea.

wler Tue Jan 09, 2007 8:43 am #18
I spent pretty much the last year investigating DVD diodes (rather then doing holograms..), and found that generaically they are good for holography only up to a fraction of the rated power. This is consistent with what others have reported here and elsewhere.

In particular, the Rohm diode (thousands of which were available on ebay for less than $10 per piece) is good to approx 50mW when the operating point is chosen carefully. Above that it strongly runs multimode, and this is not necessarily signalled by noise in the output.

The max CW power I checked was close to 150mW, but probably this leads to reduced lifetime. The max obtainable CW power is probably around 200mW.

Other, no-name diodes out of DVD burners behaved similarly; on the other hand, the one Mitsubishi/Thorlabs ML120G21 that I tested did pretty well up to 80mW (where is has some weak extra modes that may not be harmful).

So as a rule of thumb, run DVD burner diodes at 80mA or so, listen for noise in the output while also checking the fringe contrast to be sure. An excellent current and temperature stabilization is a must if you want to have reproducible results rather than gambling!

Right, constant current is the way to go. As for ECDL, I have played with one test setup with quite unsatisfactory results. The problem is that it does not necessarily run in single mode either, and hitting and maintaining a single mode spot needs an exceedingly good thermal stabilization (to a few thousands of a degree). A change of the current by 1mA already induces mode hops. While those hops are much smaller as compared to non-ECDL lasers, they are still disastrous for holography.

I will try to improve the design, but it is not clear whether this thing would generically be much better than a non-ECDL laser. They main advantages of an ECDL laser (very small line width and tuneability) are not of much relevance for us holographists.

Sergio Sun Jan 14, 2007 11:23 pm #27
According the "Laser Diode for DVD Pick-up Head" invited paper from Department of Physics

National Changhua University of Education, Taiwan

J012-SPIE Proc 3419 (1998) 110-118,

The DVD write diode laser seems to be a valuable tool to be used in holographic recording:

The other technique like data compression (e.g. MPEG-2) is used to further increase storage capacity.Since the storage density of DVD is much higher than that of conventional CD, the distance between two databits becomes smaller. How to minimize the cross talks between data and how to clearly decode data becomeimportant. One of the key factors to ensure distinguishing data clearly is to reduce the relative intensity noise(RIN) of laser diode. Intrinsic RIN for a laser diode can be as low as –150 dB/Hz. However, the variation ofoperating temperature and the light reflected from disc back to LD will cause the occurrence of mode hopping inLD and hence increase RIN of LD. According to DVD Book, the RIN of laser diode should be less than -134dB/Hz. A quarter wave plate is usually used in the pick-up head to avoid the reflected light and, in the meantime, the laser diode is modulated at high frequency to reduce mode hopping. Instead of modulating LD,another alternative is to use self-sustained pulsation LD to reduce RIN.

We verify that RIN requirements is very critical! It would explain why the simple collimator used with the DVD diode caused such mode hop behaviour with noise. However more elaborated, not cheap diodes used now in pickups may have designed to be AR coated on output face!

COD (catastrophic optical damage) will eventually occur at the laser facets due to the heat created when thelight emitted from the laser is absorbed. One possible solution for this problem is to have a relatively thin active layer. Under this circumstance optical power density is reduced due to the enlargement of the radiativespot size. However, this method tends to increase the threshold current density since the gain is lower ascompared to the regular laser diodes, which use a wider active layer. To resolve this problem a compressivelystrained-layer quantum well can be introduced to the active layer to reduce the threshold current density.Another approach to improve the COD level is to employ mirror-hardening technique such as addingprotective coating to the laser facets. This is usually done by adding anti-reflection (AR) coating at the emittedfacet of the laser diode and high-reflection (HR) coating at the rear facet. With AR/HR coating, higher outputpower can be obtained at the same power density inside the laser cavity, which in turns lowers the currentdensity. In general, the reliability of a laser diode strongly depends on the operation current density. A lower10reflection coefficient at the emitted facet and a longer cavity length help reduce the current density.

At first glance the introduction of a quarter wave plate after the expanded beam diode would be a start option if using spatial filters etc. I wonder also if a "self-sustained pulsation" could be reproduced to eliminate definitively those mode hops.
