I am sure other will explain in more detail but movement is relative. I think the basic rule of thumb is you do not want any movent with respect to the reference beam and object beam to be more then 1/4 of the wavelength you are using at most. So if your object moves a 1/4 wavelengh in a minute you have the option of exposing for up to a minute. If your object moves 1/4 wavelengh in .000000001 second then you only have the option of exposing for up to .000000001 seconds. Using the basic formula (Joules = Watts X Seconds). Looking at Slavich's site ( [http://www.slavich.com/technical.htm](http://www.slavich.com/technical.htm) ) and using their numbers for PFG01 (75mJ/cm^2) ... 75mJ/cm^2 = 75mW for one second per cm^2 = 75,000,000,000 mW for .000000001 seconds per cm^2

THUS you would need 75,000,000 watts continuous power (diode)with an exposure length of .000000001 second per cm^2 of plate.

Most holographic pulsed lasers for recording people and animals are measured around 10 NanoSecond pulse lengths and 10 joule ouput power.

Let's do the calculation a little more realistically (Jeff, I'm running out of envelopes!):

Firstly, it's not just the power of the laser, but also the exposure time. The more powerful the laser, the shorter the exposure time. The power of the laser means that it is delivering a certain amount of energy per second per unit area. This area-dependance is usually not explicitly stated, but is usuallyunderstood to mean power per sq cm.Thus a 5 milliwatt laser delivers 5 millijoules of energy per second over a sq cm. The laser beam itself has a radius of about 1 mm and so is only about 7510(-4) sq cm in area. Thus the raw, unexpanded beam is giving out 666 mJ per second. When the beam is expanded over a 2.5 in x 2.5 in plate (6.25cm x 6.25 cm) , the energy per unit area drops to 5/(6.25)^2 = 0.128 or 128 microwatts. If the plate were Slavich PFG-01, then the plate sensitivity is 100 microjoules/sec. Since the plate is receiving 128 microjoules/sec, it needs 100/128 = 0.78 seconds of exposure. In order to capture an object, the phase difference on the film must not exceed half a period (a quarter period would be better, but overly restrictive) during this 0.78 seconds. The phase difference, in terms of distance is:

phi = 2pix/lambda

where x is the distance traversed by the beam and lambda, of course, is the shooting wavelength. We can associate the change in path difference, delta x, somewhat crudely, by:

delta phi = ( (2pi)/lambda)delta x

If the expsure is delta t and the minimum phase change is a half period,then delta phi = pi and the maximum velocity that can be holographically imaged, delta v, is:

delta v = delta x/delta t = (lambda)/(2delta t)

Since we've established that your 5 milliwatt laser spread out over a 2.5in x 2.5 in plate requires 0.78 seconds to expose, delta t = 0.78 and so, with a Herculean mathematical effort, we come to a maximum velocity of

delta v = 650/1.56 = 416.66 cm/sec or approx 0.9 mph Ta-Daaa.

Understand this is very, very crude. We've assumed the object is moving only towards the plate. We've not taken into account any sideways movement or rotation. Taking this into account would lower the maximum speed by a factor of 1/2 or more. The calculation is relatively easy, you'd need to ttranslate the phase difference into a function of omega, the rotational velocity. Since omega = d(theta)/dt, the rate of change of angle, you'd end up with a lot of sin(theta)'s and cos(theta)'s. Also, we've not taken into account the Gaussian nature of the laser or the polarisations. There's a bunch more factors, but they're more subtle.

This calculation was based on the fact that the exposure was 0.78 seconds, which was, in turn, dependant on the power of your laser. Had your laser been twice as powerful, then the exposure would have been half of 0.78 or 0.39. Plugging delta t = 0.39 into the above would given a higher maximum velocity of 650/0.39 or about 2 mph. The velocity is inversely proportional to the exposure time (of course!)

A pulse laser is characterised by the energy, not the power, because it doesn't give a continous power output. It "flashes" it's energy in a very brief time. Typically, a pulse may give out 5 J of energy over about 5 nanoseconds. This is a lot of energy thrown over a very little time and so the exposure can be very, very short. This, as mentioned before, significantly raises the maximum velocity. I'm no expert on lasers, but I believe that Ruby laser rods can be inexpspensively bought nowadays, but you need to build the power suplly yourself.

However, in terms of multiple shots, yes you can do it if you're either blessed by the hologods or are incredibly lucky. You have to bear in mind that you're taking a photograph of hundreds of thousands of very, very tiny lines. The problem is that if the object is moving while you're shooting the fringes, they'll drift and cause loss of detail in the final phtotgraph - the fringes need to be captured as a sinusoidal variation of greyscale on the emulsion, any deviation from sinusoidal or drop in amplitude will result in loss. If, however, the object (fringes) don't move in the very short time of a "sub-exposure", you'll capture one set of fringes albeit very weakly (the amplitude of the sinusoidal fringes will be low). If you now shoot a second or more "sub-exposures" and the object has moved between these "subexposures" then you'll record a second set of fringes, also weak, which will not co-incide with the first set. Each "sub-exposure" will record a new set of fringes corresponding to the new position of the object. If the motion is very small, then these new fringes will cause a sort of "beat frequency" on the final hologram and you'll see a dim image with lots of dark lines across it - unless you're either incredibly lucky or blessed by the hologods and you're object did not move between each "subexposure"

I'll describe a transmission hologram system. A reference beam illuminates the recording plate at a large angle (say 70 degrees from the normal). An object (for expamle, a flat diffusing screen with a porcelain cat mounted on it facing to the left) is placed to be parallel to the recording plate. The object is illuminated by a single diverging beam from the left side, also at nearly grazing incidence.

A laser of short coherence length is analogous to a pulsed laser. Think of the coherence length as being the same as the pulse length of a pulsed laser emitting a continuous train of pulses.

When the shutter is opened, the first "pulse" is split by the beamsplitter and hits the leading edge (left side) of both the recording plate and the object screen. The object reflects this first "pulse" toward the recording plate, and the path lengths are matched so that it arrives at the same time as the first reference "pulse".

As both "pulses" progress along object and recording plate (the reference, that is), the fringes they form are recorded. Since there is a continuous train of "pulses", the exposure is built up over time as is the case with a CW laser.

During reconstruction (by a CW laser), a one-eyed viewer looking through the hologram with their eye close to the plate at the left edge will see the object screen lit up only by a thin slice of light, the width of which is equal to the "pulse" length of the laser used for recording. As they move their eye along the plate, the slice of light on the object will move with them, eventually hitting the nose of the porcelain cat and progressing down its body. What the viewer sees is essentially a movie of the wavefront of the object beam as it moves along the object, hence the name "Light in Flight".

If the plate is rotated 90 degrees (to now be top illuminated), both eyes of a two-eyed viewer can see through the same slice of the hologram simultaneously and thereby see the object three dimensionally (although now the cat is pointing up).

The above description probably leaves some questions, but it's off the top of my head. Ed Wesley, whom I believe has worked with Nils could probably fill in the gaps better than I. Dinesh as well. Best, however, is to consult Nils' book and/or papers on the technique.
