Hello,

I've sprayed one of my holograms with a black spray, but the hologram has lost some of his brightness.

Which paint should you use best? Should you use a shiny black spray or a non-shiny black spray?
MichaelH Sat Jan 15, 2005 10:20 am #2
The general rule of thumb is to use flat black but to be honest, I've never tried shiny.

I prefer to use [Oracal 651](http://www.fgsi.com/oracal.htm) though. It provides a much better protective layer and doesn't run the risk of eating away at your holograms. The contrast is much better than black paint and and remains flexible.
Colin Kaminski Sat Jan 15, 2005 11:27 am #3
I find that krylon flat works well if everything is above 70F. Below that and it dries "splotchy". Krylon Ultra Flat Black dries too quickly and can damage the emulsion. At least all of my tests were with PFG-01. I still don't have enough experience with PFG-03 to make a comment.
Dinesh Sat Jan 15, 2005 7:32 pm #4
Try gently warming the back of the plate with a hair dryer. Sometimes the base of the paint leaks into the emulsion and causes it to swell. Heating it (gently!) drives off the base and you can sometimes bring it back.
Tom B. Sat Jan 15, 2005 10:31 pm #5
I've been using flat black paint, but noticed recently that Saxby recommends gloss black instead- now that I think about it, this makes sense and might indeed be better.

I tried several spray paints by masking off strips on a hologram that I didn't like, spraying each strip with a different paint, and comparing contrast and brightness. Best of the flat blacks was Tremclad Fast-dry rust paint (lately Krylon chalk board paint seems better) . I noticed that some others caused damage to the hologram, including Krylon camouflage black which otherwise looked very promising. Technique involves building up many light coats, drying rapidly with a hair dryer between each coat.

Lately, I've been experimenting with an Oracal-type black adhesive film. It's a bit nerve-wracking to put on - any dust or bubbles are sealed in permanently- but results can be very good. I noticed that as careful as I was to avoid bubbles, there was always a slight pebbly texture from trapped micro-bubbles, but these disappeared in a day or two. Once again, try this first on a hologram that you don't care about
MichaelH Sat Jan 15, 2005 11:15 pm #6
> tomb: It's a bit nerve-wracking to put on - any dust or bubbles are sealed in permanently

It does take a little practice but as long as your laminating area is clean you can get consistently clear results.

If you're using film, you can use an inexpensive ($30) laminating machine. I affix just the top edge of the film at the table and then let the laminator roll out the rest. I get no bubbles and a nice clean dark background every time.

If you're using glass, you'd have to make your own laminator or cough up some serious dough.
tpark Mon Jan 17, 2005 11:37 pm #7
I've had good results with varothane brand gloss black spray paint. Flat black spray paint works well, but a can that I had that was marked "lacquer" caused the emulsion to go all splotchy. If you're gonna paint, test the paint on a plate you don't mind getting wrecked.

I put the plates down on a piece of cardboard, and spray them, then come back much later after they've dried to pick them off - premature moving can cause the plates to move and smear paint on the front of the plate. As with all things holographic, patience is a virtue.

--Ted.

jeff-blyth Sat Jan 22, 2005 1:52 am #12
Black Spraying gelatin side.

I use gloss-black (eg car cellulose type) rather than a matt spray ( there is nothing blacker than gloss black when viewed from any off axis position is there?). Also the matting agents can cause swelling trouble on the gelatin.

A useful tip is to warm plate immediately before you spray because the evaporating solvents cause water condensation particularly in a cold damp room.
tpark Sat Jan 22, 2005 3:35 pm #13
I usually use a couple of coats, but if I'm really careful I can get by with one coat. As an experiment, I tried black latex paint on one of my plates (I use latex for painting some jig items since it can be put on with a brush) but it totally destroyed the image, which was not unexpected. I tried some Rona high temperature black paint which I had around to try and improve the appearance of our ancient and dilapidated grill, and it works OK, but not as good as the varothane.

Thanks for the warming tip - I'll try that the next time I paint some plates.

On a drying note, I found that setting the plate on top of electronic equipment (in this case, a stereo receiver) works well - the plate dries nicely, and reasonably quickly without the nasty shrinkage and dust associated with a hair dryer.
