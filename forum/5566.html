Bastian_S Tue Dec 06, 2005 7:02 pm #26
No, the number of excited atoms of course depends on pumping. Increasing pumping increases the population inversion and with this the small-signal gain g0 which is the gain the amplifier has for very small input powers.

But the real gain you see is heavily dependent on input laser intensity because the amplification process itself lowers the population inversion and therefore decreasing gain. The saturation intensity is

Isat=(hf)/(td) (sorry cant write greek letters here)

with h : planck constant

f: frequency

t: upper laser level lifetime

d: stimulated emission cross section, i. e. the "size" the atom has that a photon can hit and stimulate another photon. the last two are material constants.

The real gain coefficient is g = g0/(1+(I/Isat)) ( I : intensity of the input laser beam)

Yes that means the wings of a gaussian are amplified more than the center, but the effect of saturation is independent of pumping which only affects g0.

But you probably wont get a flat top through this if the input beam is gaussian. The output beam might be a more or less distorted gaussian but still very gaussian looking like.

Bastian_S Fri Jan 06, 2006 5:09 pm #47
Transversal modes are beam profile patterns. in the fundamental TEM00 mode the beam has a gaussian shape and best divergence and smallest focus size. Higher order modes have patterns with more than one spot. Real laser beams can be a mixture of several modes. An aperture in the resonator can block higher order modes because they have a larger radius, thats what a cr:yag might do when it bleaches only in the middle where the highest intensity is.

Usually its said you need TEM00 for holography. I personally dont know any reason for this right now, it might be that most lasers that arent even TEM00 wont work for other reasons for holography.

Longitudinal modes are frequencies in the laser beam. Only waves that are resonant, i. e. that build up a standing wave, in your resonator are amplified. The spacing between these modes can be calculated

deltaf=c/2l ;c lightspeed; l resonator length

To have a long coherence length you need a single frequency. Many longitudinal modes would mean short coherence. 1 Mode wildly changing its frequency or several modes starting and ending lasing while your q-switch is open might be bad too.

Because of this its so interesting if your laser is forgiving enough so it still has a sufficient coherence length without an etalon selecting just 1 single longitudinal mode.

That is a good article for interest. But I contend that the best way of dealing with ascorbate developer stock and it is a way we have been successfully using for some years in our labs is to "A and B" it.

For A we have a 500ml bottle with:

20g ascorbic acid

3g Metol (4-methylaminophenol sulfate)

and top it up with 500ml deionized water

for B we have a 500 ml bottle of

50g sodium carbonate anhydrous

15g sodium hydroxide

top up with 500 ml deionized water.

(This one should be labeled "very caustic" )

Just use equal volumes of A and B from then on.

Now there are 3 bonus points for using Metol instead of phenidone.

1) is that phenidone is quite a strong silver halide solvent and tests have proved that metol gives brighter holograms.

2)The second point is that metol has a hardening action on gelatin and its effect on speeding up the development time over what you would have with just alkaline ascorbate means that even notoriously soft emulsions

juch as PFG-03 can be in and out of the developer bath into a stop bath (~5% acetic acid ) in around 20 seconds, before the gelatin is seriously attacked. Assuming of course your exposure level was good enough.

3) Metol is a weaker reducing agent or developer than alkaline ascorbic acid.

When Metol gets oxidized it goes really dark brown so this is a useful indicator to tell you when your bath is exhausted because it wont go severely dark until most of the ascorbate has been oxidized. A mild yellowing like weak tea is quite OK . Only when the stuff gets to the colour of strong British tea without milk do you chuck it out. )

Dont forget to use the floating dish method of 2 closely fitting plastic dishes with the upper dish keeping most of the air out as it floats and acting as convenient agitator as well.

Acid ascorbate in the stock soln A will not seriously oxidize for a long time

(Slight yellowing is perfectly OK and can be ignored., provided your stuff does contain Metol.)

I am doing calculations on an internal aperture (a drilled hole in a piece of sheet metal) inside the SSY-1 cavity that would force the TEM00 mode.

Colin alerted me to p.208 of Solid State Laser Engineering by W. Koechner formula for the Fresnel Number calculation. This number allows you to determine which sized internal aperture will force the TEM00 operation of a particular Nd:YAG rod geometry.

Fresnel numbers of less than 1 or even 2 will make TEM00 predominate, which is necessary for good holography.

N (=fresnel number) = (aperture radius^2)/(wavelength x rod length)

For the standard SSY-1 with no aperture we have N = ((2.5 mm ^2)((1,000,000 nm/mm))/(1064 nm) (60 mm)) = 98 which is quite poor.

If we add a 0.7 mm hole we get an N=2 which is good!

If we do not add an aperture but move one of the mirrors away, we can get an N=2 with a OC to HR distance of about 3 meters which seems impractical.

Remember that there might be a small aperture effect from having a passive q-switch in place. The intensity before lasing is strongest in the center which acts like an aperture. This makes TEM00 predominate. Then as the aperture opens, the rest of the excited rod acts like a self-amplifying rod to reinforce the TEM00 mode. But this is just conjecture. I have been reading "Solid-State Laser Engineering" by W. Koechner and found the following things:

The presence of a passive q-switch favors single longitudinal mode of TEM00. (reference p.511) This apparently applies to both dye cells and Cr4+:YAG.

Page 253-4 states that it was learned early on that the passive (dye) q-switches tend to act as TEM00 mode selectors. It was described by W. R. Sooy in Applied Physical Letters Volume 7, p.36 (1965). (Anyone able to get this article?) There are two things that both help in the generation of the TEM00 mode in the SSY-1:

1) Longitudinal mode selection in the laser takes place while the pulse is building up from noise. During this time, modes which have a higher gain or lower loss will increase in amplitude more rapidly than the unwanted modes. The higher gain waves (TEM00) bleach out the dye faster and as the aperture in the q-switch opens, it reinforces the TEM00 mode.

2) The greater the number of round trips that any particular mode takes in the laser cavity, the more it is reinforced. Since the q-switch allows multiple passes while it is opening, you get great reinforcement of the single most powerful mode. It is superior to a Pockels cell q-switch in mode selection for the reason that that cell opens rapidly all at once and does not let the waves have many passes in the cavity.

There was an example on page 511 of an Nd:YAG laser for space-borne high-resolution Lidar that exhibited this TEM00 mode selection. It was a diode pumped, 13mm long Nd:YAG laser with brewster angles on the rod (slab actually). There was a Cr4+:YAG passive q-switch (with 32% initial transmittance and 72% open bleached transmittance) in the cavity just behind the output coupler mirror. That laser produced a TEM00 beamwith an output energy of 0.85 mJ over 1.4ns up to 100 Hz.

Because of these findings, I am opting not to add an internal aperture to the SSY-1 laser cavity for now. I need as much energy as I can get.

I have not seen it in print, but I think there is another factor involved in favoring TEM00 mode with a passive q-switch. The central (or a spot slightly skewed towards the flashlamp side in the SSY-1) area of the q-switch begins to bleach out first, acting as a small Fresnel number aperture. Then, that TEM00 energy builds up rapidly from the center outwards. More and more cross sectional area of the rod begins to sympathetically dump its energy into the TEM00 mode. This is possibly similar to having a second laser amplifier rod, which some of our esteemed collegues have suggested.

I have read the above pdf article called: The Natural Selection of Modes in a Passive Q-Switched Laser. Here are my conclusions:

We are comparing 2 techniques for generating ns pulses which can be used for green pulse holography.

The first method, which is proven inferior, is Active Q-switching examples: acoustooptic modulator, pockels cells, or rotating mechanical mirror q-switch, or our common sense beliefs on laser operations).

The second method is Passive Q-switching using bleachable dyes (as in the standard SSY-1) or as otherwise shown, the Cr4+:YAG passive q-switch.

The analysis and math are based on the numbers of transits that occur by the growing wave modes just before the lasing occurs. As you recall, the mode selection of TEM00 has been shown to depend favorable to these number of transits, but also the higher gain associated with the TEM00 mode energy (which is not considered in this paper).

The number of transists just before lasing is 21 times more selective for TEM00 mode in passive q-switches than with active q-switching. After subsequent lasing occurs, the difference grows non-linearly to over 2100 times more favoring of the TEM00 mode using passive q-switching than with active q-switching.

This factor is further increased by an unknown amount if we consider the higher Gain inherent with the TEM00 wave over higher order modes.

Conclusion: Probably don't need an internal aperture to force the TEM00 mode in a standard SSY-1 laser, used for holography. It is possible that it might be running in nearly TEM00 mode already! That may be why we can make holograms with it in the first place.
