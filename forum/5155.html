JohnFP Wed Nov 02, 2005 12:29 pm #3
I think the question lies in the specifications needed. How narrow a bandwidth is acceptable for this application?

Does it have to be a "large" "collimating" reflection narrow band optic or can it be simply a small flat narrow band reflector? The latter would be simpler and could be used not as the collimator in a reflection telescope but the transfer mirror to the eyepiece. This opic is much smaller (cm^2?).

Also in the latter, simply take a collimated beam, shine it at 45 degrees though holograhic film clamped to a first surface mirror. The reflect beam will interfer with the original at 90 degrees. Then process for narrow band and wavelength.

I think you may have the capability, but processing to the specifications of the bandwidth of the 656nm line may be your major hurdle. The set up should be pretty simple. Also, being you are looking at a bright light source, if you HOE is not that efficient, I don't think it will matter that much.

Anyone see any flaws with this suggestion? Thanks!!

BobH Wed Nov 02, 2005 2:04 pm #4
My hobby is solar observing with an H-alpha filtered scope. I use a Daystar T-Scanner filter, which has a bandwidth of 0.05nm! It consists of:

a) an AR coated window,

b) a 10nm bandwidth IR blocking filter,

c) a polarizer,

d) a thin piece of mica coated to act as a fabry-perot etalon,

e) another polarizer,

f) a broadband trimming filter, and finally

g) another AR coated window.

This whole stack of filters is about an inch thick, and is in an oven to keep the thickness of the etalon constant. The oven is mounted so that it can be tilted to tune to the proper wavelength.

I have this filter attached to a Televue TV-85 telescope with a piece of Schott RG-610 glass to act as an "energy rejection filter" to remove the UV and most of the visible light before it gets focused by the scope. The whole device is held by a heliostat mount of my own design and construction. I'd be happy to sent pics of the apparatus to anyone who emails me directly.

A bandwidth of less than 0.1nm is needed to see chromospheric details, such as prominences, filaments, plague, flares, and spicules. Look on the internet for Coronado Technology Group (now owned by Meade), Daystar, or Solar Spectrum for filters for viewing the sun this way. The absolute best bang for the buck is Coronado's PST at about $500. Especially if it's "double-stacked" with one of their other filters. Google them first.
