Ed Wesly Tue Nov 02, 2004 3:19 pm #3
I have done exactly what you are writing about quite a few years ago at Lake Forest College. The laser was a Spectra-Physics 166, which is a 164 (aluminum angle iron resonator, instead of the 3 rods of the 165) with intra-cavity space at both ends of the tube, the rear for the etalon, the front for an A-O modulator for mode-locking. Mode locker was not present during the experiment.

With the broadband mirror set in, no prism in the rear, and the etalon, the laser would emit quite nicely. I set it up with the etalon at flash, and sent the beam through a prism. Then I was fortunate to have a scanning Fabry-Perot Spectrum Analyzer to place in each beam.

Single frequency modes would be observed in each of the beams as the etalon was tilted off flash. What was interesting was that the power distribution amongst the lines could be controlled by the etalon tilt.

So I think that you are on the right track! The only problem that I can see is how to precisely tune your power distribution for simultaneous exposures, maybe with some dichroic filters in the beam before the spatial filter to attenuate the stronger lines, or whatever. But I certainly have observed single frequency simultaneously in multiple lines of an Argon laser. Based on this observation, Coherent made a Krypton laser(based on the Innova 70 series) for TJ that had an etalon for all lines single frequency.

Hi John,

into this direction. But I think what you propose should more

or less work, perhaps with some tweaking. The lines lase independently

(as far as modes are concerned, intensity-wise there is some

competition) and with an etalon you should get single mode for each

line separately.

Any illumination by some extra line looks like noise for a given

line, so one should keep the number of simultanous lines small (eg

the 488nm line will create noise for the 514nm interference pattern,

and vice versa). So one could try start with two widely separated

lines, eg the blue 476nm and the green 514nm, and see how it goes

(the omni-present 488nm line could be kicked out by an HR mirror

ripped from a dead 488nm laser). One could also use an acoustic-optical

modulator ("AOM") that would allow to switch between colors one

after the other, and so perhaps easier balance the illumination by

the respective lines).

Dinesh Tue Nov 02, 2004 7:30 pm #8
[quote:9e132d9e5f]I foget what it is called but I remember that when you want to make a holgoram with multiple lines they need to be displaced at some angle relative to one another[/quote:9e132d9e5f]

That's for pseudo-color. The planes are normally at an angle to the ends of the emulsion. When you change the emulsion depth, the angle of the planes twists and so the Bragg condition changes to a new angle. So, if you shoot the first exposure at say 60 degrees the Bragg condition for replay is 60 degrees and the planes are at 30 degrees to the ends of the emulsion (assuming head-on object). Now you dunk the plate in TEA and the plate swells by, say 5%. Imagine the top of the emulsion going away from the film base, so that the part of the planes "attached" to the top of the emulsion get carried along with the rising emulsion while the part attached to the film base remain where they were. This will increase the angle of the planes very slightly and decrease the playback angle to say 58 degrees. If you shoot the second object without changing the reference, the first (unswelled) image will be at 58 and the second swelled image will be at 60. The two degree difference that will displace the two images. There's a paper by McGrew that shows you how to calculate the angular change for pseudo-color. I know Martin brought up the link some time ago. For true color or full color (different people call it different things) you don't need to change the ref angle since any swelling will be the same for all planes. There may be issues of cross-talk, but that's a different story.
