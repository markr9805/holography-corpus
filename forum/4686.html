After considering the recent discussion concerning beam ratio measurement, I am beginning to think it may be best to use a glancing angle on the sensor for the reference beam, instead of pointing the sensor straight at it. (I'll use a split-beam reflection hologram with a 1:1 ratio as the example.)

I don't know about other sensor types, but I use a cadmium sulfide cell. This is rather strongly angle-sensitive. In other words, if it faces the oncoming reference beam directly, the meter shows a high reading. If the sensor is at an off-angle to the beam, the meter shows a considerably lower reading.

Obviously, we are comparing the the object beam to the reference beam. And of course, the object beam bears little resemblance to the reference beam. The object beam is really not much of a "beam" at all, since it comes from many disparate locations, in fact from every point on the object which reflects light to the plate.

So, if we are taking a head-on reading for the reference beam, we must also take a head-on reading for the object beam, for the measurement to be the useful for comparison. But the only portion of the the object beam which is striking the sensor head-on (at ~90 degrees) is the very small portion of the entire object beam which does so. (Maybe this is less than 1 percent?) All the other object beam light which hits the sensor does so an an angle. This angle can be very extreme, for example, the rays going from the bottom left corner of the object to the upper right corner of the plate.

The object beam is arriving at the plate from many different angles, and this needs to be compared to the rather specific angle of the reference beam. I think it would be best to think of the object beam angle as the averageof all the beams coming from the object. What would this average be? I think it would be too complicated to try and figure out.

But it wouldn't be anywhere near 90 degrees. Since we probably use Brewster's angle for the reference beam, maybe it would be best to use something like that for the object beam as well. By this, I mean that "object beam angle" that we use on our sensor (for comparison to the "reference beam angle") will be an average of all angles, and say this might be somewhere close to 45 degrees.

To make things simpler, this could be approximated by using back-to-back sensors on either side of the plate, parallel to it.

BobH Sat Apr 02, 2005 6:41 pm #7
I've always pointed the detector straight toward the source I'm measuring for the following reasons:

1. Measuring an unexpanded beam of laser light is done that way. If a detector is angle sensitive, it's easiest to be consistant by using it at normal incidence. This will also give the highest reading of all angles.

2. Measuring a diverging beam should therefore be done with the detector pointed toward the source for the same reason.

3. Measuring an expanded and collimated beam is the same. The source is then at infinity, so the detector is again pointed to see the beam at normal incidence.

4. When assembling a hologram exposure system for making a simple grating (with two collimated beams), the two beams interfere in space where the recording material will eventually be placed. I want the two beams to be of equal power density so the interference pattern will have maximum fringe visibility. Why would I tilt the detector when making these two measurements? Each should be done with the detector pointing straight toward the source of the beam being measured.

5. When one of the sources for the recording is extended (for example, by a diffuser), the detector should again be pointed toward the source. In this case, the detector needs to be free of angle sensitivity so that rays hitting it from the extreme angles are measured as accurately as those comming straight in. Again, we're measuring the power density from each source at the location in space where the recording material will eventually be placed. The production of maximum fringe visibility depends upon the relative strengths of the "beams", regardless of the orientation of the recording material.

Although each beam will be refracted to interfere at different angles inside the recording material, and the power will be spread over a larger area (determined by the orientation of the recording material), the fringe visibility will generally remain the same as it was before the material was placed into the interference pattern where the light overlaps. Any variation (for example, caused by the use of p-polarized light, absorption and scattering of the material, Fresnel reflections) will be a constant for the system and shouldn't affect the measurement significantly (except maybe for edge-lit recordings).

Detectors that are angle sensitive are not best suited for pictorial holography. If that's all that is availbale, I'd recommend pointing it straight toward the reference source, and straight toward the location on the object that is responsible for the "hot spot" in the object beam at the recording plane (with the detector at the "hot spot").

BobH Sun Apr 03, 2005 5:14 pm #15
> Cosine angle attenuation and polarization effects are automatically taken care of. The light meter cell is seeing the light the same way as the recording material, whether it's object or reference wavefront.

It's true about the cosine angle thing but polarization effects depend upon the material the light is going into. For a given wavelength, glass has a different Brewster's angle than silicon (for example). Also, the variation of measurement as a function of the detector's angle of incidence is unique for each detector, and has nothing in common with a recording material's "seeing the light".

All of this really doesn't matter much. Great pictorial results can result from either method (unless you're making edge-lit holograms ). The error resulting from using the detector as Ed does is that the reference beam is probably more powerful than detected, meaning the beam ratio is higher than anticipated. This will make a pictorial hologram dimmer. You'll adjust the beam ratio to take care of that, and end up thinking you're at BR=1 when you're really at BR=3 (for example).

There will also be more light than measured for exposure when the detector is kept parallel to the recording plate. Again, the exposure time will be adjusted to optimize and all is fine.

The only problems occur when results are published and compared. Ed will be recommending lower beam ratios and higher exposures than I will for the same situation (scene, lighting, laser power, material, processing). The important thing is to be consistant with whichever technique is used, and don't take the numbers given for beam ratio and exposure too seriously.

> What's the obvious thing I am missing here?

The difference between the two measurement techniques isn't significant. When choosing a detector and/or light meter, look for one with an un-shadowed aperture, a small head (with the wire comming out the side), and a diffuser to minimize variation of measurement as a function of angle of incidence.

Tony Tue Apr 05, 2005 9:30 pm #26
Here's a newbie reply to the thread I sorta started...

There's several things to consider:

1. Even though the 'object' and 'reference' beams might be at 56 degrees to each other where they meet the plate (assumed but could also be film), when they enter the emulsion, they will be at a closer angle due to refraction at the glass- or emulsion-air interface. So accounting for the angle in air is already flawed.

2. To achieve the maximum intensity variation at the fringes, the two beams must be of equal amplitude. Anything else would result in the fringe peak-null amplitude being limited by the lower amplitude beam.

3. We would like to position this maximum fringe amplitude at the top of the optimal dynamic range of the H&D curve. At this point, light from the object which is of equal amplitude to the reference is recording the 'brightest' (most efficient) fringes possible.

4. The light from the object varies considerably over the film plane. This is especially pronounced if the object has any specular reflectors which can reflect portions of the object beam at near 100 % efficiency, and maintain the original polarization. It is these 'brightest' reflections which we want to position at the top of the useful dynamic range of the film.

5. It is difficult to make point measurements of light intensity because our sensor devices need to have a finite area, usually large enough to have sufficient conversion efficiency.

6. We typically use a light sensor which averages over some area, and therefore we don't actually measure the maximum light intensity directly.

7. I surmise that it is this averaging which causes use to use 'rule-of-thumb' guidelines for light ratios such as 4:1 or 2:1 for certain types of holograms.

8. Therefore I think one could conclude that if you had a means of estimating the peak intensity of the 'object' beam, one could position that at the optimal part of the film sensitivity. In this case, you would want to orient the sensor perpendicular to the beam - but that's only needed if the sensor has appreciable area. If the sensor was an ideal point detector, it would have an omnidirectional sensitivity and angle wouldn't matter. (It would be significantly smaller than a fraction of a wavelength!) The fact that practical sensors have finite area makes them have directivity.

But it's probably easier to just do the average, use a rule of thumb, shoot a test shot, and adjust from there. As long as you're consistent with your experience base, your first shot won't be too far off.

If the power meter's detector is parallel to the holoplate, then all the rigamarole of cosine attenuation is taken care of automatically. There is no point to pointing the detector into the normal of any of the beams because the holo-plate itself is not normal to the beams! Unless of course that's the geometry.

The refractive index of the silicon detector I would guess is in the neighborhood of glass and gelatin. Putting it in the plane of the plate would compensate for losses due to Fresnel reflection coupled with polarization. It sees what the plate will see.

And that would be true for extended objects. The light from the extremities of the object are not going to be perpendicular to the holoplate, and a detector in the plane of the plate will take that into account. (You may want to move the detector around to sample the beams, but keeping it parallel to the plate.)

Yes, you would want to have a detector that is angle sensitive because the plate is. Consider making an unslanted grating with an intrabeam angle of 45 degrees; both beams are 22.5 degrees from the normal to the plate. You would want both beams to be equal, and measure them however you wish.

But now with the same beam geometry, rotate the plate holder so that one beam is along the normal and the other is incident at 45 degrees. The beams will no longer measure equal to a detector that is parallel to the plate. Nor would they would be equal as far as the holoplate is concerned. This could be verified by exposing a pair of plates (AgX assumed for this demo) to each of the beams and see what develops. The one along the normal will undoubtedly be darker than the one from 45 degrees. The old beamspliter would need to be tinkered with to re-equalize the beams.

Usually the simplest solution is the most satisfying one. Making the electronic photo-electric detector look at the light the same way the photo-chemical detector does yields reliable, repeatable results.

I think Bob got a point, and one needs to distinguish fringe contrast and average illumination/exposure.

As for the picture above, assume for simplicity the beams form a

90 degree angle. Each beam has some illumination density, I_beam,

measured in terms of uW/cm^2, say.

Then the plate receives from each beam an illumination density given

by I_beam/Sqrt[2]. So in total the illumination is

2I_beam/Sqrt[2] = Sqrt[2]I_beam. If now the plate is turned and

faces one beam (and is tangent to the other, so it receives nothing

from it), the total intensity is now just I_beam. This is less than

before, and in order to develop the plate to a given density, which

requires say 100uJ/cm^2, one would need to expose longer, namely

by a factor Sqrt[2].

So the total exposure depends on the orientation, while the fringe

contrast does not. And one has to tune both for an optimal shot;

one needs to determine with a light meter the optimal ratio (of 4:1

say), but also the overall exposure must be right. I guess

the latter is just for being in the optimal linear region; an

emulsion with an infinite dynamic range wouldn't care about this

and the only factor that would matter for it would be the fringe

contrast, is that right?

And the polarisation. Don't forget interference is a vector effect and the fringe visibility depends on the angle (if any) between the e-fields. You have to consider what you mean by "maximum de". The de of a volume hologram is an exponential function of fringe density modulation, delta n. Therefore, the de at any place in the emulsion is not completely dependant on the intensity of the beams, but also on the response of the material due to the beam intensity. Thus for the same beam intensity, the de in lithium niobate will be different from the de in resist or dcg. You need to look not just at beam intensity but also at emulsion response. Altering the beam by the cosine of the angle will have an effect depending on the polarisation and on the film response. However, in general, Bob is right. For a given film, the de will depend on whatever light it sees since this determines delta n and will hopefully reconstruct the intensity that originally caused that particular delta n.
