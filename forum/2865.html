I'm only a newbe, but have now decided the best way to dry this is to place on a paper towel, with the plate leaned against a wall. After this I don't touch the thing for at least 12 hours. I had noticed that if I held the plate, drying occured more quickly near where I held the plate - so tempting though it is - don't.

Martin Fri Nov 05, 2004 9:27 am #5
What about hardening the thing at the end of the process – subsequent to bleaching?

You might use a “low hazardous” hardener like potassium alum. After such treatment you may squeegee as you please.
BobH Fri Nov 05, 2004 5:04 pm #6
What type of squeege are you using? A windshield wiper blade is much softer than the typical photographic squeege. I choose the largest blade I can get because they are wider, and then cut them to the length that's convenient. "Anco" is my favorite brand. Be certain to remove the mould release residue before use.

Hardening the emulsion is required. I harden in the recommended formaldehyde based bath, before development, and use a light pressure. The plate can then be dried quickly with a hair dryer.
Colin Kaminski Fri Nov 05, 2004 5:14 pm #7
The recomended hardner is (from [http://www.slavich.com](http://www.slavich.com)):

Formalin 37% 10ml

Potassium Bromide 2g

Sodium Carbonate 5g

Water to 1L

> MichaelH: In a sense. I'm not sure how you order it though.http://www.ecu.edu/oehs/HazWaste/formalin.htm

From that site:

> Are formaldehyde and formalin the same substance?Formaldehyde, in its basic form, is a gas. Most people think of formaldehyde as a liquid. The liquid is actually a mixture of formaldehyde gas and water. The most common concentration used is a 37% solution. That solution contains 37 grams of formaldehyde gas to 100 ml of solution. Formaldehyde solution will polymerize. To prevent polymerization of formaldehyde solution, about 10 - 15% of methyl alcohol is added. It is the addition of methyl alcohol that causes the substance to be called formalin as opposed to formaldehyde.We have occasionally encountered some confusion about the difference between formaldehyde and formalin. This is an understandable problem, since the terms are sometimes used interchangeably. It is incorrect to use the two words this way. The concentrations of chemical fixative that the two names represent are quite different. A fixative labeled as 10% buffered formalin is actually only a 4% solution of formaldehyde. This is because 10% buffered formalin is an example of old-time histologist's jargon describing a 10% solution made from a stock bottle of 37-40% formaldehyde (or more precisely: a 3.7-4% solution of formaldehyde).
