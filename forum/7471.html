JohnFP Wed Aug 06, 2008 10:51 am #2
Hey Glenn,

On their web site they state,

"All of the Silver Halide materials are available coated onto glass or TAC film substrate and cover a wide range of sizes"

So the silver halides are available on film or glass and the DCG is just available on glass.

The glass plates are usually made by coating the emulsion directly on the glass.

Film emulsion is usually made by coating the emulsion directly on the plastic (TAC) film.

7 mil is 7/1000 of and inch. So I would say it is more like 35mm negative film then Saran Warp. Saran wrap is probably only 1/2 mil or so.

What they are describing is an alternative way to mount the flimsy film to a rigid piece of glass to keep it stable enough to make a hologram. The most common way of achieving this is to laminate the film in between two pieces of glass and use an index matching liquid soas not to get internal reflections. The method they describe is to get a piece of electrostatic material (the call it film but it is not holographic film, it is just a clear piece specific plastic that electrostatically charges well and probably has the same index of refraction as their film and glass). They then use this electrostatic "film" as a binder between the glass and the piece of emulsion film. I am not sure why you cannot simply electrostatically mount the film directly to the glass??? I assume the specific material they describ binds well with glass or their TAC but their TAC does not bind well with glass.

john kaufman Fri Aug 22, 2008 4:03 pm #8
When I was making reflection holograms on film (30cmx40cm and 50cmx60cm), I had good success with laminating to glass using gelatin. It was a bit messy, but the film could be easily removed from the glass for final storage. Since I was doing pseudo-color reflections, I needed a mount that could go though a number of baths and be registered with ease. Mounting to glass worked. I described this technique in an issue of Leonardo ("Life in the Lab"). I am not at home now so I can't refer to the exact issue. One should be able to find it on line.

At the time I was mounting to 1/2" glass plate for stability since I found that the film had a severe tendency to curl. I couldn't afford to buy or store large sheets of glass so removing the film from the glass after processing was my procedure. I have some pieces, though, that have remained attached for over 18 years.
Jeffrey Weil Sat Aug 23, 2008 12:51 am #9
Hello Everyone,

John is correct about laminating the film to glass. For the beginner it's the best as your dealing with a plate, stable and with the emulsion exposed to the air, no sandwich problems. If your more advanced glass plates with their ability to be re-registered are the only serious way to master.

When I was making silver my sizes were much smaller than Johns so I didn't have the same storage, weight, or breakable problems that he had to deal with. I expect most here are using small film and plates too. I could permentally attach my film to glass. A 4x5 master on ¼ inch glass is almost bullet proof. Almost nothing short of a full drop will break that. I would often use an over sized piece of glass. A 5x6 gave a nice half inch border all around to handle the plate with. You could just as easily use a same sized one eighth piece. Like many factory plates.

I used permatrans, it was a water clear non-birefringent adhesive from the company Mac-Tac. There are others that would do as well. John's gelatin preforms the same function but is removable as an option. If you don't need that option ,as he did, this is much easier and neater.

After a day or two the adhesive would tighten up and the film would be pretty flat. For the most part, it's now a glass plate. Treat it as such. Chem baths, water washes all of that is no problem. I never had one lift up. I've also used a product from Adchem with the same great results. The masters still look brand new after over 20 years.

Most process's, if they make any sense, will work in holography if you try hard enough but there are a few on the forum that have tried and proven methods. You can't go wrong taking advice from John Kaufman.

I use a glue made by Pritt. I use a laminator to stick the film to glass with it. The good thing about the pritt glue is that you can process the film while still on the glass and then use a warm hair drier to separate the film from the glass after processing. When the glue becomes warm it detaches and is easily pulled off.
