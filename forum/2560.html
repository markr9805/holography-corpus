I was curious about strange effects others have gotten in their holograms by using non-standard (meaning something other than a raw expanded beam) lighting in their setups.

Saxby has a number of recommendations in his book but no pictures of the results.

In my last hologram [(see here)](http://holographyforumorg.ntitemp.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1463&view=previous) I used two pieces of a broken light bulb (my overhead light got broken and the frosted glass spread the light less than the white plastic I'd been using) to light the plane. I was a little concerned that the plane would look too speckly but found that on transfer the speckled look was transformed into an attractive kind of shimmer that makes the plane look more ghostly than I'd intended.

Anyone else run across something along those lines? What did you do and how did it come out in the final hologram?
Dinesh Wed Jun 23, 2004 9:23 pm #2
If you're talking about laser speckle, then, yes, speckle will disappear from the final H2. It'll sort of "diffuse out". This "diffusing out" depends on the resolution of the H2 plate, essentially, the angle subtended by the H1 on the H2 and the grain size of the H2.

Speckle is caused by the fact that minute structural detail such as surface roughness at a level below the resolution of your eye causes interference in very coherent light. Since the eye cannot resolve the interference lines, it averages them out. Thus, when you look at a particular point, the light from that point is the average of the interference lines caused by tiny detail at that point. If there's a lot of interference, the eye averages it to black. If you move the observed point, by shaking your head, for instance, the eye averages all the light fromn all the points your eye is looking at and speckle dissolves. In an H1/H2 system, the "eye" is the Silver grain on the H2 plate. Since the "aperture" of the grain, it's diameter, is smaller than the aperture of your eye, the averaging process averages over smaller levels of detail and so seems to smooth out.

I think I know what you mean. In this case my guess would be that you'd get a sharp edges on the H1 between the areas of light and dark due to laser illumination. When you transfer to the H2 and reconstruct with non-coherent light, the sharp edges sort of diffuse out because your reconstruction source cannot be as small as the laser. That is, it's the result of the normal 'fuzzing' of the reconstruction due to the braod source. If I'm right, the sharpness would depend on the image plane - the closer to the image plane, the sharper these edges.

Though not quite a proof but rather a clue: removing your eye glasses, you'll still be able to see a sharp image of the speckle. Moreover and very strangely, no matter if you focus on a short or a long distance, you'll still see a sharp image of the speckle...[/quote:70e41c259b]

I don't know if you can actually see the individual interference lines themselves. Rather, I think, that speckle is an average density over the interference lines so that it is uniform, ie speckle has no 'fine structure'. In this case, the speckle will always be "in focus" because basically you're seeing edge effects rather than resolving structure. Due to the high spatial coherence, very short linewidth of the source these edge effects will be very sharp and more or less independant of any focusing action. Also, I believe edge detection does not come from the fovea itself but from other detection systems in the eye/brain pathway.

I did not initially believe that the eye has holographic resolution. But then, on further thought, what does limit the resolution of the fovea? Not being a bio-chemist, my best guess would be the molecular size of visual purple molecules. In this case, the average size of the molecule would have to be about a micron or so to have limited holographic resolution - which does not seem impossible. So, I'm surprised but it's possible to have holographic resolution in the fovea! In this case, I have to revise my statement and say that speckle - the average of a interference lines over a tiny structure - would appear as speckle only if the spatial frequency of these lines were greater than 1000 lines/mm and would be resolved as lines if less.
