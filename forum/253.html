Gall Fri Jun 17, 2011 6:56 am #2
Not exactly. They're not related.

The level of reference beam is "white level" of the hologram. For best results the reference beam should be as bright as the brightest light reflected from the object (and to be recorded - you may want to overexpose some small parts of the image to make other parts better). In case of H2 recording the H1 is just counted as object.

When recording H1, measure (or guess) object reflectivity to set up correct beam ratio.

When recording H2, you'll need to know the H1's reflectivity (diffraction effectivity). If the hologram is "ideal" (100% DE), use 50%/50% ratio. If not, set up the ratio based on hologram's DE. It is possible to try to improve the brightness of H2 at this point if H1 is not very bright - just make reference beam a bit weaker.

If the beam ratio is set correctly, the exposure time depends only on the reference beam brightness. Consider you had 40/60 ratio on yout H1 and use plates of the same sensitivity for H2 but with 30/70 ratio. Since your reference beam is 1.33 times weaker, so the exposure should be 1.33 times longer.

Of course, I assume that both beams are expanded to the same area with the same losses. If no, the ratio should be corrected according to the losses.

Dinesh Sat Jun 18, 2011 12:06 pm #5
There is, actually, a relationship between the H1 and the H2. However, it's a little subtle. There are other factors that are important and often overlooked.

The "classic" standard view, still seen in many books, is that the ref/obj = 4:1 for an H1. This was because in the pre-bleaching days, the response curve for silver halide, ie the darkness level against the exposure (the so-called Hurter–Driffield curve) was "S" shaped. To get linearity - the hologram brightness should reflect the actual object brightness overall - you exposed at roughly the center of the H-D curve where the curve was straight. This was associated with standard amplifier theory with the "signal" was taken to be the object beam, modulated by the "carrier" whcih was taken to be the reference beam. The ideal modulation level was seen as 4:1, hence the conventional wisdom of 4:1. These holograms were called amplitude holograms

In modern processing schemes the holograms are bleached and so are phase holograms. In such a case, the H-D curve no longer matters after bleaching since the response is non-linear anyway (it does matter during the development process). For a bleached hologram, the "ideal" ratio is 1:1; this gives the best ratio for index modulation by which the efficiency of a phase hologram is measured.

However, the albedo of a typical object is not constant. So, not only is the albedo important, but also the variation of the albedo. Assuming no variation (or small variation), I'd agree with Gall

> Gall: the reference beam should be as bright as the brightest light reflected from the object

That is, 1:1.

> BobH: For the H1, the reference light should be about 10x the object light, at the recording plane

Bob seems to think 10:1 is a good ratio. Personally, I'd say this was a bit high for a volume phase hologram, but it's a good ratio for a surface relief hologram. Typically, photoresists are recorded in about the 10:1 ratio.

If there is a large variation of albedo, then the ability to record that variation is a function of the dynamic range of the medium - the extremes of brightness at which the material will give a faithful reconstruction. Silver has a fairly good dynamic range, but dcg is even better. This may be one reason that dcg is perceived to be brighter.

The H2 has no albedo. The "object light" is the efficiency of the hologram multiplied by the light intensity hitting it. This is not to say that the object light is constant on the H2 plate, it varies according to the albedo and linearity of the H1 recording. Assuming a low albedo, the overall average object light is as above, but if the H1 plate does not have a sufficient dynamic range, or the object is not well lit, then there may be some vignetting. So, a 30% efficient H1 being zapped with 10mW will give out 3.3 mW. Again, for a bleached silver or dcg, the hologram will be a volume phase hologram. In this case, the best ratio is 1:1 for maximum modulation. In the above example, the ref should also be ~3.3 mW. So, the relationship between the H1 and the H2 is that both the albedo itself and the variation thereof are properly (linearly) recorded. If this is so, then the ref intensity is simply the efficiency of the H1 multiplied by the light hitting the H1. If this is not so (for example, if you have vignetting due to lack of dynamic range or a poorly lit object), then you might be able to jack up the H2 by slightly decreasing the H2 ref.so as to give a 1:1 but with a weaker object (as per Gall's point).

Inherent in all this is the polarisation of the beam, the beam divergence and the Gaussian profile of the beam. Theses are the factors that are important but often overlooked. The above analysis assumes a collimated H2 ref, a perfectly inverted H2 object (that is, the light illuminating the H1 is an exact inverse of the original H1 ref) and parallel polarisation. The polarisation of the H1 cannot really be controlled since the object will probably depolarise. Kaveh has written in hs PhD thesis that an object can be made artificially brighter by selectively altering the polarisation in some parts of the object. But, the polarisation of the H2 setup can be controlled since the H1 plate does not depolarise (usually, not always so if your shooting a photoelectric crystal like Lithium Niobate). If any of the assumptions are not valid, some corrections will be necessary.

BobH Sun Jun 19, 2011 8:58 am #8
By "index-matching", I don't mean putting the whole set-up in fluid. I mean using index matching oil to put a cover glass in contact with the emulsion of a silver-halide transmission master. The intermodulation noise caused by too low of a beam ratio is composed of relatively low frequency fringes. Those fringes cause ripples in the emulsion surface. Much of what we see as the intermodulation noise comes from that ripple. If a piece of glass is index-matched to that surface, the effect of those ripples disappears, and the image is waaaaaaaaaay cleaner. That allows lower beam ratio to be used to get the same level of noise around the image, and the image has higher diffraction efficiency as well, boosting the signal-to-noise ratio significantly.

Jeffrey Weil Sun Jun 19, 2011 9:33 am #10
The zone system is a way to adjust the development of your negative to compensate for contrasty or non contrasty lighting conditions. If the light on your subject is muddy, or too hot, you can use a spot meter to read a few spots on the subject, decide where you want those spots to fall tonally and adjust your development accordingly. That's not a complete covering of the subject but its the basics.

In holography we want the fringes to be as contrasty as possible all the time. Plus, objects in holography are usually way to small to use a spot meter, and even if you did adjust your development, things other than your subjects contrast would be effected way more than what your trying to achieve.

How exactly are you applying the zone system to holography? If it's just about your lighting technique and what lights you have on the subject, that is not the zone system. That is just lighting techniques, which can effect contrast and tones no doubt, but still not the zone system.

BobH Sun Jun 19, 2011 9:25 pm #17
I understand what Danny is saying, and have seen the effects he describes, but I've dealt with it a little differently. I'd have the main source, a general fill, and often a few other beams to make effects or deal with a weird shadow. Each beam has a beam splitter, at least two steering mirrors, a wave plate, a lens or spatial filter, and often a bigger transfer mirror. That's a lot that can wiggle!

If one of them does, the lighting balance seen through a polarizer in the plate holder will certainly change. In fact, it's guaranteed that with all that loss, the exposure time will be long. The changes in the lighting balance due to the relative diffraction efficiencies of each beam path could very easily be unique to each shot. Takes a lot of very stable components to get predictable results.

Then there's also the polarization consideration. Probably a good idea to set the lighting balance while looking at the object through a polarizer in the plate holder, but consider the "specularity" of the brightest spot on your object (for example). A very specular object area will be relatively polarization preserving, and much of the light hitting the plate will contribute to image fringes. A diffuse object area will record dimmer because half the light isn't recording the image, but worse, the wrongly polarized light makes those intermodulation fringes too, further changing the resulting lighting balance. Best thing for this issue is to use a polarizer in the plate holder in front of the plate being recorded. Then, what you get will be closer to what you saw. The downside? Eats up that expensive laser light and (sorry Dinesh), it's best done with index-matching fluid.

Your looking for perfect answers to a non perfect question, holography. Really it comes down to this, try a bunch of things and finds what works best for you. I promise you, if every one of us, in our own labs, did exactly everything the same using the exact same numbers, most of the results would be off.

Every lab and every holographer does things a little differently. That's part of the fun and frustration of it all. Besides, if you just hit the home run right away, what you would learn? Your going to get a lot more out of experimenting in your own place than anything anyone of us can say after you learn the basics. In this case, the basics are the ratio most people use are between 1:1 and 1:4, some go even lower on the object beam.

So, try 1:1 down to 1:4 and see where you get the best results.

As far as how bright something is, that's harder to do by yourself. There are so many variables that can confuse the results. What is the angle of view for example, an H2 with a greater angle of view can appear to be dimmer by the viewer it but in reality be more efficient. There are lots of things like that, what color is the reflection holograms image? A deep blue playback will always look dim compared to a green or yellow one at the same efficiency.

What I used to do starting out was I kept a little piece of a good hologram in my wallet. And every time I could I put it next to a better holographers work, under the same light, and compared. When you get together with other holographers, bring holo's. When you go to a show, put a piece of one in your pocket.

Holomark,

Where do you live, maybe another member can come over and make some holos with you.

And on your 4 inch vs 8 inch question, yep...the exposure would be 4x. It's called the inverse square law. Light falls off inversely proportional to the square of the distance it travels. In this case it's inverse to the square of the area it's lighting up. The law is really written for non spatially coherent light. Like from a light bulb. An un-diverged laser beam will not follow that rule. When you spread it out with a lens it does. There's more to it but those are the basics of the rule.

So, if you have a non coherent point source like a bulb if you double how far it travels and spreads out, its 1/4 as bright in any one spot. If it goes 4x it's 1/16th as bright in any one spot. It can also be applied to how far you spread it out. Spread it out 5 times more than another case and its 1/25th as bright.

Reading is great but shooting is better if your looking to be a good holographer. Now if you want to be a theory person, no need to shoot much. If your more interested in making great, world class holograms shooting will get you there, studying alone won't.

If you shoot a bunch of stuff in your lab soon you'll be able to guess the proper exposure within 10-20% just based on how the two beams look on a white card and your reference reading on your meter after you get your ratio set.

Making a bright holo with a good signal to noise ratio will come pretty easily with some shooting and learning time. To make a really finished professional hologram requires other techniques that are not really theory related. Tricks to reduce reflections, hiding mounts, learning how to make gear and hold objects and film so they don't move, etc....Not really theory stuff at all. You'll learn how to fix those problems as they come up.

Some people on this board work very hard to get a technique under their belt but then never go onto making a final finished piece using that method they worked so hard to figure out. Concentrate on making finished pieces, you'll have a lot more to show for your work in 20 years.

That's the same as setting a photographic exposure so the whites are not blown out and the shadows don't fall away. If you have a dark cat and you meter it, the meter will try to make it 18% gray, much brighter than the dark cat, expose at that level and everything will be overexposed, so anything white in the photo will be blown out in the neg, no information in that area.

If you have a bright white cat, your meter will want to also make that 18% gray, much darker than the cat. If you expose at that number everything will be underexposed and all the dark shadow stuff in your negative will drop out, in the print there will be no information there.

If you want to record the complete tonal range of your subject, you have to expose the film correctly. This has nothing to do with zone.

Then, referring to Kuhn, he says:

I happen to be a (somewhat lapsed) member of The Great Books Society. In this organisation, we study works that have created a fundamental change in The Human Condition in politics, economics, the sciences, art etc. Some of us may choose a particular book and lead a discussion on it. I led a discussion on Kuhn. So, when I see Kuhn described as such, it seems to me that there is perhaps a lack of clarity, if not accuracy.

The elaboration of paradigms is not, a la Kuhn, the central activity of a new science; according to Kuhn, it's the shift in an old science that occurs when an old theory has outlived it's original assumptions. These paradigm shifts do not occur because of "social factors" (even though social scientists seem to think of science as a social construct!), they occur because derivations and new experimental results point to a need to reformulate the older theories; there is no "new science". I would also say that holography is almost a poster child of Kuhnism, rather than "the case of holography does not follow these models well". Holography almost died in the 50's due to the inability to separate the twin images. The paradigm shift of rotating the reference angle saved holography from the dustbin of technical history. It's a simple thing, but most profound paradigm shifts are (think, for example, of the Copernican shift - it's a fairly simple shift with profound consequences). The (perhaps more subtle) paradigm shift of taking holography from essentially an imaging medium, a la the "microscopic principle of Gabor and the imaging principles of Leith and Upatniks, to a mechanism for generating optical functions also altered the holographic landscape, taking it from an imaging science to an engineering solution.

Of course, I realise that some of this may be controversial and so, as always, I welcome intellectual or academic debate. I have actually been quite interested in the applications of Kuhnian ideas on the evolution of holography, since holography straddles all three of the main occupation of human society - art, science and technology. As such, I always thought it may be an interesting evolution of the Kuhnian idea itself.
