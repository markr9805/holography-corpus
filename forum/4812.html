Ed Wesly Fri May 06, 2005 12:55 pm #2
Take a look at the optical layout of a microscope, for instance like in Melles Griot Optics Guide, and you will see that the real image of the microscope objective is further magnified by looking through the eyepiece. Notice that the real image of an object is focussed 165 mm (Royal Microscope Society Standards) from from the mounting flange of the objective to maintain the magnification marked on the objective.

Do a feasibility study; try focussing the image of an object through a microscope objective onto some groundglass to see what's going on. Replace the GG with a plate and introduce a reference beam and that image could certainly be recorded, and even further magnified with an eyepiece or magnifying glass. But the viewing position of the hologram is going to be dictated by the exit pupil of the optics.

When objects are magnified, their depth is magnified as the product of the lateral magnification. An object magnified by 10x in each the x and y axes will magnify 100x in the z axis. This basic optical fact was what killed the idea of minified holographic portraits in the '80's (and why holographic stereograms are preferred for less than life-size people images); if the head is reduced by 1/2 in horizontal and vertical, the depth is reduced by 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4, effectively flattening the head. The microscope would enlarge the z axis. But it's not noticed in the typical lab 'scope because it's viewed with only one eye. Just like the microscope holograms that were so popular in the holo boutiques of the golden age, where you could see the image when your eye was placed in the eyepiece position of the real image.

Holographic microscopy as a scientific application may be yet another one of those ideas whose time has come and gone, like 30 years ago. The main researcher in holographic microscopy was Mary Ellen Cox in the early '70's. The idea would be to holograph dynamic events with a ruby laser, then look at the objects microscopically. The big problem was laser speckle, and details would get lost in it. Not many papers were written on the topic since 1975.

The goal was to make samples that could be reproduced and viewed by students. The big advantage of holograms over photographs would be that you could focus throughout the depth of the sample space. But the objects would get lost in all the junk that inevitable ends up on optics and speckle.

Mock it up on the bench and tell us what you see!

Ed Wesly Wed May 11, 2005 11:18 am #5
Yes, the z-axis will be out of wack with the rest of the dimensions.

Your schematic doesn't look like it will be too easy to balance the beam ratios. I'd put the reference after a first beam splitter, not after the object beam.

The second lens for magnifying the real image of the microscope objective will have to be positioned close to the real image and it's real image will be far from the lens.

Magnification is the ratio of image distance to object distance: i/o. For instance, for a magnification of 5, the image distance is 5 times the object distance. For a good rundown of all the variations of image - object distances, focal length and magnification, see Popular Optics by Sam Brown available from Edmund.
