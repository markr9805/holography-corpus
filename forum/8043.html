favalora Sun Oct 25, 2009 1:39 am #2
Hi Joe -

I studied analog circuit design, but have forgotten almost everything I know. The only thing that jumped to mind was a simple "passive integrator circuit." Maybe the best way to explain it is to suggest that you see Fig. 1 in this Wikipedia article:

[http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_integrator_circuit](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_integrator_circuit) .

The values of R and C should be chosen such that RC is roughly the on-time (I guess it's actually 63% of on-time):

[http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Serie ... oltage.svg](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Series_RC_capacitor_voltage.svg)

If you have some basic electronics knowledge, you could do better if you estimated the impedance of your LED, so that you could work out how that impacts your choice of R and C.

[http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RC_circuit](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RC_circuit)

But there's no harm in trying. Grab a, say, 1 uF capacitor, and a 1 Megohm resistor, and try it. If you want a longer delay, try higher values of R or C, like 5 Megohms or 10 uF.

Maybe this URL will give you some comfort (ignore the op amp)

[http://www.wolframalpha.com/input/?i=in ... tor.vp_5+V](http://www.wolframalpha.com/input/?i=integrator&a=C.integrator-_Formula.dflt-&a=*FP.Integrator.SignalShape-_square&f3=0+V&f=Integrator.Vi_0+V&f4=1000000+ohms&f=Integrator.R_1000000+ohms&f5=1+microfarad&f=Integrator.C_1+microfarad&f6=8.5+s&f=Integrator.tf_8.5+s&f7=5+V&f=Integrator.vp_5+V)

Fine point: the LED light output won't be linear, so the switch-on might not appear as gradual as you're hoping.

-gregg

With a 9-volt battery and the LEDs I'm using (the ones in the thread "new 8mm and 10mm LEDs") they only need about 30 ohms resistance. Anything over that, and the light output goes down rapidly.

The LEDs series resistance should have no influence on the duration of ramp-up. If the R/C resistor value for the desired length of time is getting too large to allow full brightness of the LEDs you could try transistors with a higher current gain (HFE), a darlington transistor, or higher values for the capacitor.

The smallest and cheapest microcontroller (like a PIC12F508/509) would be good enough for the LED sunrise job. I guess they cost less than $1 a piece. There are very simple programmers for them too.

You could also eliminate the NE555 and do the timing with the microcontroller. As an added bonus the stand by current would be 100nA only, so virtually all of the batterie's capacity would be used up in a productive way for driving the LED's and not for waiting to do so. Well, almost, as 2/3 of the batteries energy is wasted for heating the LED's current limiting resistor anyway. A microcontroller could also help optimizing the power consumption of the LED's and getting some of those 2/3 back.

> I can connect that to a 555 or 7555 timer and the light can go on/off only when needed, say for 1 minute or so. So, if the batteries last 16 hours, that would be 960 cycles before the batteries need to be replaced.

There might be low consumption versions of the 555 but the ordinary ones would have a standby current of 5mA@9V and the 9V battery would be discharged very soon.

a_k Tue Oct 27, 2009 9:13 pm #12
Exactly. Compared to using 9V, boosting a lower voltage would be about double as efficient.

Series diodes cause a fixed voltage drop (still varying very little) while the voltage drop with a resistor causes a variable voltage drop which gets smaller with time. In both cases the emptier the battery gets, the less current would go through the LED. The percentage of power lost by a series diode would increase, that of a series resistor would decrease the emptier the battery gets. Therefore the total amount of power dissipated by the series diode would be larger. For a 3V battery and a 2.3V LED you could use a diode to drop the voltage but for a 9V battery and a 3.5V LED i would rather use a series resistor.

Let's hope that the PIR behaves as it should.

a_k Thu Oct 29, 2009 7:45 pm #18
Joe, the circuit behaves differently when a single 20mA LED is used. If you would use a 100-140mA LED and a 30-40 Ohm series resistor, the capacitor would get discharged within about 5 seconds after the battery is disconnected and a new startup would be from zero brightness again. A switch between the pot and +9V would, depending on its setting fade the LED in or out.

The LED gets its supply voltage directly from the battery, not from the capacitor, so as soon as the battery is disconnected, it turns off. 1000uF would not hold enough charge to directly supply an LED for several seconds. A switch between the pot and +9V would, depending on how it is set, fade the LED in or out.

For automatic fade in / fade out you could use the following circuit. It stays connected to the battery. When there is a positive voltage at the CTRL+ input the LED fades in and remains bright as long as the input is on high level. As soon as the input gets low, it fades out and remains dark.

![sunrise.jpeg](https://holographyforum.org/forum/download/file.php?id=6494)

I did notice the 3sec delay too. If you take a smaller capacitor, like 100-220uF, the fade in would start almost immediately and still last long enough if you set the pot higher than 100k.
