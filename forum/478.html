If it is running in multimode, then the new gratings are coming in at the same angles as the original gratings and so:

lambda/d = mlambda/d'

where d' is the new pitch. In this case the multimode multiplier, m, is (and we're expecting an integer):

m = d'/d = (3/16)in/200nm = 4.76(10)^(6)/200 = 23,800

So, multimode seems a little unlikely.

I use a Sabre laser to make hologram gratings almost every day. The lines you're seeing don't indicate multimode operation. If the path lengths are not equalized, you'd just get no recording if that was the case. The coherence length of that laser is about 20mm without an etalon. > dcgman: the proper equation to use for this set-up is d=lambda/(2*sin(theta))

It depends on whether it's a reflection geometry or a transmission geometry. If it's a reflection geometry then the formula is true only if the index of the recording material is unity. Inside a material, the wavelength shortens by the refractive index so that

lambda_material = lambda_air/n

In this situation, the denominator needs to be modulated by the index since the spatial frequency is caused by the nodes in the standing wave pattern. You also need to be careful which theta you're using. The traditional approach is to measure the angle with respect to the plate normal. However, if the formula you quote is meant to be that pertaining to a reflection geometry (that is, counterpropagating beams), then if theta is measured with respect to the plate normal, then theta is zero and d becomes infinite; in this situation, theta needs to be measured from the plate parallel.

However the quoted formula can also be the formula for two beams in a transmission geometry (since, by Euler's formula, sin(-theta) = -sin(theta) ) where the beams are symmetrically arranged on either side of the plate normal. However:

> Feng: ...using two beams (one direct and one bounced from a mirror)

would seem to indicate a reflection geometry. If such is the case, then the former applies, so theta is to be measured to the plate parallel and the index needs to be taken into account.

So, if the geometry is a transmission with the two beams symmetrically arranged on either side of the plate normal, then the angle on either side of the plate normal would be 65 odd degrees. My impression of the description led me to believe that it was a reflection geometry with two counterpropagating beams, hence the statement of my assumption:

> Dinesh: On the other hand, if the beams are counter-propagating, then...

> dcgman: YES!! -and inside the material the laws of refraction dictate that the sine of the angle of incidence gets divided by its refractive index. The geometry of the set-up in this particular case does not require one to take 'n' into consideration- the period of the fringe field set-up by the two interfering beams is the same in air and in the substrate.

True, if the geometry were a transmission geometry. Then the n in the denominator due to the wavelength shortening would cancel the n due to the refraction

d = lambda/2sin(theta) (1)

But, inside the material, the refracted beam makes an angle theta' given by,as you point out, sin(theta') = sin(theta)/n, and lambda'= lambda/n. So now:

d' = lambda'/2sin(theta') = (lambda/n)/(2sin(theta)/n) = n(lambda/2nsin(theta)) =d

And the n's do cancel out.

But, if the geometry were a reflection geometry with both beams hitting the plate head-on from opposite sides - a counterpropagating beam geometry - then there is no refraction, since the angle of incidence is normal. However, the wavelength is still shortened from lambda to lambda/n. The angles now need to be taken from the plate parallel (otherwise theta = 0) and

d = lambda/(n2sin(pi/2)) = lambda/(2*n) (2)

> Feng: using two beams (one direct and one bounced from a mirror)

But, where was the mirror?

If the mirror was directly "behind" the plate, ie the recording plate was between the light source and the mirror (assuming a collimated light source) and parallel to the mirror, or to put it perhaps more succinctly, the incoming k vector was directly opposed to the reflected beam, then there is no refraction because the beams are hitting the plate at normal incidence. Then the Bragg planes form along the nodes of the standing wave and these are at the (shortened) wavelength peaks. Well, actually the peaks of the square of the wavelength. In this case, equation 2 holds and the index needs to be taken into account..

If the light hit a beamsplitter and then a mirror with the beamsplitter and the mirror on the same side of the plate, that is, the beam hits the beamsplitter, then part of the beam passes through to the plate and part is reflected to a mirror which is tilted so as to direct the reflected beam onto the plate, you have a transmission geometry. In this geometry there is no need to take the index into account and equation 1 holds.

I assumed a reflection geometry from the description, in which case the numbers were odd. If the geometry were transmission, then your calculation is correct.
