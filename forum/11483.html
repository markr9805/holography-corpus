I finally tried G307 and it worked very well, after only two tries. The hologram below was done today.

![P1010027.JPG](https://holographyforum.org/forum/download/file.php?id=8031)

The paper: (has it been 20 years already?)

[https://holowiki.org/w/images/8/8b/Sympos2006.pdf](https://holowiki.org/w/images/8/8b/Sympos2006.pdf)

It only needed 20mJ at 532nm. The bottom area shows the transition from narrow to broadband, which is very bright. With an incident power at the center of the plate of 2mW, the exposure time was 10 seconds (a 1 watt laser was used). In the original paper, gelatin with a small amount of potassium dichromate and glycerol was coated and oven-hardened at 100C for 20 minutes. In the above, I omitted the glycerol, and baked for about 3.5 hours. Gelatin was photo grade 280 bloom.

These days, I'm more of the opinion that pre-hardening the emulsion by baking, then diffusing dichromate, etc., is a more repeatable way of doing things. Getting the hardness just right is so crucial for DCG, and it seems I can get better control of the hardness this way. Ageing the entire emulsion by dark reaction takes a long time for me, and I believe that the level of hardening is influenced by variations in temperature and humidity (which are not easy to control) during the dark reaction period. This was noted in US patent 4422713. They used a dye-spot diffusion test to check final hardness. I recently found out about the "pencil hardness test" which I'm trying now (it's interesting, but I don't know yet if it will be helpful for DCG):

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j5Qd00uJDMQ&t=9s](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j5Qd00uJDMQ&t=9s)

Din Sat Mar 21, 2026 2:32 pm #4
From the paper:

"For over 30 years it has become almost routine

in the literature on holography using DCG to comment on

the difficulty of understanding what is happening in the

post-exposure treatment baths and yet there has been littleattempt to question the wisdom of simultaneously usinga proprietary fix and hardener designed only for use inphotographic processing." (my italics)

I have heard of pre-hardening using baking, as I recall Chris Rich at POC tried this back in 1986 or 1987. I asked Don Broadbent about baking the plate in 2000, since he'd been playing with DCG since the 70's, but he said he'd never tried it and had never hearrd of anyone else trying it (a la the comment that everyone had always used fixer to pre-harden).. At any rate, at POC, we would shift the reflectivity to below the recording wavelength by post development baking. Chris asked why we don't use the same method in the first, pre-hardening, development stage, and tried it; it worked quite well, as I recall, and he got efficiencies of over an O.D. of 4 (99.9% efficient).

At POC, I was tasked with trying to get a narrow band, almost vertical, reflectivity curve, as opposed to the broad band reflectivity. Since we were making high reflectivity mirrors in DCG, the broad band response led to losing efficiency. I reasoned that the broadening was caused by differential swelling of the layers, ie as the plate was immersed in the water, the top layers first started expanding, then the lower layers expanded while the top layers were still expanding leading to a variation in Bragg plane spacing. So, I thought to harden the upper layer of emulsion by exposing the plate to UV before exposure, causing a delay in the initial top layer swelling. This worked quite well.

However, the (obvious? ) thought occurred. If pre-exposure UV hardened the plate, why not a post exposure UV treatment, ie replace the fixer with UV. It took some trial and error, but I finally got the right dose of UV exposure to replace the fixer with the same results. I also tried to use Chrome Alum (Chromium(III) potassium sulfate dodecahydrate ) to pre-harden, but that pre-hardened too much.

As for using glycerol, I believe in about 2010 or so Nadya ( Denisyuk's student in Moscow who later worked in LA) told me that Denisyuk had tried it, and told me she'd find the paper; but she never did.

Thanks for the info. I remembered the description of your pre-exposure UV to harden the upper level to encourage a narrowband result (posted earlier here on the forum), so I recently bought a 365nm light source for this purpose:

[https://www.amazon.com/dp/B085W7B5V3?re ... asin_title](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B085W7B5V3?ref=ppx_yo2ov_dt_b_fed_asin_title)

Haven't done any testing yet, either pre-exposure or post-exposure.

The myriad factors contributing to hardening of DCG (and techniques applied to different parts of the process) are overwhelming. Quick story: I did some measuring of layer hardness using the pencil test (link above). The DCG layer I used had a reduced amount of dichromate (0.5%) which was for the G307 process. After baking for 3.5 hours, I did a pencil test and the layer had a pencil hardness of "7H" (very hard). Over time, this hardness became less and less, until finally today I measured it to be "H" which is 7 points less, and very soft. In fact, a similar layer which had not been baked at all, had a hardness of "3H" which is three points harder than the baked/aged sample. Yet the final result (in the photo) was quite good.

I was playing around with the AI and found out about the pencil test. The AI stated over and over again that the pencil test was very suitable for DCG, and that an optimal hardness was between 2H and 4H. Yet, when I asked the AI specifically if any published information was available, either in the literature or online, regarding the pencil test for DCG, it said there was none

Another AI adventure was asking about hardening the amino vs. carboxyl groups in DCG. I was always under the impression that light-hardening occurs mostly with the carboxyl groups, and that it's better to harden the amino groups when the plates are pre-hardened, to leave the carboxyl groups free for light-hardening. I asked if heating favored crosslinking of the amino or carboxyl groups of gelatin, and got both answers depending on how the question was phrased. Anyway, the results were good with heating, so maybe I should just leave the AI alone.
