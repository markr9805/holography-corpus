Ok, perhaps the result of this calculation is useless, but it's interesting nonetheless.

I am trying to see how many AgBr molecules are inside a grain of AgBr.

1] The molecular weight of AgBr is 187.77 g/mol.

2] The density of AgBr is 6.473 g/cm^3.

- If I divide [1] by [2] I should get the volume of 1 mole of AgBr: 29 cm^3/mol.

- If I divide that number by Avogadro's number I should get the volume of 1 molecule: 4.8E-23 cm^3 = 4.8E-29 cubic meter.

- Suppose a grain's size is 10nm. That means that the volume (V=0.75pir^3) of that grain is: V = 0.753.141(5E-9)^3 = 2.95E-25

- Division the volume of the grain by the volume of a AgBr molecule should give the amount of molecules in the grain: 2.95E-25/4.8E-26 = 6146 molecules.

Only 6146 molecules in a grain of 10nm? Surely that cannot be right, right? Wow, amazing. I always thought in much much bigger numbers...

So, if I now had made an emulsion with 1 gram of silver nitrate in it, there would be 0.00588 mol of silver molecules there. That means there are 3.5E+21 molecules of AgBr in the finished emulsion.

- If I divide the amount of AgBr molecules by the amount of molecules in one grain I should get the amount of grains: 5.8E+17 grains.

- If I multiply the amount of grains by their surface (A=4pir^2=43.141(5E-9)^2= 3.141E-16 square meter) area I should get the total combined surface area of all the grains: 5.8E+17*3.1E-16 = 181 square meters.

Now, suppose that this emulsion contained 2 gram of gelatin and was coated on a piece of glass of 60x40cm, then cut into pieces of 10x15cm. This would have the result that we now have 16 pieces of glass with a total combined grain surface area of 181 square meter. Or ONE holographic plate would contain 181/16 = 11 SQUARE METERS of grain surface area.

How's that for amazing!!!

Now, that number is just plain silly. I must be doing something wrong here... Am I?
Ed Wesly Thu Feb 19, 2009 2:23 pm #2
Actually I think that you are in the ballpark!

Let's look at it slightly different way: Silver halide crystals are neither spherical, as you assumed in your calculations, nor cubical, as I will do, but some sort of hexagonal shape, the Greeks probably had a word for it.

If we have a cube (using that as it's easier to visualize) 10 nm on a side, with 10 atoms (notice I'm not using AgX molecules) (since 1 Angstrom is about the size of an atom, 10 Angstroms to the nanometer) then the cube would hold 100^3 atoms, or 10,000! since a sphere of 10 nm diameter has less volume than a cube, it appears that you are correct!

Yeah, it's kind of hard to believe that there are a countable amount of silver and halogen atoms or more properly ions in a grain, but we're talking about things that are only about 100 atoms in diameter!

Apple was touting 43 nm technology in some recent ads, I guess for the width of the traces in their chips, which again is approximately 430 atoms wide. Somewhere in the not too distant future we are going to have to reach the limit of nano-miniaturization, finding the minimal amount of atoms in a "wire" that is capable of conducting electricity!

Ed Wesly Mon Feb 23, 2009 10:01 pm #5
Speaking about that 10 nm grain size, I found out an interesting fact regarding those tiny grains as used in the Slavich PFG-03M emulsions. It seems that almost everyone agrees that it goes black almost immediately in developer, with the conclusion that the plates are fogged somewhere along the line.

I had dinner with Stas from Geola and Hans Bjelkhagen a few weeks ago, and they explained that it wasn’t light that was fogging the grains but the oxygen in the air! Seems like that “countable infinity” of atoms lets the oxygen get into the center of the grain and it becomes developable.

They were saying that this is why the supply of Ultimate plates is sometimes iffy, and that a company in Russia called Spherus (sic?) made film with that size grain, and the center of the width of the roll was not fogged but it was toward the edges. It also explains why Kodak, Agfa, Ilford, Fuji all let the grains remain at the less than optimal resolution of 35 – 50 nm, otherwise the plates would act fogged by the time the customer got them.

So I would suggest that if you were to use this emulsion, a quick dip in Jeff’s Fog Busting Formula might be in order. Another useful PFG-03M trick I got from TJ, who suggested a dip in a cold water (less than room temperature) pre developer bath helped keep the emulsion on the plate during processing! My lab is out of commission at the moment so I haven't tried it yet.
