Stereofan -

Regarding your first question about the underlying principles of holography, I think the best way to bring your understanding beyond what's in the popular press is to sit down and slog through some well-known texts. I like Benton's MIT Media Lab course notes ([here](http://mas450.syntheticholography.org/reading/chaptersPDF/index.html)), and Hecht's [Optics](http://mas450.syntheticholography.org/reading/chaptersPDF/index.html) is good if you already know some physics.

You mentioned magnetic holography. Come to think of it, I've heard of acoustic holography, but not magnetic holography. Do magnetic fields undergo interference the way E-fields do? We spend so much time ignoring the B field that I just don't know. I suppose they do!

I wanted to respond to your mentioning self-similarity (such as the way ferns and mountains look similar regardless of your magnification). Holographic "fringes" aren't self-similar in that way. They're just very tiny patterns, on the order of 1,000-2,000/mm, like zebra stripes and curves and things. The patterns look different at different scales. Here's an electron microscope image of a simple hologram from an article by Jeff Blyth at The Holographer [(Fig. 1.)](http://www.holographer.org/articles/hg00002/hg00002.html).

Rather, if you're referring to how some holograms "contain" the whole image even if you break it into pieces... well, that's something I lack the narrative subtlety to explain. I actually think that's a confusing notion that's meant, rather, to show how holograms act like windowpanes.

Complex systems? No, I'm not aware of any relation to that field, though I wish there were. You could imagine using electro-optics with some weird imaginary holographic material to simulate complex systems, but I have no concrete thoughts about that.

-g

There are some clichés when it comes to explaining how holography works:

One is the notion that a hologram records phase as well as amplitude. While ths is strictly true, it is not helpful to understanding the process, except to the theoretical physicist.

The other is the cliché of each part of the hologram containing the whole information. This is not true. Each part might contain information about the whole object, but only from a limited viewpoints.

> Kaveh: There are some clichés when it comes to explaining how holography works:One is the notion that a hologram records phase as well as amplitude. While this is strictly true, it is not helpful to understanding the process, except to the theoretical physicist.

I think this is still useful! It is just when we say that we don't define phase and amplitude. It is really simply however. Amplitude is how bright a point is. Dim, low amplitude, bright, high amplitude. This is the same as photography.

Phase is the new thing and what makes holography possible. So let me take a stab at it from my 10th grade educational level. If we have two waves they have the unique ability to interfere constructively and destructively. Now what that means is when the peaks of two waves are at the same place then the wave is twice as high (constructive) and when a wave and a valley meet then the wave height is nothing (destructive). We use this concept and the reference beam to record phase.

Now we have phase recorded what does it mean? Well it gives us the relative time that the waves take from each point on the object to each point on the plate. This sounds like it is not very useful but it turns out that it is quite useful. From every viewpoint we end up with a pattern of constructive and destructive interference. Since this changes literally with every viewpoint the image does as well. The lucky thing for us is that happens to be the proper views to reconstruct a 3-D image. (It really is not simply lucky but falls out of the math.)
