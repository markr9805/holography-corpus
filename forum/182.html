Dinesh Fri Feb 25, 2011 2:05 pm #4
> holorefugee: you lose. 8% every reflection

Not so. This is a hangover from the old days. Glass with an index of 1.5 ("standard" glass) has a 4% reflectivity for normal incidence. Usually, in a holographic setup, the incidence is rarely normal. At any other angle, the reflectivity ("loss", if you will) is higher, being of course zero at p polarisation at Brewsters angle. Also, for holographic setups, you rarely reflect off glass,; usually, you reflect of a metallic (or dielectric) surface, for which the index is not 1.5. My suspicion is that, back in the day when the artists were first venturing into holography, some technical person - physicist or engineer - told them that the mirror will lose 4% without telling them why 4%. Someone later "figured out" that if it's 4% going in, then it's 4% at the back surface, hence 8%. However, the loss of 4% at an air-glass interface for glass at an index of 1.5 is not the same as the reflectivity at a glass-air interface. The light sees different index changes.

The reflectivity is a proportion of light that's reflected as a ratio of the light hitting the mirror. Hence if I_0 hits a mirror, then at normal incidence for glass of 1.5, the reflected beam has an intensity of 0.96*I_0. At s polarisation, the reflectivity increases - slowly at first, then a lot faster, till at around 80 degrees or so, the reflectivity is close to 90%. At p polarisation the reflectivity falls until Brewster's angle, at which point it's zero. Then it begins to rise until it meets the s polarisation curve, ie about 90% at 80 degrees. If the reflectivity were always 8%, then it'd be pointless shooting holograms with a reference at Brewster's!
Dinesh Fri Feb 25, 2011 6:01 pm #5
> Dinesh: Hence if I_0 hits a mirror, then at normal incidence for glass of 1.5, the reflected beam has an intensity of 0.96I_0.

Just occurred to me, this is wrong. The intensity is 0.04I_0. At normal incidence on a plane glass sheet with index 1.5, most of the light (96% of it) passes right through. The reflectivity at normal incidence from a medium of index n(i) (usually 1 for air) to a medium of n(t) (usually taken to be glass at n(t) = 1.5) is:

R = [(n(t) - n(i))/(n(t) + n(i))]^2

For glass n(t) = 1.5 and so

R_dielectric = [1.5 - 1)/(1.5 + 1)]^2 = (0.2)^2 = 0.04

However, for a metallic surface, the reflectivity at normal incidence is around 40% - 50%, depending on the electrical conductivity of the metal. The absorption of light in a metal depends on the conductivity. The refractive index is split into a real and a complex component:

n = n_r + n_i.

The complex component denotes the amount of absorption. So, the reflectivity of a metal at normal incidence is:

R_metal = ((n_r - 1)^2 + n_i^2)/((n_r + 1)^2 + n_i^2))

The n_r and n_i are wavelength dependent, since the absorption is wavelength dependent. In other words, the reflectivity for different lasers will change.

Once again the famous conflict between the practical and the mathematical. Holorefugee’s graph is essential, and hopefully all mirrors manufactured follow that same reflectance curve. But I have seen reflectance vary all over the place, with some mirrors leaking light through their backs, maybe better as beamsplitters than mirrors. Bob’s suggestion is right on, you should test them all to find the pick of the litter.

Possibly the takeouts might have enhanced or protective coats, so that they might be easily cleaned in the field, as the home or office can get pretty dirty and smoky. Since some of the applications may be white light, the substrates might not be very flat, so reflecting a spread, preferably spatially filtered beam and looking for fringes should be another phase of the checkout procedure. I have seen 10 -15 year old plain aluminum mirrors attacked by the atmosphere of the lab they were stored in with stains that neither acetone nor methanol soaks could remove.

The expensive dielectric mirrors are usually wavelength and angle sensitive, usually 0 or 45 degrees, which is constricting for creative set ups. I have seen mirrors designed for 45 degree incidence at the He-Cd wavelength of 442 nm used with the 515 nm of Argon in a perversion of Bragg diffraction. These types of mirrors are useful when you are working with large power lasers, as the 90 – 92% reflectivity implies a loss of 8 – 10 %, and where does that go? Maybe some transmission, but mostly absorption by the coating, which could be catastrophic to the coating in an undiverged multi-Watt beam.

Dinesh Mon Feb 28, 2011 1:14 pm #8
> holorefugee: I am not sure how brewester's angle or the refractive index of the substrate has anything to do with a first surface mirror.

The whole point about Brewsters angle is that the reflectivity goes to zero for p (TM) polarisation at Brewsters angle. This is because when the sum of the incident and transmitted rays add to 90 degrees, the polarisation vector for p polarisation is along the ray direction of the reflected ray. This is, of course impossible, so that the reflected ray vanishes. From Snell:

n(i) sin(theta_B) = n(t) sin(theta_t)

where n(i) is the index of the incident medium, usually air

theta_B is Brewsters angle

n(t) is the index of the transmitted medium, usually glass

theta_t is the angle of transmission.

Since, for Bresters condition,:

theta_t = (pi/2) - theta_B (for reasons mentioned above)

We have:

tan(theta_B) = n(t)/n(i)

For an air glass interface (light going from air to glass of index 1.5) n(t)=1.5 and n(i)=1, giving Brewsters angle:

theta_B = arctan(1.5) = 56.31 degrees.

> holorefugee: ...how brewester's angle...has anything to do with a first surface mirror.

It has to do with a first surface mirror because if the first surface is a dielectric, it will obey Brewsters law and have an angle at which there is no reflection for p polarisation. For metals, the situation is slightly more complicated. Metals are considered as a plasma of electrons for light reflectivity and transmissivity. Thus the plasma frequency and conductivity will determine the Brewster condition. However, there is a Brewster condition for metals.

> holorefugee: ...or the refractive index of the substrate has anything to do with a first surface mirror

The refractive index of the substrate determines the reflectivity of the substrate as per the Fresnel equations:

r(s) = (n(i) cos(theta_i) - n(t) cos(theta_t))/(n(i) cos(theta_i) + n(t) cos(theta_t)

t(s) = 2n(i)cos(theta_i)/(n(i) cos(theta_i) + n(t) cos(theta_t)

r(p) = (n(t) cos(theta_i) - n(i)cos(theta_t))/(n(i) cos(theta_t) + n(t) cos(theta_i))

t(p) = 2 n(i) cos(theta_i)/(n(i) cos(theta_t) + n(t) cos(theta_i))

p = reflectivity/transmissivity for s polarisation

t = reflectivity/transmissivity for p polarisation.

If the substrate is metallic, these don't really hold, since the optical properties of metals are different to those of dielectrics. However, if the front surface is a dielectric, then the reflectivity will change as per angle and polarisation state.

One reason I say this is that I've always heard that each mirror "loses" 4% for any angle for any polarisation, regardless whether it be a metallic surface or a dielectric surface. Firstly, if this is true, then hitting the plate at Brewster's angle will not get rid of woodgrain. Woodgrain exists because of back reflection and, at p polarisation at Brewsters angle, there is no back reflection. However, if the back surface of the glass loses only 4% on reflection - regardless of angle of incidence and polarisation - then there will be a 96% reflection at the back surface, and therefor woodgrain, no matter what the polarisation or angle. Plastics are birefringent, so the situation is slightly different depending on the optic axis of the plastic. Also, the impression is that you can calculate reflection losses by simply counting mirrors and adding up "4%"'s. Not so. However the curves given are true for metals in general (though I very much doubt that any mirror is actually 100% reflective, because if it was, there'd be an infinite number of conduction electrons - true for an ideal metal, but never in practice!

Dinesh Wed Mar 02, 2011 12:44 pm #16
Also, keep your optics clean. This is especially true for sand table users. My practice is always to put unused optics away into a cupboard so dust does not settle on the surface. If you can't, cover the smaller mirrors/lenses with a plastic sandwich bag (ziplock) and the larger optics with a trash can liner.

One other thing I've noticed, maybe a little off the intent here, is that it's important to dismantle and put away optics after you're done with a shot. This way, each new shot starts with "fresh" setups. One reason (learned from experience!) is that a mirror, say, is not properly secured. If you re-use it for another shot, you usually assume it's OK, since it worked for the first shot. However, the second shot may need a longer exposure and, while the partially insecured mirror may have worked for the first, it won't for the second.
