JohnFP Tue Oct 16, 2007 1:55 pm #4
Raul, can't you just seal your holograms from the environment? I scrape emulsion off the edge of the plate, about a 5mm, with a razor blade. Then mix up some 5 minutes epoxy and place a bead around the edge of the plate where I removed the emulsion. Then place another clear glass plate on top. It is named the o-ring sealing technique and I have DCG holograms done this way for many years that have not shifted at all. Be sure the holograms is as dry as possible when doing it.

If you want to bring back a hologram that has been exposed to moisture, just reprocess it from the water and through the alcohol baths.

Ok, now I follow you.

Yes, I would take one of your holograms and place it in a concentration of water and sugar. Maybe start with 5%. But I would then add a 5% sugar to each of your alcohols also and see what happens. I have found in playing with sugar as a post swelling agent, that if you add too much the sugar starts to crystalize and ruins the hologram. I think I tried salt also with similar results. This was for silve halide emulsion and gave me a shift from deep blue/violet to dark green. It may be you only have to add the swelling agent to the last alcohol bath ,but I am not sure.

I would be nice if you could try another technique. Place the processed hologram in a solution of AmDi or KDi and water. About 5%. Then when the hologram is in the solution expose it to a bright Quartz Halogen Light for a couple of minutes. I have not had a chance to fully test this idea but I did have good results in my preliminary tests. Hopefully I will be in the lab more and should get a chance to fully test it soon.

Yes, it sounds plausible you get a large wavelength shift due to your loss of glycerol (and water!). Moreover, in a high humidity environment (which I suspect, is the case in Cuba), the layer may quickly attract water. I am particularly thinking of the alcohol “drying” step with warm air. Not entirely familiar with the physics behind, I guess there are two conflicting “forces” involved here mainly: the warm/hot air stream (from a hairdryer etc.) and the tendency of the alcohol to cool its environment upon evaporation (leading to condensation possibly).

The condensation effect may be lowered somewhat by warming the plate on either side (e.g putting the plate on a heated surface and applying the hairdryer to the gelatin side).

I do use the same tecnique but with a little change in order to make the glass sandwich more tollerant to quick temperature changes.

If you seal the hologram in wet environment, eg 65% R.H., the water will be trapped between the plates may condense if the hologram is moved from a cold to a warmer environment . The results of water condensation are well known: blue shifting and/or lost if image.

To improve temperature changes immunity, I do remove most of moisture still present in the emulsion as follow:

1- Put a clear, clean and dust free glass plate of identical dimensions of hologram plate in a oven set to around 100C.

2- Gently heat for a few minutes the hologram with a hair dyer set to hot

3- Put the hologram into the oven set to 100C for ~10minutes.

Then I seal the hot hologram and the hot clear plate as described by John

Pre-heating the hologram with hot air (Step2) is mandatory if you don't want to ruin your hologram. In fact if a cold hologram is put into a warm/hot oven, water contained into the emulsion quickly consense before evaporating with destructive effects.

Yes a good question. If you are doing MBDCG then without any need for a delay before water treatment I would first of all do the normal water treatment with initially cold water ~20C to get rid of all excess dichromate and glycerol (tap water maybe OK for this) . (If you are however using DCG method rather than MBDCG there is a useful point of difference to remember. Where the dichromate has had to react with light initially itself rather than light having to initially react with the MB dye and then the light-affected MB reacting with the dichromate, it is best to leave the DCG plate a while before putting it in water to give the light acivated dichromate time to increase its effective chemical bonding to the gelatin. However with MBDCG this does not seem to be needed)

I would then having washed the plate in cold water , put it in carefully controlled warm water containing a high concentration of citric acid. Around say 13g citric acid in a measuring cylinder topped up to the 100ml mark with water . How "warm" should this be is I am afraid only found by trial and error and depends on your gelatin grade and the degree of bias hardening before exposure. --- Perhaps 26C

is a place to start . Then wipe off your excess solution and plunge into

100% or near 100% alcohol (preferably isopropyl alcohol). Now this goes against the traditional method of using wetter alcohol baths and then 100%

alcohol at the end. The reason I suggest doing this is that the citric acid has hardly any solubility at all in 100% alcohol but you could lose much of it in say 70/30 alcohol . Because the gelatin is full of citric acid it appears in the limited testing I tried that you can go straight into 100% alcohol without getting uneven drying affects that might occur without citric acid.

Keep us posted.
