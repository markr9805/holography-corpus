There is something in what you say. There are advantages to making a system that is circularly symmetric. An example is the alcove hologram developed by Benton at MIT.

I can't immediately see the advantage in this case, but my feeling is that there is something in it.

Regarding reduced aberrations, my approach is always to reduce aberrations, so they are as close to zero as possible. This will be the case if you have all reconstruction beams be the precise conjugate of the reference beams, both for H1 and for H2. Then there will be no geometrical aberrations in either case.

One possible advantage is that a curved surface might be more difficult for the eye to focus on, so the illusion of an object floating in space might be more real. [/i]
Dinesh Tue Jun 29, 2004 5:26 am #7
Making an H2 on a curved surface, whether concave or convex, does not intrinsically have any advantage in terms of aberrations. Aberrations are caused by a mismatch of the reference conditions to the reconstruction conditions. Note that it isn't just the difference between the beam divergences of the two beams but also the wavelengths. Petzval distortions in holography are primarily due to mismatch in the beam divergence, but coma and astigmarism occur due to mismatches in beam shape and direction. In some cases not all aberations can be made to disappear. There's also longitudinal and transverse magnifications due to wavelength mismatch.

As I've frequently stated, in every hologram it's necessary to "think backwards". If you intend your hologram to be on a curved surface, you make the H1 on a curved surface. If you make a single-beam Denisyuk on a curved surface and reconstruct using standard room illumination, you have a bunch of distortions. The geometry of the object and it's position relative to the plate determine these distortions. By thinking backwards, you can create a particular effect by taking advantage of the distortions.

The "alcove" hologram was a case in point of "thinking backwards". The intended view was calculated and the hologram was made taking this into account. In fact, there are programs that can calculate the distortion due to the slits in a rainbow, so that computer generated holograms have an inverse distortion programmed into them. This, by the way, was pioneered by the Benton team at The Media Lab. I got a preview of it by one of the people who worked on it back in the late 80's.

Dinesh Thu Jul 01, 2004 2:48 pm #9
[quote:4f324b5aa5]Are the calculations for inversed distorsion in stereograms complicated? Could they be performed using an image program like Photoshop?[/quote:4f324b5aa5]

Not really, they're simple distortions in the graphics by certain matrix transformations. The matrix elements determine the distortions and you pass the image through the "matrix sausage machine". When I first saw them, they were done on the MIT machine, I think it was a cray. But this was 15 years ago. Today, they probably could be done in photoshop, but you'd have to create the matrix elements yourself. There's a filter in photoshop that allows you to set your own matrix elements. In photoshop 6 it's in <filter - other - custom>. The exact elements can probably be found in papers relating to distortions due to slits. I know of one by Francis Tuffy, but I never actually saw the paper - he never sent me a copy and I have no idea where it was published.

Does it matter? Probably not. Unless you're doing something involving scientific data, you probably won't notice the distortions anyway.
