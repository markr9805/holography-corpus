JohnFP Thu Dec 04, 2008 6:14 pm #14
Very simply, picture some on axis (or as close as possible) transmission fringes. If you look at a cross section of these fringes and only look one fringe layer deep you will notice the cross section looks like a repeating sine wave with hills and valleys. Now if one of your beams move, lets say after 45 seconds on a 60 second exposure, and the movement holds and the movement was exactly 1/2 wavelength, then you will get some heavy fringes (hills and valleys) where they were to start and for the 45 seconds, and then for the last 15 seconds the hills will be trying to form where valleys were and valleys where hills were. As you can see it would make the constructive areas not as strong and the destructive areas would get light. This would result in a dim hologram. I mention only 1/2 wavelength but it could have moved 1/4 or 3/4 and that too would start to take away from the distict profile of the original fringes. Also, the hologram could be stable for a long period then move to 1 1/2 or 2 1/2 for a short time, same issue, dim hologram.

Now if your movement was again 1/2 wavelength but happened during the entire exposure you would not get a hologram at all.

The typical banding you see on an object or film in which are defined as movment fringes are usually the result of a very stable set up for most of the exposure time with movement for a lesser time. The number of dark bands in the hologram can be counted as the multiple number of fringes the fringes displaced. But this too will cause a slight dimming of the hologram.

Sometimes, you get an air current that may displace the fringes but then they return.

Sometimes, you get movement of an object only after it has heated up from the laser light.

You have to remember that the standing waves are a result of the two light beams interacting on some angle. When the two beams are overlapping (no angle difference) and coming from the same side of the plate, the distance between crest to crest is exactly the same as the original wavelength of each wave (theorecically, but in actuallity no fringes would form). As you start to move one beam off the perfect normal and at a slight angle to the second beam, the fringes start to get smaller. This is because in essence the crest to crest of the slightly angled beam is shorter with comparison to the depth of the emulsion. Thus the interference between that beam and the original beam slightly shortens the fringes spacing. It also starts to move that perfet cross section of hills and valley to a slight angle. If you looked at the cross section again, you would see the hills are starting to lean. FInally when the two beam are 180 degress apart (both on axis but one from front and one from back), those hills have completely laid down to form a type of plane (constructive planes) and the valleys have oppositely laid down to form destructive planes. Now the fringes are stacked horizontally compared to the face of the emulsion. Our reflection holograms. Of course this is a simple explaination and surely with multiple angles from an object there is much overlapping of fringes but you get the idea.

Colin Kaminski Sun Dec 07, 2008 12:37 am #20
My experience seems to fit with the idea that for the entire hologram to move the plate needs to move relative to the object. Usually if the plate is moving you can find a hinge point where one part has moved more than another. Someone sent me a good example once. It was very bright from all angles except one and if you moved through the very slightly dim view point you could see where the hinge point in the movement was.

When looking for movement I find it takes me about an hour to really see everything the plate has to offer so don't rush yourself.
