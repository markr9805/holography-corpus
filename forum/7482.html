Joe Farina Thu Aug 14, 2008 11:29 am #1
Yesterday I was looking at the above book at a local library. The book itself is too expensive to buy, considering the amount of jibbersih it contains, but the sections on the "scientific" origins of holography appear to be well done. When reading the section on Yuri Densiyuk, I realized that this was the clearest basic desription, in English, of his early work. It is definitely a plus when it's desribed by a person with some degree of objectivity.

Anyway, it told how Denisyuk, in 1959 made a hologram using his layout, with a filtered light source, of some "tiny concave mirrors." They must have been very small indeed, if the coherence length of his source was a few tenths of a millimeter, as was stated. Apparently he was trying this basic process for some time, and having troubles, due to the slowness of the silver halide material he was using, but after collaborating with Protas, they managed to make an emulsion that was capable of "doing the job" and in 1959 got some real results. It seems that Denisyuk was only around 32 at the time. I think all of this stuff had been going on in complete ignorance of Gabor's work.

Ed Wesly Thu Aug 14, 2008 2:04 pm #5
I finally got a copy of the book when it dipped below $100 on Amazon. I doubt if it will ever come out in paperback, since it has a limited audience. (I write it off on taxes!)

There is some gibberish (this is the correct spelling) every once in a while, but that's just the author getting into his mode as a science historian and the ways that they compile ideas and trends. The jargon of his trade. But this guy is a great historian, as he really gets to the bottom of all the stuff of legend and hearsay. And the fact that he is from outside the field makes his magnum opus very objective.

I got to hear Denisyuk give his spiel on his inspiration first hand when he came to Lake Forest College the first time. I really appreciated the science fiction origin! He also mentioned that the mirrors he used were almost flat, not very concave, because of the limited coherence of the Hg lamp. And he was amazing people with the pseudoscopic image, which acted as a convex mirror. He also said that he did not know anything about Gabor when he started, but found out later, and then added him to the only other reference in his seminal paper, the one of Lippmann.

I was quite pleasantly surprised by the book, being mentioned in it 4(!) times, twice indexed, the others footnotes, with one mention in the text that is the best epithet I could have ever given myself in the history of holography!
