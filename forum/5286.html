Howdy y'all, Recently picked up a Sekonic L558 light meter for my camera bag. I can convert EV readings to Lux or Foot Candles. Being mathematically challenged I am having a difficult time trying to figure out how all those film characteristic curves that plot density versus exposure account for TIME, when all I see to achieve a density of 2 is some value like 25 uJ/cm^2, nothing to say about duration. For example, I am presently getting from my D&S laser spread for a 4 x 5 plate: 14 Lux, or 2.05 uJ/cm^2. On the S&M light meter this reads 20 on Scale 2. My only reference to determine exposure values have been the tables presented in Unterseher et al 1982. If I can now read directly in Lux or uJ/cm^2, how can I use these values to determine exposure TIME based on a published film characteristic curve? For instance if my film curve shows 25 uJ/cm^2 to achieve density of 2 do I go for 12 seconds with the above values?

How do y'all go about PREDICTING exposure values aside from running test strips? Cheers, James McClean
BobH Fri Dec 23, 2005 6:46 am #2
The units of uJ/cm^2 are radiometric, not photometric like Lux or Footcandles. The meter to use is a power meter, not a photometer. When measuring pulsed laser light, you want to use a radiometer. An optical power meter reads out directly in Watts, and typically has a silicon, germanium or thermopile detector. For holography, look for range to go from nW to mW for exposure metering, and mW to Watts for laser output power measurement.

That's exactly right - there isn't more to it ! Once you know your illumination in terms of uJ/cm^2, you are almost done.

Still it makes sense to double and halve the time to see where the optimal values are - things are not too well-defined, as the actual film sensitivity may depend on other facters like how you process it. Or depending on the type of hologram, you want to aim to OD1 instead of OD2, etc. Also the illumination across the plate, even using a spatial filter, will often vary quite a bit, and you'll have to decide on what place on the plate you want to optimize.

Such factors cannot be meaningfully measured and one needs to develop some experience for this, and calculated exposure values serve as a good starting point. On the other hand, the exposure time is not a very critical factor at all, and doubling or halving the time often won't make a relevant difference, at least compared to other factors. Eg much more important seems to have a good beam ratio.

BobH Fri Dec 23, 2005 4:56 pm #5
> So if a film requres 25uJ/cm^2 (25 watts per second)

Actually, 1 Watt = 1 Joule/second. The calculation is:

Exposure Yime = Sensitivity X Detector Area / Total Power.

Typically: s = uJ/cm^2 X cm^2 / uW

BobH Fri Dec 23, 2005 7:17 pm #7
To measure Joules, you need to specify a time. To know how much exposure you're giving a recording material, you'll also need to specify an area (let's say the area of the detector).

That's why the detector should be parallel to the recording plane when measuring the beams to calculate exposure.
