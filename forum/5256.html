Ya know, I have been thinking. What is truly needed is a way to measure the hardness of gelatin at any stage of the game. This becomes difficult with the standard manual process of measuring hardness (bloom) that the industry uses because holographic emulsions are very thin.

Right now, I believe it is a, "find a system that works to yeild a certain outcome and use it. Then modify one variable do achieve a different outcome." But if the hardness could be monitored real time in the curing stage, dark reaction stage, Harderning stage, water stage and the alcohol stages, I feel tons of great data will be found.

Any ideas on how to measure hardness, real time through all stages in gel film thickness in the 10 to 40 micron range? Real time so as to be able to to it to the hologram being made without destroying the hologram?

JohnFP Tue Dec 13, 2005 1:19 pm #4
Ok, I think I have a design that will work. While although not real time in the sense it stays on the plate, it is a tool that can measure the hardness at the testers will.

This design is a compilation of thinking about the dial micrometer, the way bloom is determined (tested) and Joe's suggestion about a durometer. I believe very doable.

Using a dial micrometer indicator make a mount that attaches to the unit and has a three point foot system (two shown here) in which the feet can be pushed into and through the gel and the points will rest on the glass. Then have the spring loaded retractable arm for the gauge come down and rest gently on the gelatin.

1. Need to find the correct spring that will allow the best definition of different hardness to be registered on the micrometer.

2. Use a dial micrometer that has the through plunger so that this can be lowered easily onto the emulsion thus only the spring applies pressure to the emulsion.

3. Used pointed feet to register the dial's relation to the glass and flat plunger to register the depression into the gel.

This can then be used to determine hardness of gel at any point in the fabrication of emulsion, hologram and processing to achieve reproducible and predictable results.

Yes Joe, I saw your post of the Durometer. But it work with a flat that goes againsts the surface and a point that penetrates... used mostly for measuring the softness of tires.

I felt in our case the instrument could not float on the surface as the Durometer does. It needs to register from the glass. Making it homemade really is not that hard. Get a dial indicator (i found this is what they are called) that measures in the .0001" (2.54 microns and I have used these and you can see movements less then this as in between the marks) file the tougue piece flat and attach three pointed legs.

I feel making a thicker emulsion to view these parameters will be off by quite a bit. A thicker emulsion, I imagine, would cure more slowly, take more light energy to harden the same, take more time in the chemical hardening etc...

If nothing else and it fail, it should be pretty good at determining emulsion thickness. Calibrate it with no emulsion, right on the glass. Then try it on an emulsion and it should accurately measure the thickness (minus depression).

--------------------------------------------------

Gel Strength.

The most important attribute of gelatin is its gel strength and when determined by the standard method (22), is called the Bloom Strength. This is the force in grams required to press a 12.5 mm diameter plunger 4 mm into 112 g of a standard 62/3% w/v gelatin gel at 10°C. Several penetrometer type instruments have been adapted to determine Bloom Strength.

[http://www.gelatin.co.za/gltn1.html](http://www.gelatin.co.za/gltn1.html)

From E-mail exchanges back and forth to Dr. Bernard Cole (Author of above).

Q: After the emulsion has set and is exposed I place it in a fixer with hardener. I assume this chemical hardening process is needed to give the uncrosslinked (by light) layer some support in the subsiquent swelling and drying steps. Does that sound correct? These layers are parallel to the plate

A: The hardener is a crosslinking agent and in photography is used to give the dry film resistance to scratching. In Litho work it reduces distortion when the film is wet and dry.

Q: If I am exposing before 16 hours (normally I expose between 4 hours and 12 hours), bloom is important as it dictates hardness.

A: If gel hardness is a criterion Bloom strength is ALWAYS important.
