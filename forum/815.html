In a split-beam color reflection hologram, which doesn't appear to exist yet , I was wondering if the different colors in the object beam need to come from the same "point." My first guess is no.

In other words, say you had various object beams, of different colors (which are all within coherence length limitations) which are then spread out before reaching the object. So instead of a "white" combined beam (using RGB lasers) spreading out from a single point, you had separate beams of R, G, and B which then spread out separately, and cover the object. The object would look "correct" in terms of color, since the color balance is right, and there is not much separation between the R, G, and B beams (say for example they are separated by 3mm). I guess that if the separation were too large, there would be different-color shadows in the object, due to the different angles of R, G, and B (?)

This relates to the idea about using a diffraction grating to separate different orders (and colors also, in a color setup) to reduce speckle.

> Joe Farina: In a split-beam color reflection hologram, which doesn't appear to exist yet

Of course, it exists. The teapot I showed earlier (reproduced here for ease of reference) is a split beam, full colour H2. That is, an H1 shot with three beams, then reconstructed with three beams to make an H2 which was reference with three beams. Therefore, six beams in all for the final white light image.

![color teapot.jpg](https://holographyforum.org/forum/download/file.php?id=2508)

I'm afraid I can't comment on the speckle aspect. I'm not actually as interested in speckle as you seem to be . Martin posted a quote from a paper by Gabor, and I commented on it, that a multiplicity of beams will reduce speckle because there is an overlap of several statistical effects, so the mean square is relatively flat. Another way of looking at it is that the convolution of the several speckle patterns from the multiplicity of the beams will, in effect, "smooth out" the overall speckle pattern by the well known characteristic of convolution.

Why don't you simply try out these different approaches?

Dinesh Mon Feb 10, 2014 11:53 am #4
Actually, Joe, you're right. I did say that it was a transmission H1. Actually Joy (wife) pointed out that the pic was an H2, a fact I never followed up on.

We started making colour H1's around 2006 and quickly ran across the cross talk problem. We solved it by angularly separating the beams, mainly by trial and error (yes, the angles could have been calculated and I suppose it's ironic that a theorist always says: "Try it first, if it doesn't pan out, then calculate"). I was unaware that Leith and Upatniks had actually suggested such a method for colour in the last paragraph of their original paper, but were unable to actually do any experimental work on this method. The next step was to make the H2, and this posed a problem. Even with the H2 ref beams all having the same divergence, ie originating from the same point, the white light reconstruction showed a separation in the z direction. There was a sort of "z cross talk" going on since reconstructing with a different wavelength creates longitudinal magnification and there were more components in white light than simply 633, 514 and 457. Three leds pretty closely superimposed gave a better result for the z distortion, but gave rise to an x, y distortion. The only solution wwas to "pre-distort" the beam divergences, ie not have them originate from the same point. We tried this technique with the teapot and lo and behold! a perfect white light reconstruction!

However, this was private research just for curiosity and we couldn't afford to keep going, so we ended the attempt. Also, it's a tedious setup. You need six sets of everything, three H1 recon beams and three H2 ref beams, it takes a long time to set it all up and do tests. Unfortunately, holography is our day job and having downtime to play is not a good thing for us. Thus, I believe that the teapot is the only split beam white light image planed colour hologram. If some kind soul gives us a sh*t load of money for "research", then we may pick up the attempt again, but unfortunately, neither Apple, not Microsoft not Google has us on their radar!

Dinesh Tue Feb 11, 2014 4:06 pm #6
> holomark: Can you elaborate a little on the pre-distortion of the beams.

An H2 reconstruction is much more sensitive to small variations in the recon beam than a Denisyuk, and a multi-coloured shot is much more sensitive than a mono-coloured shot. Even with single beam Denisyuk in pseudo-colour, you have to alter the reference slightly after swelling. I believe there was a paper by McGrew (?) that gave the change in angle for different colours for a single beam Denisyuk in pseudocolour. In split beam, full colour holography, any hologram shot with one wavelength and reconstructed in another gives a longitudinal magnification that's a ratio of the reference to the recon wavelengths. So, if you shoot a reflection H2 hologram with, say 633, 514 and 457 references all originating from the same point, then those wavelengths may not have any longitudinal magnification, but all the other wavelengths in white light will. So, what I tried was to have the ref beams not all overlap at the plate, but have some larger than others. As I said, it was a trial and error method.

> holomark: You say that you used six sets of everything - that must require a very large setup!

Well, it's not so much the size as the complexity. We have a 5x8 table, but trying to arrange all the optics without all them getting in the way of each other, while missing posts etc was a bit of a challenge. Somehow, you've got to get six beams zapping all over the table without shadowing the plate and/or occluding the beams, then making sure that no glint from a post or something was visible at the plate. Then, you've got to make sure that all the ref/object path lengths are close to zero for each beam.

> holomark: Can I presume that you collimated all three of the H1 reference beams?

Yes, common collimating mirror. We lined up all the beams first, then sent a "white" beam to the collimator.

> holomark: do yo mean the rate of divergence

Yes.

> holomark: should I measure from beam splitter to object to plate (or should I just measure to object)

You should measure from beamsplitter to plate along both paths, so, yes, you measure from BS to object to plate. For a split beam setup, the 'object' is the H1 plate. Consider that the light is like a zipper. At the beamsplitter, you "unzip" the light, and send the two halves of the zipper along two different paths. The lights get "re-zipped" at the plate. Consider now that if one half of the zipper went a distance longer than the zipper itself, then the light will never "re-zip". If one half of the zipper came back to co-incidence along some section of the other half, it would partially "re-zip".
