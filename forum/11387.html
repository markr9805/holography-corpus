It's because the beam waist, W(0), for each laser is different.

The beam divergence of a Gaussian (laser) beam is not collimated at the output. It initially converges, reaches a point of minimum convergence, then diverges. The beam radius at minimum convergence is known as the beam waist, W(0). Assuming the beam is propagating along the z axis the divergence of the laser beam downstream, W(z), is dependent on the beam waist, W(0), in the following manner:

W(z) = W(0)√[1 + (z/z(0))²]

where z(0) is the point of minimum convergence. In other words, if the waist is large, then the beam divergence is large for any given point z along the beam. The beam waist is usually quoted in the laser spec. To be completely accurate, passing a beam through a lens creates a new beam waist W'(0), but this parameter is also dependent on the laser's "built-in" beam waist.

If you're concerned, you might look into placing an iris in the beam and widening it enough to block the "overspill" red light. Thor labs ( [https://www.thorlabs.com/newgrouppage9. ... oup_id=206](https://www.thorlabs.com/newgrouppage9.cfm?objectgroup_id=206) ) has iris's, but it may be expensive; it's the "go-to" company for a lot of technical holographers. There are probably cheaper alternatives. It may be possible to make your own iris if you don't need a variable open diameter.

As Din explained, the difference in expanded beam diameters is in the nature of things, and that's what you want to see. The input beams to the spatial filter should be smallest for blue, larger for green, and still larger for red. Likewise, the expanded beams coming out of the spatial filter (or just the objective) will smallest to largest in that same order. Just use the uniform middle part of the expanded beam for the hologram. Inevitably, there will be wasted light for green and red.
BobH Fri Nov 08, 2024 9:48 am #4
Beam diameter AND divergence need to be the same for all colors to get the best result when going through a spatial filter. It was easier when all the beams came from gas lasers which had similar divergences. DPSS laser beams have generally much smaller diameter and much larger divergence than gas lasers. I'd recommend an up-collimating telescope for the DPSS lasers to match the gas laser's beam. That's the only way you'll get the collinear beams to go through a pinhole at the same time, and the uniformity of color you want at the recording plane.

Din Sat Nov 09, 2024 10:37 am #10
Briefly, when a coherent beam (or any beam, really, but the rings are most prevalent the narrower the bandwidth) passes through a biconvex lens, it does not focus to a point. It forms a series of circles, known as Airy rings because the focus of a thin, biconvex lens is the Fourier transform of the input plane of the beam to the lens, along with all the non-uniformities of the beam such as dust etc. The circles in the Airy ring are caused by the non-uniformities - they're known as the higher frequencies encoded in the beam. If you could block all the Airy rings, the output of the lens would be due to a completely uniform input plane to the lens - this is spatial filtering, you're 'filtering' the light in space by, effectively, putting an iris in the beam at some point in space to get rid of some spatial part of the beam. This is why when you mis-align a pinhole, you get a series of rings, you're allowing Airy rings through.

At any rate, the radius of the first Airy ring is given by (this is where Colin's formula comes from, but he uses 1.27 as the coefficiant which is incorrect - the number comes from the first zero of the sinc function)):

r = 1.22fλ/D = 1.22F#λ

Thus, in order to perfectly pinhole a focused beam, the pinhole has to have a radius equal to the first Airy ring, as per above equation.

But, a few points:

1. commercially available pinholes will probably not come in exactly the calculated first Airy radius, so, you have to approximate.

2. there's no such thing as a beam consisting of just one wavelength, which is impossible for various reasons. Therefore, even the best laser has a bandwidth, and so no pinhole, calculated for just one wavelength, will completely block off the first Airy ring. However, the narrower the bandwidth, the more prominent the central wavelength. This is not too bad for display hologram - as Kaveh used to say: "The eye forgives a lot". It is important in technical work, where the hologram is "read" by a machine.

3. The Airy radius is based on the fact that the input beam is completely collimated, impossible in a Gaussian beam. As a matter of interest, Airy was an astronomer, and so was looking at light from distant stars, whose light is pretty well collimated.

4. The actual focal length is also based on a laser parameter known as M², usually given in most laser specs. If M² = 1, you have a perfect Gaussian (laser) beam, but in practice it's usually higher. The closer to M² is to 1, the better.

Having said this, it should be obvious that several wavelengths in an input beam cannot all pass through the same pinhole and have all the Airy rings filtered out - a point that Colin makes. The divergence of a beam, having passed through a lens is based on the NA (numerical aperture) of the lens, which is usually printed on the side of the objective, but only if the lens aperture is filled. If the aperture is not filled, the beam divergence is given by the beam diameter at the lens, and it's focal length (see below). I assume this is the basis of Colin's calculation of increasing the beam diameter. In the case the beam fills the diameter, the divergence θ = arcsin(NA), in the case, it does not fill the aperture, θ = arctan(D'/(2f), D' < D. So, by manipulating the input beam diameter, you may be able to have roughly equal beam divergencesrs for all three beams (another point Colin makes), but you cannot filter out all the higher frequencies in all the beams. However, remember again, the eye forgives a lot!

Airy rings: [https://www.edmundoptics.com/knowledge- ... e0lgqW_Qh0](https://www.edmundoptics.com/knowledge-center/application-notes/imaging/limitations-on-resolution-and-contrast-the-airy-disk/?srsltid=AfmBOorqTgqqPcjE0GVoSBppxVC2hwlRQFGrIcCTesqYSRe0lgqW_Qh0)

None of that math is necessary in the real world, unless you're being paid to calculate something that someone else will be expected to build. Laser beams are generally not collimated. There's no literature I've found on this "divergence problem" of non-overlapping beams. However, the problem is quite complicated. It involves the divergence/convergence of a Gaussian beam /before/after a lens, the beam waist of the laser - and so, different lasers will diverge by different amounts based on their specific w(0) - and, last, but certainly not least, the colour sensitivity of the eye, which is not at all objective. Colour is a very complicated subject and not so simple as to say that three wavelengths will give you "colour". While there is quite a lot on colour sensitivity of the eye and laser wavelengths, there is nothing I've found on the "divergence problem". In most cases, Kaveh's suggestion to me was to alter beam intensities so that the object looked the same under laser illumination as under white light; I'd illuminate the object with three wavelengths, then rapidly turn the room (white) lights on and off quickly. This resulted in a blinking of colours if the colours were mis-matched. In terms of over-spill on the plate, I made sure that the collimator 'saw' white light, so any overspill went beyond the collimator, or the plate if you're not using a collimator.

The divergence of a Gaussian (laser) beam after a lens is based on the Gaussian function, and so is not planar. However, the error between assuming planar wavefronts and analysing it using the actual Gaussian function is small (probably < 5%) for a small aperture of a lens and so, for display holography, not as important. As I mentioned, the cone receptor variation will probably swamp the 5% odd error. If you want the true Gaussian analysis, you'll find it here: [https://www.edmundoptics.com/knowledge- ... Os-d3K8Yuo](https://www.edmundoptics.com/knowledge-center/application-notes/lasers/gaussian-beam-propagation/?srsltid=AfmBOopJjOymjAy9KiSQXWCBGvOcySmtee3mFSHHFe6vcIOs-d3K8Yuo) (about a third of the way down the page - 'Gaussian Beam Manipulation', the article also analyses the focusing of a Gaussian to a spot).

Most of the papers/articles on colour holography involved the gamut, based on the CIE chart, which gives the range of colours that can be captured based on the laser wavelengths used. For some reason, people working in colour holography used the CIE 1932, which is outdated. Colour vision today (for the Western world - yes it is culturally-based!) is better modeled on the CIE 1976. In the 80's there was a quite intense "gamut wars", largely between Kaveh and Paul Hubel ( a prominent worker from Oxford University). I believe Paul Hubel wanted a larger gamut for a greater colour range, while Kaveh wanted a smaller gamut because the greater - Paul Hubel's - gamut included a lot of colours not seen in nature. I don't think the gamut wars were ever resolved because they both left holography by the 90's. Today, no one in the colour holography world seems overly concerned with gamuts

At any rate, Dr. Sunny Bains produced a magazine in the 80's called "International Holography", in which both Kaveh and Paul Hubel wrote articles on colour holography. Below is Paul Hubel's article on colour holography, with the gamut he used from the 1976 CIE.

Well, yes. There'll always be "white" at the centre of the overlapping beams. As I mentioned, I made sure the "white" area was limited to my (16") collimator, so the object saw "pure white" light, with any overspill going off the edges of the collimator. However, there will also be overspill, as I believe Alan noticed. In your case, there seems to be a turquoise overspill. The centre itself, the area of pure "white", will of course depend on how much each wavelength in the beam expanded. If the beams are not sufficiently expanded, the "white" area decreases, as a function of object area.

It would be interesting to measure the diameter of each component individually, without the other components being present. You can also plot the Gaussian of each beam individually, eg by passing a fibre attached to a plotter across the beam and so having a measuring aperture - a sample size - of about a 100 microns or so. I think you'll find that the three individual Gaussians will not overlap except at the 'centre' (which you can take at the FWHM) of the least Gaussian spread.

Din Sat Nov 23, 2024 1:15 pm #24
The problem is really with this concept of "white" (which is why I keep putting it in quotes). If you look at the "white" of the 1932 CIE, it's different from the "white" in the 1976 CIE. This is because the CIE was designed for manufacturers of commercial materials; it was designed initially so that printers from different countries printing the same product - a magazine, for example - would see the same colours, so that "white" was designed for eye receptivity from incoherent light. But, "white" is also culturally dependent. I had a friend who worked in Sony Pictures. her job was to take the artist impression of DVD covers and send it to all the countries who locally made and distributed the DVD's. When I mentioned the cultural eye sensitivity to colour, she told me that the DVD for "I Dream of Jeannie" had intense blues for the American eye. However, when the DVD from Finland was sent to Sony for approval, the intense blue was much diluted. The Finns swore blind that the colour matched, they couldn't perceive the intense blue. The reason, I explained, was that American and European advertising used blue leds, which produces an intense, over-saturated blue, and their advertisers didn't. Our colour perception has shifted to more saturated colours.

Another problem is that an object's "white" is the result of dyes imbued within the material, but those dyes have a colour profile that may be different from the colour profile in your lasers, so what looks "white" from the lasers would not look "white" to the object. You have to tune in the white so that the object looks white under laser illumination (below).

And still another problem is that the reconstruction light has to have the same profile as the laser's "white". So, on reconstruction, the "white" hologram will not appear "white". I remember a few years back Stanislovas Zacharovas (Stas) displayed a set of colour holograms on a polymer he was pushing. When I mentioned this to Rob Mundy (I think it was Rob), Rob's first comment was, "Were there any white holograms?" No, there weren't.

Your best bet is to follow Kaveh's advice: illuminate the object with the three lasers so the (white) object looks white, despite the laser illumination not looking white on a wall. This is what I did with the teapot.

BobH Sun Nov 24, 2024 8:34 am #26
The eye sees color changes with high sensitivity. If the Gaussian rolloff of the three beams is not the same, colors will shift as one moves around the viewing zone. Might be OK for very small objects but for larger holograms with more complexity than a simple test object chosen to look good, beam shape matching is important to get good color uniformity.
