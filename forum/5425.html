wler Fri Mar 10, 2006 10:15 pm #6
If you tilt your plate by 56 degrees so that the ref beam comes from the side, the actual power density in uW/cm^2 on the plate is approx 1/2 of what you measure with a detector when facing the ref beam. So if you aim at a certain value of exposure, you need to take this factor into account (the exposure curves on my site include it).

The object light comes in straight, so that will be measured correctly. On the other hand, the variations across the plate will be large anyway.

So all-in-all, the procedure I follow is:

1) measure ref beam intensity R with detector perpendicular to the beam

2) measure object beam intensity O in the brightest region, with detector facing the object

3) tune to a good beam ratio, O=1/4 R, say

then total exposure is O+1/2 R = 3/4 R

I still disagree.

When building a set-up, we split the the light and recombine it at the recording plane. Before a piece of film is placed in the plateholder, the interference fringes exist in space where the two beams overlap. If we want to maximize the fringe visibility there, we want to set the beam ratio to one. This is done by measuring the power in each beam and making them equal.

The visibility of the fringes has nothing to do with the orientation of a recording plane intersecting them. That's why I believe that measuring the beams (for the purpose of setting the beam ratio) is best done with the detector perpendicular to the beam. We want (for maximum fringe visibility) the amplitudes of the two beams to be equal.

Now, if we place a piece of film in the plateholder, each beam hits the plate at some angle. If we want to expose the plate properly, we need to know the total energy density hitting the plate. I suppose that making this measurement is best done with the detector parallel to the recording plane. Real detectors (that I'm aware of), however, are mounted within their housings, which usually vignettes the beam being measured. This is especially true when making edge-illuminated holograms.

Since flush mounted detectors are not available, and measuring for proper exposure is usually simply a starting point for an "exposure bracket", I recommend always measuring with the detector pointed directly into the beam being measured. This gives accurate control of fringe visibility via beam ratio, which is especially important in an image-planed transfer set-up where the localized beam ratios vary widely.

Ed Wesly Sun Mar 12, 2006 12:46 am #14
The correction factor for the difference between having the light hit the detector along the normal versus the actual angle of incidence is the cosine of the angle of incidence. That's the trig function which starts with cos 0 degrees = 1, and then gets smaller until you hit 90 degrees. Multiply the power reading at the normal by the cosine of the angle of the plate and you will get a better reading.

Sometimes you hear that the real reading is cos^2 but I haven't seen that documented anywhere.

Yeah, I thought of the recess problem after I had posted previously. There was a Coherent 212 I worked with a Newport 820, and they both had this problem.

I feel that there is no doubt that to get the proper exposure reading the power meter detector should be parallel to the plate because of the loss of power due to reflection and angle of incidence. Ed Wesly Mon Mar 13, 2006 4:01 pm #33
It sounds like what you would like to know is how to find the change in area between a circle and when it is tranformed into an ellipse by tilting it so that its major axis is increased.

When the circular region in 3 space is rotated about one of its diameters, turning it into the new minor axis, the orthogonal radius becomes the major axis, and will be increased by the inverse of either the sine or cosine of the angle, depending on how you measure it.

If the circle is just sitting there minding its own business basking in the light waves arriving along its normal, then we say it's moved 0 degrees, so we use cosine, (since cos 0 degrees = 1, otherwise we run into a problem with dividing by sin 0 = 0) because we will be dividing the other diameter's measurement by the cosine of the angle that we have rotated it.

Tilting 30 degrees from the normal, the major axis grows by 1/cos 30 degrees or 1/.8660 = 1.155. Then this could be plugged into the area of an ellipse equation. Tilting more to 45 degrees gives a lengthening of the diameter by 1/.7071 or 1.414 times.

Notice the areas of the new ellipses compared to the original circle are off by a factor of, you guessed, the cosine of the angle of displacement from the normal! This is why the light meter's reading is attenuated, the area of radiant flux at right angles to the beam is now spread out over a larger area of the transformed ellipse! So there are less photons per unit area! Now does everyone see the wisdom of the detector parallel to the recording material?
