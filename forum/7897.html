I've been searching a box of various optics i've had laying around for some time. How do I get to know what direction the polarisation is in without having any starting point?

Smart idea to ask, since a couple hundred milliWatts will melt a polarizing filter! I know that only 100 mW of red or green does that! But it's pretty cool to see the bea wiggle as the filter bubbles up!

I would guess it's vertically polarized, as it's a rare laser that is horizontally polarized. But it could be!

Another trick to see which plane the beam is polarized non-destructively is to use a piece of glass. If you rotate the glass so that the beam is incident at 0 degrees you will get a reflection, and as you increase the angle of incidence the reflection will get stronger if you the beam is polarized at right angles to the pane of glass, but if it is parallel to the plane you will see the reflection diminish as you get to Brewster's angle and then it will get stronger.

I forget which one is the s and p vector in that language, and I just taught two back to back and I am on my last legs for the day. My own internal logic tells me s is the one that Sticks out of the surface while the other is Parallel, but without lifting a finger to get a book I am confused.

If you have a Newport Catalog or Melles Griot Optics Guide, they have good drawings of which way the polarization exits from the PBS cubes.

BobH Fri Jul 10, 2009 1:19 am #5
Doesn't matter what the output polarization of the laser is if you go into a 1/2 wave plate with it. If your laser beam is parallel to the table, and polarizing cube is oriented so that the reflected beam is also parallel to the table, then the transmitted beam is p=polarized ("P" for Parallel to the Plane of incidence, and in this case Parallel to the table) and the reflected beam is s=polarized ("S" for Sticking up off the table, like the face inside the cube is Sticking up off the table).

Another way I keep track of it is by visualizing the polarization vector being a knife: "P" pushes through, "S" skips off.

A tip: I always like to do my setups with the reference beam being the beam transmitted through the beamsplitter. That makes it easiest to adjust the centering of the reference spot throughout the process, and the object beam(s) afterward. I also like to have a small iris diaphram mounted just before the beamsplitter. It and the center of the plateholder gives my constant reference beam path regardless of how the table mushes around, or how the laser beam drifts as it changes temperature.
