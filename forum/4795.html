JohnFP Fri Apr 29, 2005 2:55 pm #4
The lens will be the window in which the object will be seen in the finish hologram. If the lens is such that it needs to be far away to focus the image where you want then your viewing window will be small. If the lens is such that is needs to be very close to focus the image where you want then there will be great distortions as you move side to side. If the lens is small in diameter then you will have a small viewing window.

So I belive the largest lens possible with about an f1 or f2 would be the best.

These are just my thoughts and I have not tried this technique yet.

BobH Fri Apr 29, 2005 6:25 pm #7
The geometry shown will make a Transmission hologram of the lens and the image formed by it (presumably near or at the recording plane), but if the reference beam is divergent as shown, the reconstruction will be terribly distorted. Even if you bring the reference beam in from the other side to make a Reflection hologram, you'll need to make it convergent to make the final image look real. A collimated reference beam might do, but the image will still have lots of "swing".

The reason nobody does this (and makes Transmission masters instead) is because the optimum lens is very expensive to buy, and making it (in the form of the master) is much easier.

JohnFP Fri Apr 29, 2005 7:56 pm #9
Why not take one of your Denisyuk hologram and make an image planed hologam from it? It too will also be a single beam hologram.

Proceedures:

Make a Denisyuk hologram as usual and process to get it to replay as close to your laser wavelength as possible. Then take the hologram and lay it down on some flat black surface face down (with the vertual image down). You will have to arrange it so that the light is now coming in from the bottom and you should see the real pseudoscopic image floating above the plate. Now place three spacers on the plate. Two at two corners and one it the middle at the opposite edge. The spacer thickness will be approximately the amount the object will be image planed to be in front of the plate. Shutter the laser. Take an unexposed plate and lay it on the three spacers. Let settle and expose. Develop. You just made an image planed hologram.

For DCG, and this will apply to Silver. When You make the first Denisyuk make is such that the emulsion is toward the object. Process. Then when you lay the plate down on the black surface the emulsion is facing up and the real, pseudoscopic image is up also (bottom reference now). Place the second plate emulsion down on the spacers. This way there is no glass between the hologram emulsion and the unexposed emulsion.

Have fun!!!
Kaveh Sat Apr 30, 2005 3:51 am #10
This is a good experiment to try, and can give very good results. I think you should try the transmission first, as you will get a very nice black and white image, viewable in white light.

I think that using a fresnel lens will give a lot of distortions, and a blurred or fuzzy image, althought you will get a wide field of view. The proof of the pudding is to look at the image before making the hologram and judge.

Ideally, you are looking for a low f/no lens, e.g. f/2 to f/3, to get the best viewing angle. Also, best to get what I think is called a "copy lens", designed to image at a 1:1 ratio. Then you get minimum distortion. I think these were used in photocopiers, and possibly hard to find, but keep an eye on eBay and you should come across one cheaply. I have one somewhere, which is around f/2.8 and I got nice results from it. I looked on ebay and I think these are worth trying:

[http://cgi.ebay.com/ws/eBayISAPI.dll?Vi ... 25158&rd=1](http://cgi.ebay.com/ws/eBayISAPI.dll?ViewItem&category=74930&item=7511725158&rd=1)

Might be a risk, but it's fun to have these huge lenses around anyway!

Here is one advantage over H1-H2 that most people don't realise. There is no background noise when doing a 1-step image plane hologram, so the background looks pitch black. It is surprising how much this increases the realism in the image. In H1-H2 transfers, there is always a background "fuzz" which detracts from the realism of the image.

Now, using a lens has two effects:

1. you will get longitudinal magnification. suppose the center of the image (which you focus onto the film) is imaged at 1:1. Then you will find that object points closer to the lens are imaged larger, and are closer to you than they should be. And the opposite for points further from the lens. So the perspective is distorted.

2. The image is vignetted. Suppose you use the same recording and reconstruction beam. The imaging lens will be faithfully reproduced behind the hologram. This means you get a port-holing effect, with an annoying aperture behind, and probably you will not see the whole image from any one point.

There is an elegant solution to both of these problems [ref 1]. Use a short reference and a long reconstruction beam. What happens is that the image distortion is corrected, or minimizsed, and the image of the lens pops into the viewer space, where it belongs. When your eyes are in the projected porthole, you will see the image in full, and without "vignetting".

I have some details and diagrams in my thesis if you are interested:

[http://www.focalimage.com/public/kaveh-PhD.pdf](http://www.focalimage.com/public/kaveh-PhD.pdf) Chapter 5

Good luck.

Ref 1. Kakichashvili and Kakichashvili, "Reconstruction of Focused Holograms with Normal Perspective" Sov. Phys. - Tech. Phys. Vol 16, pp 1184-1185 (1972)
