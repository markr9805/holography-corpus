Joe Farina Sat Jul 28, 2007 12:31 pm #5
I did some quick testing of the electrical resistance of a DCG layer, and it turns out it's very high, and maybe this is partially due to the thinness of the layer (which is around 10 microns). I took an old MBDCG hologram (Jeff's formula) which had been exposed to ambient conditions (stored in an unheated shed) and tried to measure the resistance. This layer was way too damp for sealing, as the shed is quite humid. Anyway, the resistance was off the scale (I'm not sure how many megaohms my digital meter can register though). Then I wet the DCG layer under the faucet, wiped it off, and tried again. This time the resistance was around 20 megaohms, with the probes as close together as I could get (without having them touch each other).

I tried some wood as well (a piece of oak that I had in the basement, which is damp). This did not register either. Then I wet the wood, and was able to get resistances in the 10 to 20 megaohm range, even if the probe tips were held very far apart, which surprised me.

What this tells me is that a commerical wood moisture meter must be much more sensitive than a simple ohm-meter. So there must be different electronics to measure very high resistances (because the piece of oak should have easily registered, before wetting, on a commercial moisture meter).

So maybe the idea has promise, if an ohm-meter can be built that registers ultra-high resistances.

The way thin materials have their resistances measured is in “ohms per square”, because it is independent of the size of the square . But for that to be so the opposite sides of a square have to be relatively perfect conductors. I used to do this with the (generally available) “silver conductive paint” painted along 2 opposite edges .

(However I would not buy “epoxy” based silver conductive paints which would probably be quite unsuitable as temporary electrical contacts).

Although the paint is supposed to be baked after application to bring its resistance down to negligibly low level, when you are dealing with the high resistance levels you have found then it is enough to just let the paint dry without baking and then after you have made your ohmeter measurement it can be completely wiped off the gelatin film with an acetone soaked tissue. So to get an “ohms per square” reading on say a 5” x 4” hologram , you need to put one strip of paint along the 4 inch side and then a second parallel stripe 4 inches away, being acetone based it will not scar a DCG

hologram. You should get exactly the same resistance reading as the same gelatin hologram in a 2” x 2” or 8 x 8” format.

Tom B. Sat Jul 28, 2007 9:15 pm #10
Since both gelatin and pure water are essentially non-conductive, resistance measurements will be strongly dependant on the degree of ionic contamination (by salts etc.) of the water/gelatin mixture. I expect it will be difficult to translate resistance measurements into moisture content without some other independent means of measuring moisture and gelatin thickness to calibrate the measurements for a given batch of emulsion.

It should be possible to measure moisture content by weighing the plate on a sensitive scale before and after baking in a dry environment. A 100 mm^2 plate with 10 micron emulsion, would, if the emulsion was 100% water (obviously impossible), show a change of (100 mm^2 * .01 mm) = 100 mm^3 or 0.1 cc (0.1 gram) after baking (if my math is right), so the change should be large enough to be measurable with a scale that can resolve milligrams.

> Hans: But I still don't understand why the residual water is so important with DCG. If the plate shows a hologram after drying and is then sealed, how will the state of the hologram alter after sealing?

According to Rallison:

"Baking at about 150 C will cause the gelatin to densify and if tilted fringes are present they will appear to lay down, baking also makes the gel much more stable and a little less hygroscopic. While it is still hot a glass cap or at least an epoxy coating can be applied without trapping too much moisture in the film. Trapped moisture can become active upon heating and cause the gel to collapse here and there and everywhere. Mysterious color shifts in capped reflection holograms can be explained by the action of trapped moisture and lack of 150 bake down."

I was searching for another comment by Rallison, and although I couldn't find it, it was something to the effect that "all" of the DCG holograms he ever did that did notreceive the final baking failed eventually.

So this is why I'm harping on the subject. I guess you could say my "dream" for a long time has been to find a DCG tuning method that can be done in the dry state (no noisy or damaging reprocessing) that does not depend on how much the layer has been dehydrated. There needs to be a certain level of dehydration for a DCG hologram, period. But if you are relying on dehydration (and here I mean by heating, which is the method used in the past) to color-tune, then you are manipulating a parameter which really shouldn't be manipulated. So basically what I mean is that, in this case (controlled shrinkage by baking), color-tuning and dehydation do not agree with each other, yet they are linked inseparably.

This is why Jeff's method is exciting to me. Dehydation is done solely for the purpose of dehydration, nothing else. Once that has been done adequately, then a completely different process can be used to tune the color (heating to cause controlled shrinkage). So there will be no compromise regarding dehydration.

Of couse, we don't know if this process will work out. Hopefully it will. One question on my mind is: why did Jeff's first hologram shrink upon baking, while the second hologram did not shrink upon dehydration? Assuming a process can be worked out, then it occurs to me that the initial dehydation should be measurable, at least at first, so that a protocol can be established.

Rallison and others indicate that a final bakeout is necessay, but dehydation without heat is slower and is not an established (documented) procedure in this application. So what I'm trying to do is measure the "equivalent dryness" for a dehydrated hologram, as compared to one that has received a standard bakeout. Jeff, will you please consider the following question: How does the dryness of a baked DCG hologram compare to one that has been dehydrated for a week?

one way to measure the humidity content of the coating might be to expose a small area (say 1cm diameter) of the plate to a precise amount of microwave radiation. The change in temperature of the gel in the irradiated area would be an indication of the amount of moisture in the gel. You would need a sensitive infrared thermometer to do this.

Another possibility would be to have a microwave detector placed directly opposite the emitter. Interposing a gel coated plate between the two and measuring the resulting attenuation might provide the moisture content of the plate.

This technique need not be limited to microwaves- it could be done using Infra Red wavelengths which are strongly absorbed by water.

Yes, in the reading research I have done, it has been stated that if you dry the gelatin to a 2% water content, the gelatin becomes insoluble to water again, just as the collagen was in which the gelatin was made. This is due to the extensive crosslinking achieved by dehydration.

So , in using a scale:

Mix up

1 gram of gelating

30 ml of water

.3 grams of sensitizer

Coat a large plate with entire amount

expose and process

Starting weight (we can elliminate the sensitizer as it is removed during processing) = 31 grams

Dry this to... (2% of 30 = .6 grams water) + (1 gram gelatin) = 1.6 grams.

At 1.6 grams the gelatin should be insoluble to water.
