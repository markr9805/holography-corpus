Ed Wesly Thu Apr 10, 2008 6:06 pm #2
John:

Your technique #1 will work, no problem. #2, nope.

Check out the math in the Holo-Wiki, and you will see that since your distances 1 and 2 are equal, the quantity in parentheses in both the horizontal and vertical foci will drop out as their difference will be zero, and since that is multiplying the ratio of recording and reconstructing wavelengths, that will drop out also, leaving 1/output distance = 1/ illuminating distance. So no matter where the illuminating source is placed behind the holo, the reconstructed image source will be the same distance away. This is a curious kind of grating, where the two interfering beams are angularly symmetric to the plate and the same distance away. I think that this is a Fourier transform?

What you want is 1/ output distance to be = infinity. You could juggle the two interfering distances and the replay distance to get that result when plugging into the horizontal focus distance equation, but then plugging those same numbers in to the vertical focus equation, with its evil cosine^2 numerators will give a different focusing position, making the system astigmatic. In other words, the output beam will be parallel to itself in one dimension, the other dimension will be diverging or converging depending on how the beams were configured.

The trick would be to have one of the interfering beams to be astigmatic originally in the recording mode. I thought of using a cylinder lens after the spatial filter to do that, but it took a real genius like Rich Rallison on how to do it perfectly. He used a ray-tracing program that had a library of the commercially available lenses from Melles Griot, Newport, etc., and found lenses that when the beams went through them at odd angles would yield the proper degree of astigmatism!

So the timeless quandary of making cheap HOE collimators has always been hampered by finding that perfect lens or mirror to generate the first collimated beam!

Ed Wesly Tue Apr 15, 2008 7:34 pm #9
The biggest problem is dispersion, since diffraction bends the different colors into different paths more vigorously than refraction.

Since the beams are usually off axis, other nasties like astigmatism and distortion rear their ugly heads.

The HOE will accurately reconstruct the wavefronts that were used to construct it when those conditions are duplicated, like the illuminating or replay source is at the same distance and incident angle as designed; but once the illuminating beam is moved, angularly or distance-wise, all hell breaks loose.

Lenses are more versatile, but the optimum in design, to be diffraction-limited, takes place usually at one and only one conjugate (object-image) position.

The trick in designing at lest on the math part, of what John wants to do, is calculable. Maybe if I'm not doing anything over the weekend I can give it a shot. (Yeah, right! It's the final week of classes and I'm in a grading frenzy. But then again, this might make a better diversion than looking at helpless papers...) But what will turn out is that one of the beams in the recording will have to be astigmatic; horizontal and vertical divergences will be different.

A mock up of what I am trying to describe is to look back at your spatial filter (at an appropriate power level) and the bright dot of the pinole will look a certain distance away. Then put some sort of cylindrical optic in from of it, like those wide cylinders Edmund used to sell, or eyeglass lenses that correct for astigmatism. Now looking at the pinhole, you may see something that looks like a stripe or a plus sign of light. If distances are long, you could use an SLR camera to focus on the spot, and you would find a nice vertical stripe at one distance, a horizontal at another. The math trick is in calculating these distances; the practical trick is finding combinations of lenses that would provide the requisite foci.
