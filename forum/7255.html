BobH Tue Apr 01, 2008 2:51 pm #2
I think it may be a matter of spectral bandwidth. DCG tends to be wider bandwidth than other materials, and I think that's why it captures metallic luster. The best results I've seen on AgX was done by Vic Rice, an '80 era holographer from silicon valley. He formulated his own processing scheme for Agfa materials and shot on-axis (or very nearly so) holograms of little metal cars and guns. Many were sold on little 2"x2" stash boxes by Holos Gallery in San Francisco. His images came very close to capturing bronze and copper colors. He now lives in New Orleans I think, and is unfortunately now battling a very serious illness. Bay area holographers will certainly remember him, I hope all who read this will send a few good thoughts his way.

Colin Kaminski Tue Apr 01, 2008 10:47 pm #12
> Dutchelm05: JohnFP wrote:cross modulationCan someone define this?

Cross modulation is when a specular reflection from the object creates a new reference beam and that causes a new set of fringes competing with the normal set of fringes. It can cause all sorts of problems like noise and dim holograms. This is one of the reasons that beam ratios have to stay below 1 to 1.
BobH Wed Apr 02, 2008 12:30 am #13
> beam ratios have to stay below 1 to 1

Beam ratios are usually stated as reference : object, so I think you may have meant to say "above 1 to 1".

And at the risk of veering too far off topic, I'd go on to say that when making HOEs (especially when all beams are from point sources), I state beam ratios as farthest off-normal : closest to normal. I've found that if one of the beams carries information, it's probably the beam closest to the normal of the plate, so I consider that to always be the "object" beam.

DCG holography will color shift due to film thickness, hardness and processing , its like a little switch that suddenly changes color, you may have seen this if you have ever reprocessed a dcg hologram (as it always shifts towards the bluer end), all of your answers seem to be correct ,as far producing an even colored hologram. if you look carefully at your hologram you’ll see a greening/shadowing effect from the background around the key, and also some of the coins in the mid area, this is telling me that area is receiving a lot of energy , now if you look in the lower right corner you will see a less bright, not so clear section, this area needs more energy to give the nice clear background, so based on your answers ,if you expand your beam a bit and (probably)make a longer exposure you should reduce your chances of "greeneies" ,this greening effect is really what you want when making a DCG master as you want all the area to become green/blue. hence to harden/crosslink and replay at the same wavelength it was recorded in. after you make an adjustment to the lighting, and the greenies don’t go away, i would suspect your film to be a little to thin (the hardness is more difficult to control when using rapid fix and very thin emulsions), possibly try to make it slightly thicker (only a few microns more should do the trick)!

Nice work, John. The only issue I see here is we're not doing Lippmann photographs but holograms. In other words, the holograms having been recorded with very narrow bandwidth light at 458nm, show an extremely limited spot within the 400 – 700nm range of visible light. So what the laser “sees”/records is only related to this particular wavelength.

Assuming that all three metals, Ag, Au and Cu do reflect fair amounts of light at 458nm, each will form nice DCG holograms – though at various DE, corresponding the degree to which they reflect light of that wavelength.

Upon reconstruction this narrow bandwidth thing does become broadband again - particularly when its' a DCG like this.
