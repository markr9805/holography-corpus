MichaelH Sat Sep 15, 2007 1:18 pm #2
If by "spots of ugly" you mean burned out areas, the best way to control these is careful subject choice.

In essence, choose subjects that aren't as contrasty. Bright specular reflections combined with dark or dim areas will create hot spots that don't copy well. They look great in transmission though.

You can also move the hot spots out of the plane of the copy film and that will lower the effect somewhat but depending on the subject you may never be able to get rid of the burnout.

Having said all that, some bleaches make this effect worse. What are you using to develop and bleach the reflection hologram?
BobH Sat Sep 15, 2007 1:26 pm #3
Those ugly burn spots can be avoided by:

a) composition, and pre-visualization of the location of the recording plane of the transfer (H2) within it,

b) lighting of the scene when making the master (H1) so as to avoid the highlights near the intended transfer plane,

c) possibly manipulating the polarization of the scene lighting to reduce the highlights,

d) setting the beam ratio by measuring the "object" light in the transfer recording plane at the location of the burn spots (which are easy to find by placing a card in the plate holder) and with a detector about the same size as an average burn spot,

e) using a beam ratio and exposure time that gives optimum performance at the location of the burns, and

f) using a processing regime that doesn't shrink or swell the emulsion as a function of beam ratio or exposure intensity.

This is the biggest challenge of "image-plane" holography and I wish you the best of luck.

MichaelH Mon Sep 17, 2007 10:44 am #6
> Justin W: As far as moving my H2 film out of the plane of the object...

You mis-read my post. You just need to get the point where the hot spots are focused out of the plane of the H2 film for their affect to be lessened. You don't need to get the entire H1 image out of the plane of the H2 film. If the bright spot is out of focus that may be enough. Trial and error will tell you.

> Justin W: (this I seem to have achieved in my first attempt, with the results being the front portion of my object [the shaft of a model engine] disproportionally large and kinda blurry)...

Sounds like you're using a diverging beam to reconstruct the H1. This will make the effect you're seeing worse. Moving to a collimated ref when making the H1 or a converging reconstruction beam on the H1 when making the H2 will reduce this effect.

> Justin W: But on the subject of planning shots out a bit more... and please tell me if I'm wrong here ...

You're on the right track. Bringing the H1 closer and/or moving to larger masters will afford wider replay angles on the H2.

> Justin W: BTW: for those who were wondering - I develop/bleach my VRP-M with the JD-2 kit from Integraf.

The potassium dichromate in that bleach will increase the effect of burnout as the film shrinks in the bleach. EDTA bleach won't shrink the film but in my experiments adds to the fog level in the film. I'm still working out a reliable process for myself so don't have any alternate bleaches to suggest with VRP-M.

Colin Kaminski Sat Sep 22, 2007 1:55 pm #12
The reference beam to illuminate an H1 needs to be the complex conjugate of the reference beam used to create it. If you use a collimated beam then the beams are the same. If you use a diverging beam then the replay beam needs to be converging. If you can not get a collimation mirror then the best compromise is to have the weakest diverging beam you can make. I saw a lab that had the diverging beam leaving a lens about 15' from the plate. The H2's showed a little "swing" but were very nice.
MichaelH Sat Sep 22, 2007 5:38 pm #13
> Justin W: Cool, Dave, are you saying I can reconstruct my diverging-ref-beam-exposed H1's with a collimated beam when copying and get better focal characteristics from my image?

Yes, they may or may not be noticeable but it's mathematically provable.

> Justin W: Or do all my H1's absolutely need to be exposed with a collimated reference beam?

In general the rule is to use a conjugate beam which is simplified by using a collimated beam when making the H1 and reconstructing it to make the H2. In practice you can often get away with ignoring that rule. It really depends on what your goals are. In general, only another holographer would see the swing or change in size you'll end up with as a defect. Most other people wouldn't think twice about it unless it's a subject they're very familiar with.

> Justin W: Would my H2 have been even better if I had dialed the focus of my H1 reconstruction beam into a big-lens-sized tube? - which is, coincidently-enough roughly the size of my 2.5X2.5 plate, minus the very corners...

> Justin W: Should I copy my H1's with a big collimated beam?

If you have the optics, yes. Just remember, "rules" were made to be broken so use what gets you what you're after. Don't forget to every once in a while try something people say you shouldn't You never know what you might discover.
john kaufman Mon Sep 24, 2007 6:04 pm #14
I thought Bob Hess covered most of the important aspects of image burn. The later comments about mirrors leads me to suggest that camera geometry and components be the first thing to consider in Bob's list. Pre-visualization begins here as well as with the choice of object. Types of mirrors used, distances from object to H1and H1 to H2, as well as reference lens to H1 and H2 all affect the distortions of the final image, the amount of information recorded, and final image brightness. Often, our camera systems are not so flexible, but being aware of characteristics and limitations of the system can aid pre-visualization.

For hot spots I usually thought in terms of information management where less information made it easier to deal with hot spots. In Justin's image of coral it looks as if there are hot spots but they do not burn in so much that they block the playback. This is positive. Part of his problem is that the object is naturally diffuse (overpacked with information, a more complex wavefront) and the lighting does little to help. I would try some more contrasty lighting to reduce information, and also give the image some visual snap. As per Bob Hess, try to anticipate where the eventual hot spot will occurr and design lighting on the object to mitigate the problem if possible.

In Justin's case image swing can be helpful in distributing hot image areas more evenly over the plate and allowing more efficient beam ratios. Final display objectives are an important consideration, though.

Justin, I agree with another post that a spatial filter or just clean lenses would do wonders for you images. Cleaning up the reference beam and working on squeegee techinque would be high on my to do list.
