ErichRose Wed Feb 13, 2008 8:16 pm #2
Two different things.

Coherence refers to the fact that for the most part (nothing's perfect) all the light waves are lined up, marching in step.

Collimation is merely a beam of light travelling parrallel to itself, not diverging or expanding. It could be laser light or white light.

Laser diodes emit a beam whose shape is often oval or squeezed. I believe this is an artifact of the inherent design of diodes. Tube lasers produce beams that ebven in modes other than TEM00 are still "overall" round based on the mirrors used at each end of the tube.

Colin Kaminski Thu Feb 14, 2008 2:28 am #4
Yeah, that is right. Laser collimation is nice because a normal light source dissipates at 1/d^2. Where d is distance. But, a collimated beam loses no power in a vacuum.

However, for holography we have a different reason, the math of a perfectly collimated beam is easier to "reverse". So, when we are trying to make a real image from a hologram it is easier for us to pass the beam "backwards" through the original hologram.
JohnFP Thu Feb 14, 2008 3:11 pm #5
Maybe this will help.

Think of the light as a wave and not a particle. Now lets look at a photon coming off an atom. The photon really does not shoot out like a particle (unless you are looking at it that way) but shoots out like an expanding baloon, a wave. This is a naturally diverging field. Now with a diode the wave is allowed to propigate out from the inner layer between the P and N junction. This is not a tube but a rectangular area that the light comes out. Now with a gas laser, that same light wave is still expanding in a balloon fashion but only the part of the wave that is traveling down the bore is allowed to exit the cavity. As you can see this restricts the expansion greatly. As long as each wave is in sinc with the others, you will have coherence even if you sample the whole expanding (balloon) set of waves, or sample a very small area on that balloon (the gas tube laser).

Again, this is just a layman explaination, one I use to realize what is happening.
ErichRose Thu Feb 14, 2008 7:22 pm #6
Let's be a little more clear about where collimation comes in. A typical tube laser such as a HeNe or Argon will put out a beam that for the most part has a very tiny amount of divergence. Collimation in that case is used after we have expanded the beam and we want the reference light hitting the full plate or film to be Collimated. As mentioned that makes it much easier to do H1 to H2 transfers and so on. In that case you are using rather large lenses or parabolic mirrors to collimate a beam that has been expanded thru a small lens or spatial filter.

But in the case of a diode laser I think the question was about the actual laser output of a diode needing to be collimated so that it acts more like a typical tube style laser. In that case, other than when shooting a single beam hologram, the output needs to be collimated into a small beam so that it is possible (or at least a lot easier) to stear it around the table.

I've not worked with diodes and have a 10mw SP HeNe awaiting use. But I can see the great advantage of using the diode laser's inherently diverging and "clean" beam to illuminate a single beam hologram. But for longer exposures doing multi-beam set ups I'm going to rely on that old SP workhorse.
