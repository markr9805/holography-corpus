Ancient photographer Alfred Watkins had a useful rule of thumb when developing his photographic plates.--- It is such a very useful guide for developing our holographic plates.

His guide was to time how long it took to see the first dark specs of silver starting to show up under safelight then multiply that by a certain factor and then immediately stop development. His factor did vary with developer and plate types but it was typically 5.

So with well exposed holographic plates they should first be given a water rinse, then plunged into a (white) dish of developer quickly (not placed in slowly!)

watched carefully in developer and all being well the more exposed areas should start to darken within about 5 seconds. My “Watkins factor” is usually FOUR rather than 5 .

So after 20 seconds (i.e. 4 x 5) I very quickly whip the plate out of dev and into rinse water for 2 secs and then into either an acid “stop” bath or acid bleach bath .

There are 2 important reasons for this apparent haste compared to the much longer leasurely times (in minutes rather than seconds) taken by folks back in Watkins’ time.

1) We need to keep the silver metal nano-grains as spherical as possible and prevent them from forming long light scattering filaments of silver which they usually do given enough time.

2) Very delicate gelatin coatings like Slavich PFG –03 or insufficiently anchored coatings like those described by Hans in the “Meeting in Holland” thread , need to be in and out of the developer as quickly as possible before irreversible damage is done.

The very alkaline ascorbate –metol developer is the best to get the highest fringe contrast and fastest development , but asking delicate plates to sit in it for 2 mins is likely to be asking too much.

Actually the A and B system can accommodate this so easily without resorting to propriatary devs.

For Example in the excellent dev:-

Solution A

40 g ascorbic acid

6 g “Metol” 4-methylaminophenol

Made up to 1 litre in distilled or deionized water

Solution B

100 g anhydrous sodium carbonate ( Na2CO)3

30 g Sodium hydroxide (NaOH).

Made up to 1 litre in distilled or deionized water

instead of

"Mix equal volumes of A and B for the developer".

Mix say 1.3 A with 1.0 B . It will then have a lower pH. As proportion of A goes up the pH goes down. But if you reduce pH too much you will not get that brilliant fringe contrast needed for brightest results.

Actually on a second point about my original post I should have mentioned the fog factor that can often occur. I find that silver development from chemical fogging causing an overall grayness independent of light exposure seems to take place fortunately more slowly than that caused by the brightest areas from laser exposure. So a Watkins factor can be used OK unless the fogging is very heavy.
