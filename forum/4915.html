Dinesh Thu Jun 23, 2005 1:11 pm #5
There are a couple of ways to do this. The easiest is to light the microscope with one or more lightings. Place a transparency under the microscope and lit it from underneath (ie have a lighting from beneath so that the light goes through the shaft of the microscope) The magnified transparency can now be seen by a real viewer through the eyepiece of the real microscope. Since this is real light, it'll show up on the hologram. Thus the illuminated microscope will become a hologram of a microscope, because that's how you lit it, and the image of the magnified transparency as seen through the microscope wll be seen "through" the eyepiece of the holographic image of the microscope. If you place your eye at the eyepiece of the image, you'll see what you would have seen through the real thing simply because what the plate sees is what you see. Think of it as a memory of all you saw had your face been in the plate holder at the time you shot the hologram.

DJ Mathson Thu Jun 23, 2005 2:30 pm #8
I think the microscope hologram is made with the "key hole" technique. One way to do this: You make one master hologram of the microscope, and one master of the object that is to be seen through the eyepiece. Then make a second master where you first make an exposure from the microscope master with the eyepiece aligned to the second master plate and with the area of the eyepiece masked off. Then make a second exposure from the master of the object with everything exept the eyepiece area masked off. Then you can transfer this second master to a new hologram.

In this case there won't be any magnification of the object, since the light from the object will not have gone through the lenses of the microscope. I have a similar piece of a telescope trained on a distant house. When seen from a distance, you can't make out any detail of the house. When you place your eye to the eyepiece of the telescope, you can see a couple in the window of the house.

I assume the idea is that you'd use a single lens and a dedicated H1 to simulate the magnification of the miscroscope. This would probably be a better way of doing it because the image in a real microscope is virtual. Also, you're expected to put your eye right into the eyepiece which then blocks off stray light, whereas a holographic eyepiece will not block stray light. In my original reply, I gave the "easiest" method of doing this, which was intended to be a sort of: "here's one way of doing it". I thought that no-one had replied to Nick about the hows and whys of these type of images and gave a broad overview. If I were actually trying to do it, I'd have a real image (from maybe a curved mirror) project where the virtual image would have been in a real microscope and create a seperate H1 just for that. The aperture would have nbeen much larger than a real microscope aperture, but the ligth gathering power would also be greater and the leeway in eye position would be better.

In the great Science Museum at South Kensington , London, up on the top floor there is an Optics section. The hologram is a transmission HIG (Holographic Image Generator) which has depth, this means you have to have the light the other side of the plate to yourself and the light needs to be monochromatic to see it clearly. This hologram demonstrates beautifully why the light needs to be monochromatic and also an additional weird effect if you don't display a transmission HIG in the same color as you made it in.

It is displayed in the glass case with a small point-source mercury lamp shining through the plate towards you. The thing about this lamp is that it has 3 distinct

colors which are fairly monochromatic. Violet, green and red.

This produces the weird effect of creating 3 distinct partially overlapping alligators in each colour.

The red one being closest to the plate and the violet one being not only furthest from the plate but also sightly longer and thinner, the green one being intermediate in all respects. So how can one set of fringes produced by one object finish up replaying 3 objects depending on your reconstructing light color. You can understand now why

you cannot use a white light to replay the HIG or you just get a rainbow fuzz of multiple alligators. I hope someone out there can point you to a good website that explains some of this with animations. There are always those old fashioned books of course, the best I think being by Graham Saxby.
