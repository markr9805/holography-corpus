Ed Wesly Tue Nov 01, 2005 9:28 pm #1
This was a thought experiment that hasn't been run to my knowledge. It came up during discussions regarding Beam Balance Ratios.

While we worked at Fermilab, Hans calculated that you could have a greater than 1,000,000 to 1 reference to object beam ratio and still create a detectable image of a point source. This was relevant to the Bubble Chamber holograms we were recording, as the bubbles in the liquid hydrogen that were the objects were point source scatterers, and illumination brightness was kind of hit or miss in the Gabor type holo set up being used.

But then we started thinking about the role of the beam balance ratio. It is generally agreed that the lower the ratio, the higher the fringe contrast, but in the case of diffusely reflecting objects the noise spectrum can go up. If there is the luxury of test shots, I would normally play with the exposure (keeping development fixed) and find the brightest exposure. To get a brighter image, I might then add more object light, and reduce reference to get something brighter, hopefully significantly so.

But the point of the Conundrum as I have christened it is thus: Suppose you are recording a transmission hologram of a large backlit groundglass, the basis of a stereogram printer. The exposure and ratio parameters are set, and the hologram processed.

This hologram is interrogated with the conjugate beam, and the real image is projected onto a 1 cm square detector. The brightness of this square is recorded.

Now back to the set up. A one cm^2 aperture is placed on the groundglass, coinciding with the spot that was just measured in the larger field, significantly reducing the object beam. The Beam Balance Ratio is jacked up in the reference beam's favor by there being less object light, not changing the flux in the reference. This remains the same.

A new hologram is shot, changing none of the flux in the reference beam, exposed and processed exactly as the previous one. Now when this is interrogated, shouldn't that same 1 cm^2 spot be just as bright as in the first one, even though the reference beam ratio is much higher?

We seemed to think so, but never got around to shooting any holos to prove it.

I'm surprised this didn't get any comments .

My intuition leads me to agree that the photometric spot brightness should be roughly the same, neglecting (probably small) effects caused by operating at a different place on the film gamma curve due to less total light on the plate in the small-spot case.

I think, though, that there would be lots of room for increasing the brightness of the small spot because the object beam brightness could be increased greatly before the film saturated. For very small spots, the hologram would be acting as a holographic lens, focusing all diffracted incoming light onto a tiny point with very high brightness.
JohnFP Thu Nov 03, 2005 11:09 am #3
I appreciate the post and have read it many times to understand your point.

I have to agree. Here is my laymen’s thought.

Let's look at a single fringe area, including the constructive and destructive area, remembering the fringes are three dimensional (they have depth). In the first set up there is a spot on every part of the ground glass trying to make its way with the fringe. Shaping, molding conforming it with it's constructive and destructive interactions. Take one point on the ground glass, interfere it with the reference beam to get maximum constructive interference at one point on the plate with a sinusoidal fall off toward the destructive area to the side and a sinusoidal fall off toward the destructive area down in the depth of the emulsion (depth meaning normal to bisections of beams, side meaning perpendicular to bisection). Then as you move away from that point on the ground glass (changing distance of just the object -changes the phase at the same point on the emulsions that interacts with the reference beam) but continue to monitor the fringe area you will see the constructive areas turn destructive and the destructive areas turn constructive but shaping the fringes ability to bend (refract) the replay light toward the new direction of the object beam light.

So although the ratios seems to change from your one thought experiment to the second, not all object light is being used in the first to make the part of the fringe that will refract the light upon replay to the "square" spot.

So when making a hologram of a piece of ground glass, I wonder what the ratio really is when you aperture down the ground glass smaller and smaller.

Also, is there some complex happening such that somewhere on the ground glass the light is diffracted in such a way that it reinforces constructive and destructive areas with other spots off the ground glass? That is, is it possible that a point on the ground glass way off to the side actually helps the replay spot on the hologram to be diffracted toward the center of the real image, not the side where it came from? What about if the simple ground glass had irregularities that were uniform to cause the diffraction?

I do believe limitations in the recording medium will alter these findings somewhat but an absolute recording medium will prove it true 100 true.

OK, I have thought a little about this too. I measure the object light on a point by point basis. I am really looking for the brightest object spot. This allows me to make sure I don't "burn in" the film in that spot.

In H1 to H2 transfers Lon taught me to make sure the brightest spot on the image is slightly outside the film plane.

So in this case I only would consider the local ratio anyways.

So back to the Conundrum:

Now it is quite possible that the image is brighter in the second case! Since each spot on the ground glass is acting as it's own reference beam it will tend to create lots of noise. (I assume in a Stereogram printer one needs to be very careful about the bright spots on the ground glass.) So simply by making the ground glass smaller with an aperture would make less cross talk from the object beam.
