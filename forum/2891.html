Speaking of reflection holograms: unless you apply special techniques like SHSG, some colloidal developers etc., no fixing is involved.

The following section is taken from Richard Birenheide's 1997 website. Birenheide had designed the HRT emulsion - the processing baths baths were based on Jeff Blyth's ascorbic acid developer (now called "JD-4") and his copper sulfate bleach.

"Suggested Processing Formulae

Rehalogenating process

a. Developer

700 cc water (deionized, if available)

70 g sodium carbonate, anhydrous

15 g sodium hydroxide

4 g metol (4-(methylamino)-phenol-sulfate)

25 g ascorbic acid

add water to 1000 cc

Please allow any constituent to dissolve be-

fore adding the next on the list. It is important

to limit the time the surface of this developer

is exposed to air (oxygen). Therefore the use

of two closely fitting plastic dishes is strongly

recommended so that one dish floats

("floating lid") on top of the developer and

the only time the whole surface is exposed to

air is when the development progress is being

monitored. Develop to a density of at least

2.5. Minimum development time: 3 min.

b. Stop bath

1000 cc water (deionized, if available)

5 g sodium hydrogen sulfate crystals

Rinse briefly in cold water and then bleach.

c. Bleach

700 cc water (deionized, if available)

35 g copper sulfate (pentahydrate)

100 g potassium bromide

5 g sodium hydrogen sulfate crystals

add water to 1000 cc

Bleach until the hologram is clear. Rinse in

cold water for at least 5 min. Normally, unless you

are making reflection master holograms, this bleach

should always be followed by an "anti-print

out" bath such as:-

d. Anti printout

700 cc water (deionized, if available)

3 g potassium dichromate

6 g sodium hydrogen sulfate

add water to 1000 cc

Treat for about 1 minute with agitation.

To rinse off, just give a brief dip in a bath of

deionized water free of any trace of devel-

oper. The brevity of the rinse is necessary to

actually leave some of the anti-printout solu-

tion within the body of the hologram.

Please note that dichromate is a toxic material

and treated unprotected holograms should

not be handled by young children.

This "anti-print out" has the additional advan-

tage that it will break down any residual dye

present in the emulsion from sensitization.

However, with the "pyrochrome" system (see

below) that bleach will serve as a dye

remover."

It depends on the bleach. A reversal bleach will remove the silver and leave just the silver halide, a rehalogenating bleach will rehalogenate the reduced silver. This sounds crazy since you've just put back the silver halide you've reduced. We talked on this in the old forum, but I can't find the thread.

The periodic structure of the fringes causes diffraction, but also there is absorption of the black silver and scattering from both reduced silver and halide. Whic process dominates depends on the grain density inside the emulsion and the fringe structure. If the fringes are sinusoidal diffraction is very strong.

In any cae, you need to wash thoroughly both in between develop and bleach and after bleach.

> Is PBQ a bleach using p-Benzoquinone?

Yes, as Dinesh pointed out, it's considered quite toxic. I understand the health risk mainly stems from handling PBQ as a solid, in „dust“ form (a problem it may share with stuff like dichromates). However there is another PBU bleach formula that allows in situ PBQ formation (it takes about 2 hours to form the PBQ):

cupric bromide....................1g

potassium persulphate.......10g

citric acid...........................50g

potassium bromide............20g

hydroquinone (quinol).........1g

water...................................1 L

In contrast to PBU-amidol, PBU-quinol keeps very long. On emulsions like PFG-01 etc. PBU-quinol may produce a bit more noise (not sure whether this is an issue with PFG-03, HRT or Ultimate). On the other hand it doesn't stain the emulsion as PBU-amidol does.

The very best PBU formula in my view is PBU-metol:

cupric bromide....................1g

potassium persulphate.......10g

citric acid...........................50g

potassium bromide............20g

metol...................................1g

water...................................1L

> Colin Kaminski: Can you explain LD50 for us? I know a little about it but I am not sure everyone here does and I think it is very important.

The LD50 is the lethal dose, that causes deaths of 50% of animals in

an experiment. The European Union defines a LD50 of 200 mg/kg

for oral ingestion for rats as the limit to classify chemicals as toxic.

Chemicals with 200mg/kg-2000 mg/kg are classified as harmful (Xn).

You can compare those values with those of common chemicals

as ethanol (LD50 about 7000 mg/kg), sodium carbonate

(about 4000 mg/kg) or Barium nitrate (355 mg/kg), silver nitrate

(1173 mg/kg). Rather toxic chemicals like HgCl2 have an LD50 of

1 mg/kg, KCN has 5 mg/kg.

For Hydroquinone a value of 320 mg/kg was given. So 640 mg

Hydroquinone could kill a rat with a weight of 2 kg.

For p-Benzoquinone a smaller dose would produce a similar result.
