alesnick Wed May 04, 2005 12:28 am #5
i made a hologram of a frozen ceramic top to a sugar bowl a few years ago

i was doing a series of interferometric exposures -- i _wanted_ the fringes to appear - so i could interpret how various stresses affected objects. I did double exposures of cantilevers with stresses applied and removed, an oscillating tuning fork, and this ceramic bowl. I did an exposure with it thawing, and also another where i heated the object with a lighter in a certain spot

i ended up getting a very bright hologram with a 1.5 second exposure, but with fringes indicating the swelling of the object. Note: i did not freeze the plate or anything else in the setup. the object was not resting on the same surface as the plate either.

the final holograms are on display at my old high school, so i won't be able to take pictures, but i think i have a few pictures of the double exposure cantilevers i could post. the holograms look very similar with respect to the brightness and fringe formation.

I didn't expect it to be that bright as I did not arrange the table for this hologram. (Edited-Added) Also, the grayish look to the entire hologram with a pretty reflective back is due to the reference beam being almost perpendicular to the plate in the creation. I didn't want to tear down my existing set up on the table.

I put the cube purposely on a steel mount and put both in the freezer such that the mass of the mount kept the ice cube from melting at it's base, thus moving the entire cube. I think an insulator might work but I felt cold mass was best.

What I was tying to do is take advantage of what I remember about ice. That is, when you take energy (heat) out of water it drops in temperature. Then when it gets to 32 degrees it is still water. Then take a little more energy out and it turnes to ice but is still at 32 degrees. Then more energy out and the temperature falls. What I was hoping, which I think I accomplished, was to allow the ice cube to rise temperature but keep it in the frozen state, thus the massive cold base. Also, this was a perfect time to try this as the temperature in Maryland has been cool, thus the lab was cool and I did not turn on the heat. Ambient temp may have been about 63 F.

MichaelH Fri May 06, 2005 1:31 pm #20
yep, way too ambitious. I'm not going to work out the math, but in a 60 degree F room, an object is going to thaw fairly rapidly depending on the mass. A flower has much less mass than an ice cube or steel plate and will warm much more rapidly. Four minutes may be an eternity in that case.

On the other hand, flowers can (and have) been done by keeping the room as cold as possible and speeding up the processing (in the one case I know of by Lon Moore, by heating the developer).
