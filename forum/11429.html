Having said all that, in answer to your question, the holograms you show, on the Canadian notes and the Pokeman cards, I believe their what are called Kinoforms. This is a computerised technique developed in 1969 ("The Kinoform: a new wavefront reconstruction device", IBM Journal of Research and Development (1969) by Lesem, Hirsch, Jordan). Kinoforms today are variously called kinograms, OVD's and DOVID's.

First, some background.

1. Consider light like a moving clock. As the light propagates, the hand of the clock rotates; the hand's position, or rather the angle of the hand from some arbitrary zero at any point along it's path is known as the phase of the light. So, if we set our zero at twelve and the hand shows 4 when it reaches a plate, it's phase is 95 deg - the angle bwtween 12 and 4. Now, it hits a medium, such as a hologram, and it "sees" a different medium. The new medium speds up the clock, and also causes the light to deviate from it's path - the phase change causes light of a specific colur to change in a specific way and white light will form a blur. The grooves of a hologram are variations of height, and the phase change is based on the variation of thickness.On a real, say resist hologram, every point on the hologram contains a height variation, causing a difference in phase chage, which together form the image.

2. According to Fourier Theory, every real image - say an image generated by Photoshop - is made up of a series of sinusoidal lines, at diiferent directions, with different spacing. Some lines may be spaced out at 10 lines/millimetre and some at 20. These lines determine the phase information of the picture. If this phase information could be directly imposed onto a resist plate, you could make a pure phase hologram.

The kinoform is a way of computerised holography, where the phase information on a picture is directly imposed on the plate. The computer "decides" on the phase profile, and "decides" how to alter the phase, and so to deviate an image into a specific colour when turned a specific angle. So, if you have a JPEG of stars, you extract the phase information, alter the phase information according to application and transfer phase information onto a plate as a variation of height. This can be done with an e-beam, where the intensity of the e-baem determines the height at every point. There are different algorithms, but the popular one is the Gerchbert-Saxton algorithm. There are also lots of MATLAB codes for OVD's and DOVID's all over the internet. However, DOVID's are being promoted as the ultimate in security because most holographers cannot grasp the maths, physics and computer skills involved, while most people don't understand holography into a very technical level. You need someone skilled in diffractive optics, mathematics and programming - and there ain't lot of them!

You've got to differentiate between phase information and phase difference. It's an unfortunate co-incidence that Fourier Theory uses the word "phase" in one sense, while Optical Physics uses it in another sense. The phase of a beam of light (Optical Physics) is based on the distance the light has travelled from some point A to some point B. If this distance AB = d, then the phase of the light at B, relative to the phase at A, φ = kd = (2π/λ)d, where λ is the Greek letter lambda, traditionally used for wavelength, and φ is the Greek letter phi, traditionally used for phase in the literature and in textbooks. In a lot of cases, it's assumed that the phase of the light at A is zero, hence you see remarks like 'the phase at B is kd', insinuating the phase at B is absolute; it's not, it's relative to the phase at A, assumed to be zero. You see the same thing with temperature statements in weather forecasts, where the forecaster says it going to be X degrees tomorrow, the X degrees is not absolute, it's relative to a presumed zero of temperature. The reason we have two types of temperature scales is that we use two different zeroes - one for Celsius and one for Fahrenheit.

Anyway, getting back to the point, suppose you have a 3D object like a coffee mug - by the by, my holography mentor, Peter Miller, used a coffee mug to demonstrate phase, who told me his mentor, Fred Unterseher used a coffee mug to demonstrate phase, who told me that his mentor, Lloyd Cross, used a coffee mug to demonstrate phase, so you'd be the 6th generation holographer to see the use of a coffee mug to demonstate phase. But I digress. When you see the mug, you see different aspects of it, some nearer you, and some further away. As you move around the room, these aspects change, but the relative distance between two points of the mug at two different distances remain the same. Because the relative distance between two points on the mug remains constant, the phase difference between the two points remains the same. When you see a hologram (traditional), you see a (hologram of a) coffee mug because the relative distances between all the points of the mug remained the same, the hologram is a map of phase differences between any two points on the mug. If you rotate the mug as you record, the phase difference is no longer the same and the image goes black. By the way, the reason you see in 3d is because there's a small difference in path length between any point and your two eyes. The brain calculates the phase difference and translates this difference to perception of depth. How does the hologram capture the phase differences between points on the mug? By introducing a constant beam to which all the light from the mug is referenced - a 'reference beam'.So, the phase difference between the (constant) reference and the phase from any point on the mug is fixed. For another part of the mug, the phase difference between the reference beam and that part of the mug is also a constant, but a different constant. The hologram is the difference of all the differences from all points on the mug and the fixed reference - it's a set of differences of difference.

For a kinoform, the phase referred to is the phase of the Fourier Transform. In Fourier Theory, any 2D image can be reeconstructed by a set of lines at different spacing (ie different frequencies) , different thicknesses and different orientations; anything from a simple white square against a black background to the Mona Lisa can be broken down - representd - by a series of lines. These lines are the 'phase information' of the image in the sense of Information Theory. This has nothing to do with the word 'phase' used in Optical Physics. So, for instance, if I give you a hundred cards, each with a set of lines drawn at different spacings and thicknesses, and I tell you, "Take card #1 and orientate it North-South, take card #2 put it on card #1, but twist it by 10 deg. clockwise, take card #3, put it on card #2, but twist it the other way by 25 deg" etc for all hundred cards, you may end up seeing a cat. These hundred cards are the phase information of the cat.

In a kinoform, the 'kinoform machine' is given these cards, and, for each card, the machine creates a hole of a certain depth in an emulsion based on the properties of the card - frequency and orientation. After all the hundred cards are fed into the "kinoform machine", you end up with an emulsion that has a depth profile corresponding to the specific set of cards for the cat. When you look at the hologram, you'll see a cat changing colours continuosly based on the software telling the machine how to process the cards - a kinogram. The final hologram is just the profile of the phase information of the picture - a cat in this case.

Of course it can be a dot matrix! What you see is a visual effect. Is there more than one way of demonstrating a visual effect? Of course, that's the basis of stage magic!

There are two problems with all the "holographic" images and video on the internet:

1.You have no idea how the image(s) are reconstructed, ie the lighting on the hologram. Any actual dot matrix can look like a kinoform, and vice versa, just by selective (or accidental) lighting. To see a set of bright vibrant colours go by as a video is played may be a dot matrix, or it may be a kinoform, or it may be just diffractive effects from a diffractive structure that has nothing to do with holography. If, for example, you took a video sequence of a DVD (I mean the disc itself) as it's rotated/twisted, with bright sunlight illuminating it, it would look just like a dot matrix/kinoform hologram

2. Any 'hologram' can be made in any number of ways. In fact, any apparent 'holographic effect' can be created in a number of ways, including just by photoshop. For example, I take a large black card and cut it up into random geometric shapes - masks - that could be removed from the card to creatre random geometric "holes" in the large card. I place the card in front of a holographic plate. I now have a constant beam, say on axis (zero deg) covering the entire plate/card area. I now remove one mask, and record with a beam at, say, 30 deg - the exposed mask area is a grating recorded with an on-axis beam and a 30 deg beam. I replace the mask and remove another geometric area - another mask. Now I record this new exposed are with a 45 deg beam. Now I have two small areas on my (larger) plate, one grating at zero and 30, and one at zero and 45. If I repeat this proces several times, I'll have a smorgasbrod of different geometric shapes, all recorded with different angles, and so showing different colours as I rotate/translate the plate. Depending on the size of the mask, it'll look just like a dot matrix; if the masks had fuzzy edges, it'd look like a kinoform.

In other words, simply showing a video/image of a 'hologram' on the internet, without showing the lighting conditions, it could be anything from a dot matrix/kinoform, to a set of complicated gratings, to even small spheres that look coloured by diffraction (In the US, some traffic signs have these spheres to highlight them at night).

I suspect most people who claim any video sequence is a dot matrix have never actually used/seen a dot matrix hologram. Some people see a video of a "dot matrix" hologram, then see another another video that looks remarkably similar and claim that too is a dot matrix - adding 2 + 2 and making 5.

I repeat, as I said earlier, unless I can hold the hologram, and reconstruct it under controlled lighting (I've been asked to analyse various types of holograms and have subjected them to laser light), any video sequence or image on the internet claiming to be a hologram of one sort or another may, or may not, actually be such a hologram, or even a hologram itself.

The Visa dove, and the Mastercard were made as Rainbow holograms. It'll be too long to explain how make split beam holography - there are plenty of online explanations, but previous to the '60s, the only holograms were laser viewable, you couldn't see them in ordinary ('white') light. Two people broke through with white light holography: Yuri Denisyuk (below, with me ) and Stephen Benton ([https://www.flickr.com/photos/din_p/7761754188/](https://www.flickr.com/photos/din_p/7761754188/) - Steve Benton, me and Joel; that's me with a bag slung over my shoulder, and Joel Kollin an MIT grad who ended up in Micrsoft Research). Yuri Denisyuk had a single plate solution - now called 'Denisyuk holograms, and Steve had a two-plate solution - now called Rainbow Holograms. Rainbow holograms could be stamped onto foils or into credit cards.

ABN (American BankNote) was the company making banknotes. As far as I was aware, Ken Haines ( [https://www.flickr.com/photos/din_p/7761755088/](https://www.flickr.com/photos/din_p/7761755088/) ) showed someone in ABN how to emboss, which got them to create a new company American Banknote Holographics (ABNH). I wasn't around ABNH in those days, but Steve McGrew also presumably was involved in showing ABNH how to emboss rainbows. As of the 90's, when I was around, there was a long ongoing lawsuit between Ken haines, Steve McGrew and ABNH - perhaps someone else here knows more details.

At any rate, by about 1986, ABNH made the first run of Visa doves - about two million of them - and the Mastercard holograms. BY 1996, the master hologram (H1) for the visa dove was wearing thin, and it was decided to shoot a new H1, slightly redesigned, and a new Mastercard H1. At ABNH, in 1996, at the time the holography group consisted of me, Lenny Vincatore, Todd and Joy. For the dove, it was decided that Lenny would shoot the H1, and I would do the H2 transfer. I promptly shot the H2, and sent it to the Production Dept., who then transferred it to foil and printed (I believe) 500,000 Visa cards. For the Mastercard hologram, it was decided to overlay a dot matrix witya conventional image. I made the dot matrix, and Joy shot the H1 and someone (me? Lenny? ) combined both into one single hologram and sent it to Production.
