The following may be considered a „by-product“ of POLYGRAMA's ([http://www.polygrama.com](http://www.polygrama.com)) quest for photopolymers...

PC-bleach is a new rehalogenating bleach based on POTASSIUM CHROMATE:

Potassium chromate..............................6g

Potassium bromide..............................80g

Acetic acid...........................................50g

Water......................................................1L

In contrast to traditional rehalogenating dichromate bleaches, this one has the advantage of remaining stable over a long period of time (a serious drawback though is its inherent toxicity – compared to say, Fe EDTA bleach). Hence, there is no bromine formation.

Mixing the potassium chromate with an acid like this, is likely to generate some dichromate also. According to Jeff Blyth this kind of bleach may actually form sort of an equilibrium of dichromate and chromate.

Despite the high levels of chromate it is a rather mild bleach. So, based on acetic acid, potassium chromate and without the bromide, no reversal bleach can be formed.

This new bleach is supposed to work at a higher pH range (pH 3-4) than traditional dichromate bleaches, therefore I would expect gelatin crosslinking to be improved. So it seems well fit for special applications like SHSG and alike.

The bleach may require additional fine-tuning.

For example, the amount of acetic acid can be lowered to about 20ml/L. Below that level, the higher pH leads to increased levels of stain though. Actually, the stain is not that far in character from that of a colloidal developed layer or a PBU-Amidol bleached emulsion. So it may not be completely useless.

As of now I tested the new bleach on 8E75 and PFG-01, where it produced nice bright H3 reflection holograms.

I guess for finer grained emulsions the formula is likely to require some adaptation, most likely by lowering KBr concentration. 30-40g/L KBr would seem to be a good start for Colourholographics, Ultimate and PFG-03.

I really like the look of this bleach----such a simple improvement I can see would give really improved results and no Bromine release with the bonus of hardening the gelatin which FeEDTA, CuSO4, ferric nitrate bleaches just do not have. Acetic acid is technically a weaker acid than dichromate (i.e. chromic acid) so that means not much dichromate is likely to be produced.

An interesting point discussed here a year or 3 back is the differential hardening of light fringes compared to dark fringes with chromate bleach but then the discussion centred on dichromate reversal bleach not chromate rehalogenating bleach----the point has been made (Haiharan) that with a reversal bleach the light fringes get tanned more than the dark fringes as the dark silver metal dissolves up into solution by reducing dichromate to the tanning CrIII ions in those sites BUT the diffraction comes from the unaltered AgBr still left in the dark fringes. --- so slightly higher refractive from

both tanning in the light fringes and the other type of tanning from pyrogallol developer used in Van Renesse's "pyrochrome" system would seem to work against the higher refractive index of the dark fringes, the fringe contrast would be lowered a bit.----maybe but the pyrochrome system can still give remarkably good diffraction though and the printout resistance is way better than the rehalogating types. But now you and Sergio have made a

marked simple improvement which certainly makes a high printout -resistant rehalogenating bleach as well with the benefit of no loss of silver that occurs with reversal bleach . But I not completely clear in my mind as to whether the tanning effect is working for or against increased diffraction efficiency because the tanned light fringes I would think would be emptied out of silver as the reoxidized silver settled down onto the AgBr still in the dark fringes. ---(talk about academic waffle!)--the bottom line is if it works well, it works well. I will be using you bleach from now on.

Jeff

As for the tanning action of the PC-bleach, I am not sure what's taking place on a microscopic level. My assumption that there must be tanning were solely based on a few reflection SHSG holograms I did. They were made on 8E75 mostly. Though not particularly bright (I didn't try to push them by using elevated processing temperatures either) they looked very clean. This may relate to the relatively low gelatin swelling during the bleaching step. I guess gelatin „damage“ beyond the light fringes may be kept reasonably small in that case.
