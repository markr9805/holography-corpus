I'm looking at one of my MBDCG holograms. It's not big (6x6cm), it's not pretty and technically speaking it's a failure - sign of movement, interference patterns, unewen swelling etc. But when the holo is laying between me and the monitor and acts as a mirror I can see the most wonderful shades of blue, green and red. Actually it's only the thing on my messy desk that seems to have a color at all. But this colour doesn't look like an ordinary metallic blue met on the cars. It seems to glow and have it's own luminescence. And I can't stop staring at this colour and I can't stop smiling

My Kasia says it's a colour that one can meet only at soap bubbles and I guess she's right. Everything else I can think of including reflections from CDs, patches of oil on the water or parrot's feathers, colours of the rainbow etc. seems to be only an imitation of MBDCG interference colours.

Maybe this is the reason why I fell in love with MBDCG. Not only because of 3D appearence but mainly because of these magical blues, greens and reds. I think guys that we are lucky that we have a chance to preserve and show them to other people.

You CAN mix Rhodamine 6G with MB OK Hans. Let us just get this into perspective now . We are dealing here on this site with 2 different types of dyes,

cationic and anionic. If you look the dyes up in the chem catalogue you will see the cationic ones are all chloride or iodide salts.

You will see that MB, Rhodamine 6G and Safranine 'O' are all chloride salts and that means that they can be mixed together and stay in solution OK.

A potential problem may come when they are mixed with anionic dyes. Anionic dyes are those in the catalogue linked to sodium ions.

So erythrosin, eosim, rose bengal are all anionic dyes . So you have for example sodium erythrosinate salt in effect. If you mix this with

MB ie. methylene blue chloride salt you run the risk of precipitating out a methylene blue erythrosinate salt and being left with sodium chloride in solution.

However in this particular case I have noticed that the MB erythrosinate salt is still sufficiently soluble to do the job sometimes under water rich conditions but once it gets into a well dried gelatin film it may form an unreactive precipitate leaving you with one or other of the dyes that were in excess over the other one , still able to do the job singly.(unless they happened to be exactly matched on a molecule for molecule basis of course.)

Now the problem with the cationic dyes and dichromates is that they all seem inclined to form insoluble inactive precipitates with dichromates

ie form dichromate salts instead of chloride salts. In this tight chemical combination the dichromate part has a secondary effect which can quite quickly

get to work oxidizing the dyes beyond repair apparently. Fortunately by keeping solutions alkaline the dichromates become chromates and precipitates don't form with the cationic dyes here. ------ Yes this does mean you CAN mix the anionic dyes with dichromates without the same precipitation problem because they cannot form dichromate salts .

I have not managed yet to find an anionic dye that can replace the cationic MB for working with red lasers and straight acid dichromate. I think it has been a bit of a Holy Grail for us for a long time .

Joe Farina Tue Nov 06, 2007 12:24 pm #10
> mrutic: I am working on colour holography. I would like is anybody using a single spatial filter to clean all the three laser beams (633, 532, 473 nm) simultaneously. If somebody is doing this and if you dont mind please help me in this regard.

Someone by the name of "alackofcolor" on this forum has done it, but he doesn't participate any more. If you use the search feature on this forum for the thread called "my full color holography setup" you will see some photos of his layout, complete with spatial filter.

He shows all the lasers on the table, along with the spatial filter. This makes alignment much easier, compared to doing it with the lasers off the table (which is what I'm working on). I don't know of any special tricks in this regard, you just need a way to position the lasers properly, make sure beams are circular (if you're using DPSS lasers with less-than-perfect beam quality), have a way of changing the diameter of the beams (using some type of telescope configuration), and use an appropriate focusing lens or objective in combination with the pinhole. A larger pinhole will make it easier. There is also some information on the Wiki.

Are you sure your 473nm laser can make good holograms? I don't know of any which can produce non-banded holograms.
