MichaelH Mon Jan 30, 2006 12:16 pm #2
The light being reflected off the subject/object forms the second "beam" in a Denisyuk hologram. To make a hologram you just need interfering wave patterns and a means of recording them, which typically means a reference beam and some other source of light that is generated from the same laser.

That can be another raw beam (in the case of a HOE) or merely the light reflected off the "thing" you're making a hologram of. Just to name two.
Tony Mon Jan 30, 2006 12:23 pm #3
The reference beam in a Denisyuk is the (expanded) beam coming directly from the laser to the film. This passes through the film and is reflected from the object. The light reflecting from the object back to the film is the 'object' beam.

The coherence length of a diode laser is usually fairly long, 20cm or more, depending on the particular diode.

As to your hologram - congratulations on getting something on your first try! Here's some ideas on improving things:

1. Missing parts of the subject. Most likely, either motion or a thermal gradient. It's entirely possible to have only parts of the subject dissapear with motion - if there is rotation, some parts of the subject will move too much, while others will move less. Try to support your subject on three points so it can't rock. Also, mechanically coupling the plate/film to the subject will help in a Denisyuk setup.

If they are in fixed locations in the hologram, then it could be film movement if your using film sandwiched between glass. If using the glass sandwich, make sure the clamps apply pressure inside the area of the film so the glass does not bow outward (very slightly, but enough to allow film movement).

To test that (and you always need to test assumptions in holography) put a piece of opaque tape across the top of the plate so your reconstruction light can't enter that edge. Do the stripes change? If either case happens, the stripes are and were caused by light entering the edge of the glass and bouncing between the two surfaces.

![Image](http://www.dragonseye.com/gallery/d/2846-1/LightEnteringPlateEdge.jpg)

I say "were" because that happened during the exposure and "are" because when you shine a light to reconstruct the hologram it happens again.

It will fade out as the beam moves down the plate because a little bit of energy is lost at every reflection.

> Carolyn: How should I be determining exposure time? Is it always guess and check? My instructions from integraf said to try 10 sec, but I could go down to 5 sec and up to 40...which was a little, lot, vague for my liking.

Without a power meter it's always guess and test. If you have access to a laser power meter you can see how much energy is hitting the plate and set your exposure accordingly.

The simplest way to test your exposure time is to do a three exposure test. Set your "table" up and use a white card as your subject. Now cover 2/3 of the plate with a black card and do an exposure of 1/2 your normal exposure. Uncover another 1/3 of the plate and do another exposure of the same length. Uncover the last 1/3 and do the last exposure of the same time. IE, if your guesstimate expsure time is 12 seconds you'll end up doing three exposures on the same plate of 6 seconds each. Develop the plate to the kits instructions and whichever strip is the brightest is the exposure that's closest to the "right" time for your setup.

Intensity information is encoded and reproduced as follows:

The intensity of the interference pattern recorded on the film is determined by the ratios of light interfereing. So a bright area on the subject will produce a pattern with greater contrast between the light and dark parts of the fringes.

On reconstruction, the greater the difference between the fringe 'peaks and valleys', the greater the intensity of the diffracted light from it.

Shiny objects are difficult, because the bright reflections can 'burn out' the film in spots, and if you lower the exposure enough to prevent that, then the very low reflections get lost. Remember, you're doing single-beam Denisyuks (or maybe some single-beam transmissions). This is a lot like starting off with a very simple disposable film camera - it works, but it's limited. Using a diffuse object will give more light over a wider area, which will show up better in the limited brightness of a Denisyuk.

I am going to try my hand at explaining why a shiney object does not work as well.

A hologram is made by the interference of two beams. The object beam and the reference beam.

Now, back to the mirror analogy I used. Lets say your object is a doggy and he has a flat nose. If that nose is shiny like a mirror then his nose will only reflect light like a mirror to one spot on the plate, let's say the lower left corner. That is the only place the nose light hits the plate and "mixes" with the reference beam to cause interfernce. If you try to look at the nose throuh the center of the plate where no nose light hit then there will be no nose hologram there.

Now lets say the nose is made out of a diffusing flat white paint. The light from the nose now gets diffused and reflects back to every spot on the plate interfering with the reference beam everywhere on the plate. Now the nose can be seen everywhere on the plate from all angles.

OK, here goes:

> -why does the hologram distort itself if I change the angle of the light? By moving my flashlight I can make my rhinosceros' horns grow...or his legs etc...and it gets blurrier as it distorts.

In order to reconstruct the same image, a hologram needs to be illuminated by a beam that exaclty matches the original reference beam (assuming no emulsion shrinkage during processing). Because your flashlight doesn't exactly match the laser diode (especially in terms of divergence and curvature of wavefronts), some distortion will occur. For a reflection hologram, this distortion increases with the distance of the object from the plate. So the farthest parts of the subject from the plate will be the fuzziest and most distorted.

> if a shiney object doesn't work as well because it doesn't spread it's beam, then how DO the awesome holograms of the wine glasses work?

These are probably made through a two-step process, which is how the best and brightest reflection holograms can be made. First, a transmission hologram is made using a split-beam method, with multiple object illumination angles. Then a hologram of a holgram is made, where the image of the H1 is copied onto a reflection hologram called an H2. In this step, the brightness can be independently controlled.

> if one of my problems with the amethyst is that it's purple, would a longer exposure time (if I just had the amethyst and not the pewter train on top) help?

Nope. The brightness of the resultant hologram is controlled by the ratio of the object light to the reference light. Exposing or developing the film more just makes the fringes darker in both the peaks and the valleys, but the ratio stays (pretty much) the same. Overall, holograms are remarkably insensitive to exposure.

It usually does, however the problem is to make an HOE at one laser wavelength, but to use it at another wavelength.

Example: a Heads Up Display. These are used in fighter planes. The problem is that a pilot would have to avert his gaze from his quarry to look down at his instruments, losing precious milliseconds. The HUD HOE is transparent so that the pilot can look through it. The HUD uses a reflection HOE to not only reflect the light from the instruments which are pointed upwards (the reference beam, although not a point source) toward the pilot, but also magnify them, so that the pilot's eyes are focussed on something many meters in front of him, with billboard sized dials out there also. This is one of the problems of multi-wavelength recording, and it does lend to dimming of the image. Nevertheless it does work!

Yes, that is plenty of detail. In fact very good.

Here is what I now presume happened with your transmission hologram. The object or plate moved.

In your reflection hologram the plate is leaning against the object. So the reference beam hits the plate and goes through the plate and hit the object. This light then bounces off the object and goes back to the plate interfering with the now arriving reference beam at the plate making the hologram. So in essence part of you reference beam is reference (that which interferes with the object beam) and part of your reference beam is your object beam (that which passes through the plate, bounces off the object and comes back to the plate). The stability requirement is the plate relative to the object. Because the plate is leaning against the plate, relative movement between the two is very diminished. It is the most stable set up, particularly without a vibration isolation table.

Now in your transmission set up the object and the plate are not touching. In this set up the object and plate are separated by a distance and each individually mounted on wood. To make a hologram the two beams again must stay stable to one another, in fractions of microns. Coupled with the fact that you are only using a mouse pad for dampening and the fact that your exposures are 10 seconds long, I would be real surprised if you get a transmission hologram with this set up. If you are insistent on getting a transmission hologram, proceed with the reflection design in mind. That is, take the object and secure it to the piece of wood, front facing to the right relative to the laser. Now take your plate and lean it against the back side of the object with your support ledge (pop sickle stick) behind it all and at an angle to the laser such that the laser is lighting part of the object (hopefully some of the object that can be seen by the plate) and the plate all from the same side. Again, what you are ultimately trying for is to get the plate to lean against the object.

Three ways to find the emulsion side of a glass plate:

> ok so can someone explain Brewster's angle to me?

I'll give the simple, useful explanation, instead of the fully accurate one. In your case, if the reference beam is at an angle of 56 degrees to the normal at the plate, and the polarization is correct, all the light will enter the plate and none of it will reflect off it. Get a protractor and cut a wedge of paper with a 34 degree angle on it. Use this to make the angle between the plate and the light 34 degrees (which is 56 degrees to the normal). Rotate the laser and watch the reflected beam on the wall or a card. It will get nearly totally dim as you get the angle and the polarization correct.

This is the situation you would like to achieve when making holograms - it minimizes reflections from the front and back surfaces of the plate, which can show up as 'woodgrain' on the finished hologram.
