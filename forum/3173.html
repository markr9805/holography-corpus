BobH Sat Mar 05, 2005 6:14 am #17
> Also, if it is something you don't mind disclosing, how would you differ the design of the table, 4 foot by 8 foot?

My tried and true table was, from the bottom up:

a) four cinder block legs, stacked with the holes pointing and three layers of two 7.5x15" blocks each,

b) 24x24x3/4" plywood sheets on top of each leg, with an air escape hole in the center of each,

c) a fork lift inner tube on each leg with the valve stems removed and extension hoses connected, passing through the air escape holes,

d) the hoses from two legs on one end connected to a "tee" along with a third piece of hose having a valve on its end,

e) extension hoses as above on each of the other two inner tubes,

f) a 4x8' piece of 3/4" plywood,

g) a layer of 7.5x15" cinder blocks, rubbed to remove burrs and laid out interlacing with the holes pointing parallel to the short side,

h) 7.5x96" pieces of lumber, drilled to allow 5/8" threaded rod to pass through the cinder blocks, which squeeze the blocks together by way of large fender washers and nuts,

i) a layer of "Fixall" patching material (from the hardware store) spread out with a tiling trowell,

j) a sheet of plastic laid over the wet "Fixalll", and finally

k) a 4x8' Newport breadboard.

The plywood base for the cinder block layer was supported to keep it flat and level while the blocks were laid out, so that they would also be flat when cinched together by the threaded rods. After the top was constructed, the inner tubes were inflated to raise the top off its temporary supports. I used the sheet of plastic between the "Fixall" and breadboard to prevent adhesion (so I could take the whole thing apart to move). I still have the breadboard (after 23 years) and the rest of the table cost was about $400.

BobH Sat Mar 05, 2005 7:07 pm #24
The first time I built a table, I had all the valve stems moved to the outside diameter surface of the inner tubes at a tire shop. For the second one, I used threaded-end hose extensions I bought from a specialized hose shop here in Santa Cruz. I screwed them on tightly, and put epoxy around the joint (just in case). This worked well, so I repeated it on tables 3 to 6.

For the seventh table, I delegated the task to someone else helping me set up the lab, and he used tire patching cement between the valve stem and a hose that fit over its outside diameter. This table only had three legs, and one of them failed. He didn't know that I was building the table with only three legs, however, so he made up four inner tubes and I had a spare to put in immediately. Seems to be a couple of lessons there!

I strongly recommend against the use of all inner tubes connected together. If you use six inner tubes, I'd connect them together as follows:

BobH Tue Mar 15, 2005 9:29 pm #53
A consideration to keep in mind when making H2s from reflection masters is the "burn" pattern of the master. Reflection holograms made with the image close to the recording plane show a variation of efficiency as a function of reflectivity of the object and its proximity to the film. The transfer made from these masters will show this variation, in addition to the "burn" pattern caused by the image being in the plane of the H2, as a "noise" pattern floating above (and close to) the final hologram. In my opinion, this is the biggest reason to avoid this geometry, despite the large viewing zone associated with it.
