Most of what follows applies to Slavich and old Agfa film. But there may be still circumstances where you may want the new Fuji film with the contactant on the emulsion surface.

Some years ago while working with Agfa film I found that if you held the unexposed film in place on a glass sheet by putting lamp oil/white spirit on the emulsion side , it made a significant difference to the final colour of a reflection H. if the liquid was “damp” or ultra dry. With the new Fuji it is a great asset to have gelatin on both sides because with the later Agfa and modern Slavich film the triacetate substrate forces you to have to put oil only on the emulsion side anyway or it curls off the glass and in any case it gives a nice result by eliminating the gelatin-air interface which “eliminates” any gelatin surface defects and of course the notorious wood-grain effect if you put your film in a dry glass sandwich. But if the oil

is now put on the back of the Fuji gelatin layer then whether it is damp or not is not relevant but in the past with Agfa film

I found it helpful to actually use this damp/dry effect to tune the colour (using the rehalogenating bleach system.)

1) Dry System:

So to avoid a final small blue shift (ie. shrinkage effect) I made my lamp oil/white spirit ultra dry by keeping it in a well stoppered bottle over well-desiccated silica gel crystals . (ie. silica gel that had been roasted at ~ 200C) . I also used to hot blow the unexposed emulsion before applying the dry lamp oil/white spirit, and gave the emulsion 30 mins against the glass-liquid interface to equilibrate. This resulted in no final shrinkage and a close replay to the original laser wavelength, provided you used the metol –ascorbic acid developer with NO sodium sulfite. Followed by Ferric EDTA bleach. (The Copper sulfate/bromide bleach was actually inclined to cause replay at a slightly longer wavelength than the original but its diffraction efficiency was lower than that from using Ferric EDTA system.)

2) Damp system

If on the other hand you want slight shrinkage in the final colour then you can have 100% water saturated lamp oil/white spirit.

Store your lamp oil/white spirit with some free water as a bottom layer, shake it up and of course give it time to separate as the top layer(you only need do this once and it stays saturated from then on.), you must avoid having any actual water droplets in the oil layer of course.

Compared to the amount of actual water held in a gelatin film at 60% relative humidity, the effect of this very damp contactant liquid is only marginal

because the amount of actual water dissolved in it is only around 100 ppm.-much less than in the gelatin layer ;

however you can increase it dramatically by adding 10% acetone to your lamp oil/white spirit , thereby making it much less

hydrophobic . (Ethanol would probably work O.K. too, but it is only slightly soluble in lamp oil/white spirit and I did’nt try it.)

The curl is just due to the hydrophobic substrate of cellulose triacetate taking up hydrophobic solvent. (It will also respond to the hydrophobic part of the hydrophilic alcohol molecule). There has to be a very thin hydrophilic subbing layer ontop of the triacetate enabling it to "take" the gelatin coating but it is too thin and weak I think to have any effect on the "bi-metal strip " effect

which causes the curl. (A nice thing about cellulose triacetate is that you can fairly easily make its hydrophobic surface take a gelatin layer by first treating the surface briefly with a very concentrated solution of alkali eg NaOH in say 50/50 water ethanol).

Good grief! I had only been considering the first order effect of gelatin on one side of the film swelling or shrinking with humidity causing it to bow in or out (as in Jeff's bimetal strip analogy). Presumably having gelatin on both sides will help to cancel this, assuming there is no "memory effect" curl from the film being cut off a roll.

Now I see that with a volatile index matching fluid involved, there are several additional mechanisms to worry about at the oil/film interface:

- dry oil absorbs water from gelatin and/or film base, shrinking them

- wet oil adds water to gelatin & base, expanding them

- volatile oil components soak into gelatin & base, expanding them?

Oh well, as long as the film stays reasonably flat and tape is needed only to stabilize its position (and not to hold it down against its extreme desire to leap off the plate and roll up into a cylinder), I guess a small color shift is OK if it can be predicted and prevented or compensated for.
