As usual, the trick is to try it and then optimize the process. Making test exposures by varying their times and keeping development time constant to find the threshold and saturation of the material at the different wavelengths is essential.

I am curious as to why and how you are making the blue and red exposures simultaneously, and then the green afterwards?

A little bit of the history of the color lab at Lake Forest College: Around 1984 or so Hans Bjelkhagen and Venceslas Sainov, assisted by Steve Kupiec, who was still an undergrad, started working with the system with the translating mirrors, shooting on Sainov's Bulgarian Academy of Science plates. Probably the reason for the translating mirrors was because that was what was on hand in the lab. The blue and green light was provided by a single Spectra-Physics 166 Argon laser, with the wavelengths switched between exposures. I seem to recall that they were using 488 (a poor choice!) for the blue, probably because it was stronger than 476.

I "inherited" that system when I worked at LFC full-time, and around 1989 or '90 used it to make three successive exposures on DuPont's panchromatic polymer of a Martyushka doll that Denisyuk had given to my infant son, the first true-color exposures on that material.

I also used this system to shoot other three color holograms on a variety of materials. What was a surprise was that the Agfa 8E75HD could handle all three expo's (633, 515, and 476 nm). It could only do this in a two beam scheme; front reflection style reference, a back lit groundglass for the object beam with a transparency colored by RoscoLux filters. What were the best results I could get (don't forget, this was over 15 years ago and it was next to impossible to get Russian plates) was using Sainov's HP-490 (blue-green sensitivity) for the short wavelengths and Ilford or Agfa for the red, in a two plate sandwich. I developed the Bulgarian plates in CWC2, not its recommended colloidal stew.

TJ came up with a different way of combining the beams, using an HOE. I shot a transmission HOE with one beam normal to the plate and another one at 45 degrees from the normal at 515. When an undiverged 515 beam was incident at 45 degrees, it would come out along the normal, and so would 476 and 633 if their angle of incidence were properly positioned. (These angles, as they say in the textbooks, are left as a problem for the reader. As it turns outyou can figure them out with the math I have written so far in the Holo-Wiki)

This set up was described in a paper by TJ and myself entitled "HOE for Holography", but since I am at work I don't know where it was published. A photo of the beam combining HOE in action graced the cover of Laser Focus World! It's pretty cool, with the RGB beams incident at their appropriate angles on one side of the HOE, and the white beam output on the other!

Then when I left there, Hans resumed these experiments that Joe had witnessed probably in the mid to late '90's. I kept bugging Hans that there was too much flakiness with the 3 mirrors, but there was "no money" to get the dichroics. But there is no doubt in my mind that if you are working with three separate beams the dichroics are the only way that makes sense.

Uli Fri Jul 21, 2006 11:17 am #15
Hi everybody

This is a very interesting subject. I have never tried a white light beam for recording a colour hologram on panchromatic emulsion. I could see Hans Bjelkhagen back in 1995 working with PFG-03C plates in LF using the sequential recording, and when I first started trying experimental BPan plates in 2003 I used a very similar setup for my tests. The main reason for this is the different powers of the three lasers. I have always used HE-Ne, 532 Yag and He-Cd for recording because those were the lasers available when working at University and now at Colourholographics. I do not know if they make the best combination for colour reproduction, but I think they work pretty well. The He-Ne I am using now is a small 5mW unpolarized, that expanded gives 2 microW power. Optimum exposure time for this colour is about 60 secs. If I was to use this laser combined with the others to get a white beam, I would need to reduce the power of the He-Cd and the green lasers about 4 times in order to get the best colour balance I have got so far. Reducing the power for the 532 laser is not a big practical problem, since it can be regulated. But for the blue you need to use some sort of variable optical attenuator that normally affects the beam shape and quality. And we must not forget that all three lasers need to be filtered through the one and only 12.5 micron pinhole (I am using a 40x microscope objective as beam expander).

The next thing to consider is the different spatial frequencies of the three holograms that have to be recorded in a Denisyuk configuration: the red structure is about 4500 to 5000 l/mm (two fringes separation is around 200 nm), the green is about 6000 l/mm (170 nm between fringes), and the blue about 7000 l/mm (140 nm). The higher you go with spatial frequencies the worst it is for the stability requirements of your system. By this I mean that you must keep your recording times for the higher frequencies as low as you can, i.e. you must use all the power you can get from your blue and green lasers. Ok, you are using a longer exposure time if you add all the individual times of the sequential setup together, but you are reducing the risk of a stability problem by taking down the exposure times of the higher spatial frequency recordings.

On the other hand, adjusting the colour balance using the three folding mirror setup, playing with different energies for each colour, is as easy as changing the individual exposure times. You must move things on the table between exposures, but nothing is disturbing the path between the beam expander and the plate and object, and, if you work carefully, not hitting the table but sliding the mirror holders on the table for the shorter distance required to move it out of the way for the next colour, you only need to wait for 1 or 2 minutes between exposures. I use physical marks (metal pieces or magnetic bases) on the table for quick replacement of the mirrors, so I do not need to readjust the setup between holograms, just replace the mirrors, check the individual beams and then start again.

The other thing I found out recently (and Slavich people at the conference mentioned the same thing) is that, at least for our BBPan plates and Slavich film, blue must be left as the last exposure. If you do blue first the hologram is dimmer, and you can reduce your overall exposure times, than if you leave it as the last exposure. The reason I find for that is that all the emulsion is sensitive to blue (especially at 442 nm, since silver halide crystals have their natural absorption band near 400 nm), and doing blue first affects all the grains, even those sensitised for green and red. By exposing all of them together you can also have the same effect, since blue would be acting on all the grains from as if it was the first exposure. I must say that I have not tried it, I am just guessing.

So, even if you are making the hologram in two steps, with a red-blue beam and a green beam, I would suggest you to use the green first and then the red-blue.

And I thought about it for more than a while, until I realized that the effect is related to photography, rather than holography, and therefore it is a density effect, in fact a printout effect (for those interested in it this is a good link: [http://www.madsci.org/posts/archives/ap ... .Ch.r.html](http://www.madsci.org/posts/archives/apr2000/956162738.Ch.r.html)). On the other hand, the amount of energy you need to get the Herschel effect is much higher (between 10exp4 to 10exp6) than the exposure you try to bleach, which is not the case.

Comparing the resulting densities obtained after processing plates with the old exposure order BGR and the new one GRB, in both cases they appear to be the same (in fact, they were pitch black). The effect I found is that you needed slightly different exposure energies for the two different exposure orders for getting a well balanced colour image.

BGR order: B-240, G-90, R-120 microJ

GRB order: G-90, R-120, B-120 microJ

Lowering the blue in the first case did not decrease the density, but made an image with no or very little blue component, but the image was dimmer than with the second order. In this second order, GRB, the red laser is not showing any printout effect on the previous green exposure, being both at the same level in both exposure orders. So the only difference is with the blue. That is why my feeling is, as I have mentioned in a previous post, that the blue radiation is being absorbed by all the grains exposed to the blue interference pattern (being or not sensitised with the blue dye, since all grains are sensitive to a deep blue band around 400 nm), and those grains cannot be used by the other two (G and R) patterns, therefore reducing the population of silver that those two can use. This population is related to the refractive index modulation you can achieve with the plate, and finally to the diffraction efficiency of each of the colours. So, in a way, by exposing the plate with the blue laser in the first place you are killing it.

Anyway, good point about the Herschel effect. Maybe it can be used for some other application. I’ll think of it.

A few years ago I tried a three colour exposure on a BB640 (red sensitive). First I tried holographic reflection gratings (a Denisyuk of a flat mirror), and, using the BGR exposure order I got a three colour mirror with DE of about 25% for each wavelength (He-Cd, Verdi and He-Ne). Of course, due to the low sensitivity of the plates for green and blue I needed to make a strong exposure on both (especially the green, well over 1 mJ, but I was using a 8W laser although I never needed more than 400mJ output). After the gratings I went for the image trial, and I still have the hologram, dimmer and with more scatter than what you get with a BBPan, but visible and with good colour balance.

What I learned from that is that, although I have been told that spectral sensitizing for one specific band dramatically decreases the natural absorption band of the silver halide grains, with this emulsion the sensitivity is still there, although a blue sensitized plate is much more efficient for blue than a non sensitized plate. That meaning that all the grains of a pan plate are going to be blue sensitive. You then need to make your blue exposures at that point where the grain is mainly working with the sensitivity of the blue spectral sensitizer and not with the intrinsic sensitivity of the silver halide crystal. That is why getting a sharp, bright and with good colour balance image is most of the times so complicated.

Joe Farina Mon Aug 07, 2006 4:21 pm #31
Any dye that is not actually bound to the surface of the silver halide crystal is useless. In fact, it is harmful because it simply absorbs light.

How many dyes are in panchromatic silver halide emulsions for holography? I don't know. For PFG-03C, perhaps only two dyes are involved. If that is the case, I assume that each silver halide crystal has both types of dye molecule physically attached to it. I think it unlikely that it's a "mixture" of grains, some sensitive to one wavelength range, and some to another. But only Slavich knows for sure, I guess.

As Martin and Ed suggested, these questions become very complicated, and personally I wouldn't be too concerned with them. If you are using a material that you didn't make yourself, there will always be plenty of questions, and unknowns. I would just experiment with them, and hope that the manufacturer stays in business, and produces consistent emulsion. Because of these factors, I think there's a lot to be said for panchromatic DCG, even though it's slow.

By the way, in my tutorial-manual from "Color Holography" taught by Hans B. (1995), he states on page 15:

"The publication by Johnson et al. in regard to the decrease in diffraction efficiency of reconstructed images in multiple-exposure holograms can be considered. This effect is dependent on the time dealy between the exposures and is directly related to the lifetime of individual silver atoms in the latent-image prespecks. Due to the dissociation of single silver atoms the first exposure is favored. Subsequent exposures reinforce the signal of the previously recorded images, increasing the noise in later recordings. Therefore, the most critical recording (the blue) is selected to be the first exposure of the emulsion. The second is green and the last one is red exposure."

Is this the best method (or is it even true)? I don't know. Personally, I would favor simultaneous exposures and not worry about it.
