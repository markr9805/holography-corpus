BobH Mon May 08, 2006 3:31 am #2
I have a few comments!

1. I wouldn't recommend washing with water and/or alcohol only to see if that does the trick. If you're going to get the mirror wet, might as well apply some soap.

2. Be certain to thoroughly clean the sides and back of the mirror, without touching the front, before attacking the front. Otherwise, you will probably contaminate the cleaning fluid with particles that will scratch the coating.

3. I use way more soap dissolved in about a liter of water.

4. Use surgical cotton wads cut from a large roll instead of (what on earth is he thinking?!?) Kimwipes! Kimwipes have fine abrasive fibers in them that make them good for general no-lint cleaning. They will scratch the coating. Don't use cotton balls. They turn to mush and seperate in the soapy water. Get a big roll of surgical cotton at the pharmacy, and make a bunch of grapefruit sized wads before wetting the mirror.

5. Mount the mirror face up in a sink, so that it's off the bottom. Blast off loose dirt with water flow in the sink. Rinse with distilled water. Pour some of the soap solution onto the middle of the mirror. Wet a cotton wad, and use it to move the soap from the center straight to the edge, in a spoke like pattern, turning the wad to use a new face for each wipe. Let the weight of the wet cotton be the only force on the mirror. Add more soap and/or distilled water as needed. Use distilled water to avoid particles of rust from inside the pipes.

6. Use the last of the soap and the cotton wads to gently swish around on the surface. Rinse with a gallon of distilled water. Follow that with 99% isopropanol, and blow dry fast with a hair dryer. Touch up any residual marks with methanol on lens tissue.

7. You will get many more than three cleanings of your coatings if you don't use Kimwipes on 'em!

dave battin Tue May 09, 2006 2:47 pm #7
when cleaning large windows/mirrors i start by water washing the sides and back first with tap water, then lay mirror faceup under waterstream,using mild diswashing soap (Dawn has always worked well)

introduce the soap into the stream,keep adding stright soap fluid until you see the film cover the entire surface. if its a very bad build up i now use bare fingers to skim the surface (while keeping tap water /soap streaming),eventually turning to large cotton swabs,where you swipe once and turn over the cotton to a new surface, all the time the water is running i SLOWLY turn the water temp up a little so by the time its clean the water is ALMOST hot , now spill some iso alch insted of the water,

eventually spilling all iso alch to cover the entire surface, spill off alch and do a swipe with more cotton (as befor) and if you have warmed the glass with enough warm water, the surface should dry out quickly.

chances are you will scratch the surface while cleaning ,so you will have to make that call.

we used to regularly clean dilectric coated mirros using cotton swabs and a windex type product. a funny thing would be these small spots,impossible to remove using any product,acetone,MEK, windex,until you used some good ol spit, i guess saliva has an acid and it worked great!
JohnFP Wed May 10, 2006 10:19 am #8
For small mirror and dielectrics I use the roll method. I take a quality grade Q-tip and dip it in methanol, then start at one end of the mirror and while pulling it across the mirror with light pressure, roll the Q-tip such that if any particles were on the mirror the would be rolled up and off the wiping contact surface and not pulled across the mirror. This is very beneficial for sand box tables where the particle could be a very fine abrasive piece of sand. Make a single rotation of the Q-tip equal the distance your are cleaning.

I have also found that Acetone leave a residue behind. Not sure why. Maybe the "plain" acetone I used had some other ingredients in it, even though it stated nothing but acetone in the ingredients.

> I take a quality grade Q-tip and dip it in methanol

> I have also found that Acetone leave a residue behind. Not sure why.

If a plastic handled Q-tip is used, the plastic could be dissolved by the solvent. Acetone will certainly eat a plastic handle. Acetone leaves a residue because it evaporates too quickly. On small optics, use acetone first, then methanol to remove the residue.

jeff-blyth Sat Jun 03, 2006 5:35 pm #12
> Colin Kaminski: I put a disclaimer in about not using this method for dielectric mirrors. I however have never tried to clean a dielectric mirror. Will the same process work?

A word of warning here on dielectric treated surfaces, particularly bloomed lenses and anti-reflection sheets.

They typically use fluorides of light elements such as magnesium.

These have very low but still significant water solubility , getting a film of water on them can be quite bad so that a say 135nm MgF2 layer carefully coated to transmit say green could suddenly have patches only say 100nm or so because some of it has got washed away in water film so it then it works in the blue instead of green region. Evaporating acetone very fast in a cold damp room can produce significant water condensation. A hot hair drier should stop this from happening, I dont have a perfect answer, I am just pointing out something to be aware of.
