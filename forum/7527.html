Ed Wesly Tue Sep 23, 2008 8:38 pm #6
The first time I met Rudie was on my most incredible motorcycle trip in the summer of 1981, 5,000 miles in 6 weeks. I was staying with my pal Serge Honinow (are you still with us?) and we were delivering some sleeves in his family’s van (they took in sewing and added cuffs to hundreds of sleeves that were to be added to jackets at the place we were delivering to, so it was a crazy looking cargo!) and he came back into the van with this skinny punk guy in a dago tee and a cup of coffee and I was wondering who the heck was this cat and Serge said “Ed, I would like you to meet Rudie Berkhout.” So I immediately shook his hand.

Everyone had been telling me about Rudie, I had seen a slide of one of his pieces called “Milkshake”, a pulsed image of him being hit in the face with a spray of milk, (Freud would have had something to say about that!), and some postcards at the Museum of Holography, so he was on my list of people to see while I was on a busman’s holiday in NY (I got talked into helping set up a show at the MOH by Posy Jackson herself, driving around Manhattan in a big Chevy station wagon they had rented to pick up their S-P 125 with Ed Bush, Editor In Chief of holosphere, the advocate of holographic art, science, and technology at the wheel, going to the lumber yard to get 4’ by 8’ sheets of plywood that did lay flat in the back of the car) and now we were driving to his apartment!

So we got a buzz going, and he plopped on the turntable Einstein on the Beach by Phillip Glass and went around the room turning on the replay lights for the likes of 12 mW Boogie, Transfer 142, etc. Talk about being in holo-heaven!

So Rudie alternated with Sam Moree and Dan Schweitzer as being my top 3 holographic artists of all time! I think he was born in 1949, so he wasn’t all that old! What a shame! He still had plenty of great images left in him I am sure! But he had a hard life, sometimes eating only oatmeal as the only thing he could afford. I am so sad that I could never buy one of his artworks, all I have are the postcards published by the NY MOH, and Rudie’s Flower, a time lapse holographic stereogram printed by the Holographic Film Company.

Vita brevis, ars longa!
Jeffrey Fri Oct 31, 2008 9:54 pm #7
I'm sad. He was one of the finest - an artist with full technical grasp. He could still make me wonder "how'd he do that?" with his warped reference beams...

When I was working at RGB Holographics in New Hampshire, he came up for a couple days to try putting images on photopolymer. He was the only artist allowed in to work at all. The boss - who shall go unnamed - the only other human out in the lonely woods, did not know who he was, (imagine running a display holography company and not knowing that), did not trust him, lurked around, couldn't wouldn't participate or come in the same room, and Rudy finally said about the lurker "what's he doing here?" - making me realize that the guy really DIDN'T belong there at all. Ever. Well, that company is long forgotten, anyway. Management forgot that our purpose was to make full-color holograms and show them. More artists would have helped.

Rudie and I did get some great colors on various films, laminated to glass, but never a display piece - the boss would never buy film, but for free samples, so I really never accomplished anything for an entire year. A full-color holo lab, great equipment, I could have made a masterpiece a day, then printed posters, but no film. That was a long year - and Rudie fully understood my plight, bless him - I felt a bit better in my solitude. Then I left.
