Hans, you are going to find out that if you do not scrape away the emulsion from the edge of the glass your hologram will start to disappear over time from the outside in. Let me explain why.

If you looked at a cross section of the sealed hologram the way you explain it here is what you would see. First you will have glass, then emulsion, then the sealing agent then glass again. Having this edge of emulsion exposed to the air will be the downfall of the hologram. As gelatin is very hydroscopic, it will eventually absorb moisture from the air at this edge and pass the moisture eventually destroying the hologram.

I know of at least one person that used to seal their hologram in a similar manner and then shape the final hologram with a grinder. This left the emulsion edge exposed as I just described. Every one of the holograms I bought from this person have start to disappear from the edge in after only a year or so.

So my suggestion is to still clear emulsion from the edge of the hologram then seal it with the sealant you have found. UV epoxy works great but is outrageously expensive. If your resin does in fact work and is less expensive then it is a very exciting find.

Do you know what the index of refraction is for this resin? Do you have a link I can research. If the index of refraction is close to that of glass and gelatin, then it would work great for HOE's and Masters.

A DIY UV sealant

In keeping with the grand DIY philosophy of the Forum I thought I would put down some basic ingredients for making your own out of materials which are fundamentally cheap because of their big industrial use.

However before that a hypothesis that fits observations I have made.

I believe that the reason DCG has been so notoriously difficult to seal up

and prevent moisture getting in is not necessarily due to any fault of hydrophobic glues being somehow rather more moisture pervious than expected. I believe the real trouble has been that sandwiched between 2 glass sheets the gelatin layer contracts with age and builds up a significant vacuum. This results eventually in outside air getting through microcracks inspite of diligently thick glue having been applied around the edges of the sandwich.. This contraction effect might be just to do with the basic properties of the gelatin under prolonged lighting but it could well be more to do with the final stubborn traces of water /alcohol still hanging about and alcohol vapour can very gradually (we can be talking “years” here) make its way through the edge sealant increasing the vacuum effect. Anyway whatever the cause an obvious way to minimise it is to put the newly processed DCG in a really dry warmer I am not sure what temperature is best but 60-70C for as long 24 hours seems to work or alternatively I have left them in a really effective desiccator for a week. Then without giving the ultra dry DCG a chance to re-absorb ambient humidity a dry glass cover plate with dry sealant

can be put on. This topic has been discussed on the forum before and some of you guys have had vastly more experience than me at sealing.

To make a UV curable sealant,you need a monomer, crosslinker, and free radical generator for UV.

Monomer :

Methyl methacrylate or better (more hydrophobic) is butyl methacrylate (NB. Not tert-butyl methacrylate)

Crosslinker:

Ethylene dimethacrylate (alternative silly name by Sigma Aldrich is ethylene glycol dimethacrylate).

UV sensitizer (free radical generator in UV.):

DMPA or dimethoxyphenyl acetophenone.

One can use about 1 part DMPA to 100 parts monomer to 5-10 parts crosslinker.

It gets harder the more crosslinker you add of course. (~80-100% crosslinker just cracks up).

But the mix has initially a rather low viscosity , lower than the commercial stuff.

It is a good idea to store mixture over silica gel in a fridge in the dark.

Joe Farina Tue Feb 06, 2007 8:27 pm #18
All adhesives will no doubt absorb water over time, but this in itself should not pose any problem for sealing DCG. It's not the adhesive itself that really counts, but rather the quality of adhesion to glass. Of course, a water-resistant adhesive such as epoxy should be used (I plan to use another type of adhesive, but epoxy should probably do about as well from the standpoint of water-resistance through the bulk of the adhesive). But epoxy is not especially good at long-term adherence to glass, when the glass has not received any special treatment. It's deceptive, because the bond seems good at first, but over time (months or years) if often pulls away from the glass surface. Blyth has speculated that cracks may be formed (exactly where, he didn't say) due pressure-stresses caused by a vacuum being generated when DCG holograms are sealed using epoxy. However, epoxy-on-glass bonds will also fail without any such pressure. I believe the failure is simply caused due to the long-term effect of water vapor at the interface. This effect has been researched and documented in great detail, see for example Moisture-assisted crack growth at epoxy-glass interfaces, Journal of Materials Science 33 (1998) pages 4581-4588.

Joe Farina Thu Feb 08, 2007 4:01 pm #27
John, all I can say is that many years of experience with epoxy has made me nervous about it. It all depends on your needs. Both epoxy and UV adhesive, carefully applied without the use of silane, may hold indefinitely, if they are kept under normal indoor conditions.

The problem arises when the humidity, for some reason, goes up to about 80% or more, and stays there for a prolonged period (days or weeks or months). If silane is not used on the glass beforehand, I would expect failure in that case. It doesn't take "complete delamination" in the sense that the glass comes completely off. It just takes a harline crack, literally the width of a hair or smaller, to introduce mositure. Many years of experience with epoxy on glass has shown me that failure is highly irregular in nature, some areas adhering stongly to the glass with no sign of delamination, while other areas on the same piece showing tiny areas of delamination, visible by the different way light is transmitted through it.

If losing a hologram isn't a catastrophe for you, and you don't let the holograms stay above 80% humidity for days or weeks, then they should probably last a long time, perhaps even indefinitely in some cases, without silane preparation. But if you take a close look at glass conservation literature involving the use epoxy, the importance of the silane becomes apparent. Perhaps you are familar with the epoxy called Hxtal NYL-1. This is probably the most prestigious epoxy used in glass conservation (from the standpoint of permanence), and an important part of the Hxtal system is the use of a silane preparation for the glass. They wouldn't go though this hassle if it wasn't important. But actually it's not a hassle, just an extra step in the process. If we really care about the permanence of our DCG holograms, one extra step shouldn't be a big deal.

For reference, see:

Review of CCI research on epoxy resin adhesives for glass conseravtion, by Jane L. Down

Crack propagation in polymer adhesive/glass sandwich specimens, Journal of Adhesion (1995) Vol. 49, pages 97-112

Subcritical crack growth along epoxy/glass interfaces, J. Mater. Res., Vol. 7, No. 9, pages 2621-29 (1992)

A book called Conservation of Glass by Newman/Davidson (1989)

Also, there is a voluminous literature on silane coupling agents.

This is my std procedure for sealing MBDCG holograms, I have 3 yrs old holos and the are still beatiful):

1- Clean a clear glass plate same size (or bigger) of the holo plate

2- with a sharp knife, remove about 3-5mm of gelatine from edges of the holo plate

3- heat the glass plate (emulsion side) with hair dryer. This step must be done very carefully (slowly) to avoid humidity condensation into the emulsion (hologram rapidally desappears)

4- put both plates in a heated (~70C) oven for 10 minutes

5- remove plates form the oven and seal with epoxy glue. This operation must be done with hot plates.

6- put the sandwich into the oven (~50C) for 30 minutes.

I used the following glues: Araldite, Pattex, Saratoga with the same results

Joe Farina Fri Feb 09, 2007 1:32 pm #34
I would like to make a few last comments on this subject. The whole point is DCG hologram longevity, in particular what is meant by "longevity."

If a DCG hologram is kept in a normal, indoor, year-round living area, then the techniques described by John and Cristiano may do very well for many years, and, perhaps (as I said above), indefinitely. So in other words, if you keep the hologram with you, (essentially "living with it") in your house or apartment, then that is one set of circumstances. In colder enviroments (in which many of us live), indoor conditions can get very dry for long periods, due to central heating. (This is no problem, of course.) Things can get very damp too, but this is usually for brief periods, at least where I am. As I mentioned before, problems occur when the dampness lasts for days, weeks, or months. So under normal indoor conditions, this doesn't really happen, at least where I live. So, again, there is no real problem for the types of sealing techniques described by John and Cristiano.

However, there is something else which needs to be considered, if you intend to place your hologram into the hands of another person, or intend to sell one. Namely, conditions outside the "normal." Would you like to sell your hologram for a decent price and have to say "Oh, by the way, the hologram can't be allowed to stay above 80% RH for a month, or it will start to vanish?

Living conditions and storage conditions can be two utterly different things. In an unheated storage building, the humidity can easily go above 80% and stay there for a long time. I have a nice hologram by August Muth which was stored in such a building, and the edge started to delaminate irregularly, causing the edge to vanish. Luckily, I caught this in time, and brought it inside the house, and it didn't seem to deteriorate any further.

Or, think about storage in a damp basement, or in any environment where the humidity is not controlled. Holograms do no always hang on the wall. People may get tired of them, and put them into storage.

Or, think about other climates where it is very damp.

Do you guys think I like these complications? Do you think I enjoy worrying about this stuff? Not at all. It's just that I'm "serious" about sealing DCG holograms, for good reasons.
