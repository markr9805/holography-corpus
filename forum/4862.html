Ed Wesly Tue May 24, 2005 4:39 pm #4
Is the Agfa plain old 8E75 or 8E75HD, for High Definition? The HD came out about '79 or '80, and was a definite improvement over the non-HD when it came to reflection holos. If it's a box of 20 in a plastic container then it's the HD series.

The 649F plates are a real collector's item! Leith and Upatnieks used them! I could never really get too bright of results with them. I think they are grainier than one would like. But the beauty of them was that they have an almost horizontal spectral curve (since they were spectroscopic plates!) so would be good for true color work. But definitely not reflection.

The other two Kodak products should have anti-halo backings, and so you're stuck with transmission holos. But that's OK, since the SO-173 emulsion on glass, the Type 120 plate, was pretty anemic in the reflection arena. But it was the prefered choice of the New york transmission holographers like Rudie Berkhout, Sam Moree, Dan Schweitzer for their multi-color rainbow work. They liked to process in D-19, fix, then bleach in Ferric Nitrate.

But the SO-253, like Agfa 10E75, is much maligned. You can get some decently bright holos using either of them with the Pyrochrome processing. (aka JD-1, not sure of its current availability. For the formula, check my ramblings on this site.)

The only caveat is that Kodak used a sensitizing dye that didn't cross into the deep red and NIR like Agfa, so you may have trouble with your 658 diode. But the SO-253 needed only 1/16 of the exposure of 8E75HD!

Too bad they are fogged. But it might be possible to erase them as an earlier thread had mentioned.

BTW, where did you discover these? Kodak quit making their holo products before Agfa, so this must have been some ancient lab!

Dinesh Thu Jun 09, 2005 2:54 pm #6
A couple of points, more physics tha chemistry:

Old plates may be slower. So you may need a higher exposure. Agfa used to quote 25 microJ/sq cm, I usually found more like 50. Older plates will probably need more. Also, you may want to warm the developer a little, say about 25 - 30C (80 - 90F).

When taking film out of a fridge allow them to stabilise. Firstly to let the film come up to room temperature and secondly, the emulsion gets dew and collects water so you need to allow the water to evaporate. Glass is particularly susceptible because glass is hydrophlic. In terms of getting it up to room temp, remember that as it warms, the rate of warming slows down. It may seem up to temp, but microscopically, it's still warming and expanding.
