Tony Wed Jan 10, 2007 12:46 pm #2
Here's a hand-waving explanation. The action of the lens is to align the wavefronts coming from all points across the beam so that they line up at the focus. The wider the beam, the more quickly these wavefronts become mis-aligned, as you move away from the focal point.

Consider a finite beam width with a finite 'spot size' at the focus. Actually, the spot size is a scaled copy of the beam profile. Now widen the beam. The additional light on the sides of the beam destructively interfere with the original focus profile to effectively make it smaller.

Tony Wed Jan 10, 2007 2:17 pm #4
In the case of the example you gave, the difference is that the focal points are at different locations, which track with the incoming angle. In fact, if you had something 2 miles away, it would appear as a very small dot.

And yes, it is interference. The action of the lens is to adjust the time of flight of the waves such that they only align at the focal point. Recall that the speed of light in glass is lower than in air. Light passing through the thicker central portion of the lens is delayed more by passing through more material with slower speed than light passing through the edges.
Ed Wesly Wed Jan 10, 2007 7:36 pm #5
The size of the pinhole is related to the size of the smallest spot that a lens can resolve. Resolution is related to f/#, and surprisingly, a low f/# gives better resolution than a high one! This might seem counter-intuitive to the old photographic rule of thumb wherein a larger f/# gives greater depth o' field, but it is true!

How to image this is by getting out your transparencies for making moire patterns and looking at how the interference fringes get smaller as the angle between the sheets gets bigger. The bigger angle between the interfering beams corresponds to a lower f/#.

When the extreme rays from the converging to a focus beam meet they will create an interference pattern. The size of the fringes gets smaller as the angle between these extreme rays gets bigger. The further from the center these edge rays are, the lower the f/# the lens is working at.

By filling up more of the objective with laser beam, it works at a lower f/#. So with that wide beam of yours, the spot size gets smaller at the focus compared to an under-filling beam.

But as usual, try all your different pinholes to see which one does get rid of the noise. Working with a 5 micron pinhole is no fun, so if you can get by with a 10 or 15, great!

For reference, see resolution in Optics Guide by Melles Griot, or Airy's disk in other optical references.

Joe Farina Thu Jan 11, 2007 1:23 pm #7
On the subject of spatial filters in general, they seem to be cumbersome, wasteful, and antiquated.

Losses can be huge if a microscope objective is used. If anyone cares about conserving laser light (and this would surely apply to almost all holographers), then a singlet (or single lens) would be vastly preferable to a lossy and often expensive microscope objective.

Personally I would like to see a spatial filter designed with the holographer in mind. My "ideal" spatial filter would include the following:

1) A good, simple mechanical design, using the screws from Thorlabs. This would be similar to the kinds of movement found on some of the simpler commercial spatial filters.

2) Most importantly, it should have the provision for using a variety of SMALL LENSES instead of objectives or mounted singlets. It appears that the singlets from Edmund are good, but there are only a few focal lengths available.

3) Although a luxury, it would be fantastic to include two piezos (for fine-tuning of the up-and-down and side-to-side movement of the pinhole) in addition to the screws. (I don't think a third one would be needed for the back-and-forth movement of the lens.) This should enable easy off-table use of the laser, with on-table use of the spatial filter.
