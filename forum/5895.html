Tony Wed Nov 22, 2006 8:40 pm #1
Of course, at least that's what I've figured out lately. I have been working on a project to do semi-lifelike 'underwater' holograms. My approach ahs been to use scultpure polymer clay covered with sand for a sea floor, and wrinkled aluminum foil for a sea surface affect. Originally I tried to shoot the sea floor, sea surface, and critter all at once. I was having trouble getting enough illumination on the subject, and still get the desired effect of the sea floor and surface.

I decided to try shooting two separate H1 masters - one of the sea floor and surface, another of the critter. Each could be lit and exposed optimally. Then I copied them both at once onto the H2. This worked out really well. The first, a Dolphin, has the H1's sandwiched together:

![Image](http://home.comcast.net/~stampydoo/DSC_8357b.jpg)

This worked out well so I tried it with another, using the same 'background' and an H1 of a spotted eagle ray. For the copy, I spaced the two H1's apart a bit to optimize placement:

![Image](http://home.comcast.net/~stampydoo/DSC_8354b.jpg)

details:

35 mW HeNe laser, BB-640 plates for H1 and H2, JD-4 developer, Fe EDTA bleach, H2's presoak w/ 3% TEA.

Tony Wed Nov 22, 2006 11:52 pm #4
OK, here's the part that involves thinking...

I figured that other than doubling the noise from having 2 H1's, it was about the same as having a single H1, except of course each H1 had been individually optimized.

But when deciding if it should be done in a single exposure with both H1s, or two exposures with each H1 alone, I figured it was better to do it in a single exposure. My reasoning was that this way there was only a single reference beam exposure instead of two, and this would better use the available dynamic range of the film.

If you expose twice, then each exposure can only use part of the total dynamic range, so as to leave some for the other.

N'est ce pas?

BobH Thu Nov 23, 2006 12:38 am #6
I did my "Rasta Dream" image (previously pictured in the home page mosaic) in a similar manner. The first H1 was of the top lit bust in the foreground, and a back-lit transparency of my thumbprint in the background. The arch shaped lens above the bust was glued to a large piece of glass, AR coated on both sides, and it was covered with black tape to block the light from the background image.

The second H1 was also of the bust, but this time the lens was uncovered and the plate it was mounted on was covered with black material. A moire pattern was then projected onto a diffuser behind the lens. I wanted the random and personal pattern of the fingerprint to contrast with the ordered and generalized pattern seen through the lens.

Both H1s were 30x40cm Agfa plates, with their emulsions facing each other and index matched with Decalin. There is a very small "bullseye" pattern under his chin, caused by the overlap of both bust images, but it's hardly noticeable. Fifteen transfers were made, fourteen sold and I have the first (the one pictured). Like many of my images, it's signed and dated holographically, in space and below the viewing zone.

> BobH: Like many of my images, it's signed and dated holographically

Good. I would also like to see holograms numbered. For example, "BobH, 1 of 15, 2006."

Everything else equal, the value of each hologram will tend to go up as the quantity goes down, so if there are only 15 in existence, then that's something very different from an unspecified number.

Artists have been accused of being "greedy" by creating limited editions. What rubbish. As if everyone resents the "wealth" of artists! Really, it's a simple question of being able to produce art, or not being able. If someone wants to work with holography as an artistic medium, then I think limited editions are the way to go.
