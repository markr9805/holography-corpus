Hi all,

I've been following progress in this forum, particularly Bill Jensen's SSY projects and Ron's latest MOPA work. I like the idea of Bill's 'for the masses' approach and wish him all success with it.

I have SSY1 and 'tank' ruby laser units which I have been playing with (off and on) for some years now. I find things like this to be a source of much fun and new ideas.

I have several different laser projects on the go, but I am currently embroiled in a capacitor charger project which I am building into a 19" rack. Its not a laser in itself but it will let me make progress in that direction

I am very intersted in laser materials, particularly the discovery of 'cheap' ones which may include household or readily available chemical compounds. There are a great many materials which support laser gain - many more than are ever going to be used for this purpose or probably even checked. Only a few might be considered efficient or practical and only those which are considered 'miraculous' will be treated in physics papers or produced for laser use. Unfortunately they also cost $$$$$$.

I'm not that interested in the latest super-material grown in a lab. I have a few small samples of some of these materials to play with but I cant afford to keep sourcing new ones. I'm happy to look for cheap materials such as dyes and polymers which support laser gain and can be readily adapted into a laser for pulsed operation.

I'd like to build a pulsed laser capable of producing a recognisable beam profile, using common dyes, organic compounds, plastics or whatever might be made to work. If this can be done, it would bring down the cost of producing a device which otherwise depends on YAG/Ruby or other materials with a volatile pricetag, which tend to be damaged if they are going cheap, and which can't be repaired for less than they can be bought new. Even if the cost of building such a device remains high, it does make the prospect more accessible to more people in terms of component availability.

One of the best example of this kind of 'scratch build' work can be found here. A pulsed nitrogen laser which runs off electrostatic power (i.e. rubbing plastic together). A much simpler problem perhaps, but this device works and he is able to lase dyes with it. You won't be making holograms with that beam profile (!), but the fact that it does anything at all, and is made from those materials is just stunning.

[http://www.jarrodkinsey.com/pages/laserpower.html](http://www.jarrodkinsey.com/pages/laserpower.html)

I should mention that he has built several of these, and has run them off real power supplies and electrostatic machines. His main area is electrostatic generators. The laser was just a fun app.

I'm not saying that building a pulsed laser for holography using only what you find in your garage is easy, practical or even possible but it does appear to be a relatively unexplored hole. Look for a list of laser gain materials and you'll find the usual stuff and perhaps a mere mention of 'jello' and 'vodka' lasers - but nothing you could likely duplicate.

Anyway one of my projects is an attempt to get flouescent acrylic to lase using a strong pulsed UV source, with various hacks which help to reduce losses due to homebrew build quality. It will be interesting to see if the device will work at all, or if one of _many_ known issues will get in the way.

I'm interested to know if anyone has lased materials other than your standard lab-grade crystals and the usual gasses? If you are interested in this kind of stuff please let me know.

Doug.

According to A. E. Siegman in his book, "Lasers", A. L. Schawlow (who got a Nobel Prize for his more mainstream laser work) put fluorescein dye in Knox gelatin and pumped it with a nitrogen laser. After observing laser emission from this medium, he ate it. It is my understanding that Dr. Schawlow is alive today despite this activity. (That was in 1998; he died of unrelated causes in 1999. --- Sam.)
