Joe Farina Thu May 04, 2006 1:34 pm #1
Being nearly done with my new coating station for DCG, I thought I would ask other DCG holographers how they exclude dust. My approach was to use a large glove-box (plexiglas) which circulates hepa-filtered air from one end to the other (through a duct on the exterior of the enclosure). The whole thing is sealed off. Supposedly this can achieve Class 10.

I guess there is another approach, for lack of a better term I will call it "positive pressure" where the enclosure is somewhat open to the front, but hepa-filtered air is constantly being pushed out. So supposedly, the interior stays dust-free. Honestly, I don't know what is meant by "laminar flow" (is it any different from what I call positive pressure?).

The main practical difficulty with DCG (as I see it) is that coatings need to made in an essentially dust-free environment (that is, for high-quality, "finished" holograms, not just tests). The construction of my coating station dragged on for months, but maybe it was over-designed. It would be nice if someone could design a simple yet effective coating station.

Jeff Blyth tells me his new paper on 532nm-sensitive DCG will be coming out in July, which may help to make DCG holography more accessible than ever.
Colin Kaminski Thu May 04, 2006 1:49 pm #2
Joe, I have a laminar flow station. The entire back wall is a 3x6' hepa filter. The filter medium goes all the way to the edges. The sides and top go all the way out to the front edge of the table. The fan blows from a prefilter up the back of the filter and out to the front.

If you take a candle and check the air flow, there is no difference in air speed from one place to another. I have done tests where I sanitize the entire internal area and then place petri dishes with growth medium on the table. If the fan is off in 15 minutes the dishes all culture airborne bacteria. If the fan is on I show no colonies after 24 hours of exposure.

Laminer flow simply means that there are no eddies in the air flow. Eddies tend to trap dust and bacteria. A candle is an easy way to look for eddies. A better way is to dip a bent piece of nichrome wire into a food grade oil to pick up a little oil and power it up. It will make a streem of smoke like in a wind tunnel.

As a science fair project you can make a small laminar flow wind tunnel with a few cases of straws and a fan. Make sure to buy straws that are un-wrapped and then you onle have to cut the ends of the box off. Forcing the air through long small tubes will make the flow laminar. The has to do the Renold's Number. If you search for Renold's Number in Wikipedia I bet there is lots of good information.

Another use for Renold's Number is making turblent flow in laser cooling aplications...

JohnFP Fri May 05, 2006 9:48 am #4
Joe, I too built, what I thought to be a laminar flow station, but I was corrected. I am not sure if it is truely laminar or not but it works quite well. I would have posted this yesterday but my web site was down.

The top heppa filter is the type you buy for a furnace. It is about 20x30". I use this to capture most of the dust and is changed very easily whereas the two inter filters are fixed in place. The two heppa filters sandwiched in the upper compartment are the hollow cylinder type found in air purifiers (both available and off the shelf at Home Depot or other hardware store). The fan is a very strong 120V computer type fan. It moves the air nicely. The air is pulled in the top throgh the first filter, through the hole by the fan. It is then pushed through the sides of the two ring filters and down through holes in the center of the two ring filters. This air then goes into a compartment that opens to the back of the work area as seen in the side view. The back of the work area has a plastic grid (florescent fixture grate) covered with nylon screen to diffuse and help get a more even flow through the work area. Then entire unit is made from the white lenoleum coated shelving (also found in Home Depot). It is very hard, not porus and cleans easily.

I have used a cigar to test the flow and I do get nice postitive flow. If there are eddies they are not where I work. I turn the unit on a few minutes before I begin my work, wipe it down and let it run a few more minutes before I begin.

Colin Kaminski Fri May 05, 2006 1:48 pm #6
John's station could easily be made laminar flow if the screen was designed correctly. If you look at this page:

[http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reynolds_number](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reynolds_number)

"vs - mean fluid velocity,

L - characteristic length (equal to diameter 2r if a cross-section is circular),

μ - (absolute) dynamic fluid viscosity,

ν - kinematic fluid viscosity: ν = μ / ρ,

ρ - fluid density.

Laminar flow occurs at low Reynolds numbers, where viscous forces are dominant, and is characterized by smooth, constant fluid motion, while turbulent flow, on the other hand, occurs at high Reynolds numbers and is dominated by inertial forces, producing random eddies, vortices and other flow fluctuations.

The transition between laminar and turbulent flow is often indicated by a critical Reynolds number (Recrit), which depends on the exact flow configuration and must be determined experimentally. Within a certain range around this point there is a region of gradual transition where the flow is neither fully laminar nor fully turbulent, and predictions of fluid behaviour can be difficult. For example, within circular pipes the critical Reynolds number is generally accepted to be 2300, where the Reynolds number is based on the pipe diameter and the mean velocity vs within the pipe, but engineers will avoid any pipe configuration that falls within the range of Reynolds numbers from about 2000 to 4000 to ensure that the flow is either laminar or turbulent."

We can change the fluid velocity and the "characteristic pipe length" of the screen. Since this has more to do with the radius than the length a piece of 1" surplus honeycomb would likely do. But, I would highly recommend playing with the equation to see.
