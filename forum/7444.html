I'm posting another rant in an effort to explain my plans for suspending what will surely be a heavy load of concrete pavers on some small inner tubes without fear of soft or hard spots due to differences in pressure. My central tire pressure plan will also allow me to bleed the air and settle the table on blocks if it's not to be used for a period (another great insight per Colin), then be able to re-inflate the whole rig from one single valve.

My thoughts initially went like this: I'll need some sort of pressure rail - a distribution point. From there I can send air down flexible valve extenders to individual inner tubes. I figure the more inner tubes the better. Say (rough figure) the table with all the pavers weighs in at maybe 200lb... If I can arrange for that 200lb to be suspended by many inner tubes, then my (perhaps faulty) logic says that each individual tube will be supporting a small fraction of said weight, and therefore be actively suspending the weight at much lower pressure than would be the case for just a few tubes...

Right... so I'm visualizing distributing pressurized air... I'm a do-it-myself kinda cat, and a pressure rail strikes me as a simple-enough endevour: perhaps a short length of sch40 PVC pipe capped at the ends and drilled to accept a series of snap-in valve stems - one of them air in, the rest air out... Simple enough - but - ARG! there's no such animal as a female valve stem

That fact left me in a lurch for some time - the flexible valve extenders that were clearly such an integral part of my plan all had a nice threaded female end perfect for coupling firmly to the inner tubes, then of course terminated in a standard schrader valve male end with the valve core and all that.

So how in the holy heck do I couple that male end to my pressure rail? I considered cannibalizing a bunch of tire pumps for their hoses - it was the only place I could think of to find female valve couplings. Still unacceptable; A quick-release coupling strikes me as unreliable for holding pressure over a period of time.

Then inspiration in the form of common sense struck: Why not just remove the valve core from the male ends of my flexible lines and epoxy those mothers right into my pressure rails? YEAUH. My plan now is to fabricate one or two pressure rails from short lengths of sealed PVC pipe with the de-cored male ends of flexible lines glued straight in and a single snap-in valve stem to pressurize from. Chances are I will thread an extender onto the inlet valve to reach toward the floor and make charging the system easier with a single pump.

If I get a "flat", I can just drop the table onto the blocks I'll leave there (between tubes) and slide the blown tube out, replace it...

Yah? Any comments, my friends?

Jeffrey Weil Sun Jul 27, 2008 1:43 pm #6
Hello Justin,

Theres really no need for more than 4 tires. Its very counter intuitive but they will not get to a high pressure. Really.

I've made many tables and right now have a 1250 pound Newport. All on 4 tires. And small ones at that. They came from lawn mowers.

They are only about 15 inches in diameter so people are expecting them to be bulging on the edge of bursting. But, if you push your finger into the side of one you quickly realize that its under very very low pressure in fact.

So low that if you want to read the pressure no regular gauge would do. You have to use a syphmomanometer, a blood pressure gauge. You can get one from walmart or wherever for 30 bucks or so. So it has to be well under 4 or 5 pounds or a reg gauge would register some pressure. Thats low. You don't need the gauge though, inflate them so the table is just floating, not much more. You don't want it too bouncy. Don't worry if the pressure is not the same in the tires. Just that the height the table has been raised is about the same.

Colin is right about the distribution system, forget about that. If you put something heavy on one side of the table you want that tire to reach a higher pressure than the others to support that weight. The manifold would mess that action up.

Your right about using the valve extenders. Its a drag to have to lift up the table, remove the tire and fill it. Much easier and more exact to fill it in position. Somewhere in your town is a place that makes up hoses. They will have all the parts and hydraulic crimper's to attach the ends well. Down here in Miami my place is Amazon hose and rubber. I had my 1.5 foot extenders made up for just a few bucks each. Beautiful brass valves and connectors to the existing tire valves. Proper Goodyear rubber hoses. All pro crimped together. Well worth it. Don't go cheap on this, you'll just be leaking some air slowly and it'll be a pain.

Inside the tires center, the donut hole put a small piece of 2x4. That way if you release all the air the table won't crush the tire, its also a nice safety feature to keep anyones hands from getting crushed in case something unexpected happens.

If your ever here in Miami Beach your welcome to come to the lab and check it out if you like.

You don't really need to worry about the valve extenders. I'm not saying remove the existing valve from the tire. That's an importaint point. PVC hoses will require you to remove the valve stem and have it all sealed up. A proper extender leaves the valve stem in place and closed. Only when air pressure is put into the extender does a little pin come down and open the tire's valve to allow the air to come in. After that the valve closes. So if you have leaks in the extender it won't matter. Pressure will only be lost in the extender itself, not the tires and thats all that counts.

BobH is right about the air in the middle of the donut. His way around this is fine, all I did was run my extender tube under the innertube. That allowes it to breath too.

Forget your compressor and stuff like that to fill the tires too. Its not much air. I bought the cheapest smallest bicycle pump I could get. 10 bucks. Its so small that I can get under the table and use it in my hands with no problems. Works great.

Your weight will not be a problem. I have 312,5Lbs. on each tube, and thats not counting equipment. My plate holder alone is probably 50 pounds.

Hey everyone,

Yeah, you can put a few pieces of wood outside the inner tube to make it easy to get the tube out if you like. I always make a small stand about 8 inches less than the tables lower height and get a small sissor jack and another block of wood to go on the top of the jack to protect the bottom of the table. With this I can easly jack up the table and remove the inner tubes if I had to. It's also just good in general to have the ability to easily lift your table.

As far as adjusting the tubes its very easy. I pretty much never have to. I've used many of these systems were discussing. I had the manifold and pvc tubeing with the valves removed. And the rest of it too. I've also had a few pairs of proper automatic newport legs. After all that I'm using this simple set up. Anytime you remove the valve and replace it with a bunch of tubes, silicone and barbed fittings its going to slowly leak and your going to have to adjust it much more often. Even at this lower working pressure we use.

When I do need to adjust I just grab my little pump and fill up the tire I need. I usually have to do this 2 or 3 times a year. I'm doing resist, its very slow by any standard. My exposures are very long and almost every hologram is composed of many exposures. Usually 6 or more. Using this system I have not had a single bad exposure in 6 months on a final holo. My newest lab is only 6 months old. This works.

if you want to start cheap, don't get the extenders. Just build the stand to jack up the table and use the jack from your car. You should have the jack stand anyway. Its the safest way to lift the table and remove a leg for work or whatever. Use the tubes as is. And jack up the table to get to the tire to fill and dump it. If the system works, then go spend money on extenders. If you don't like it that way, try the manifold.

But, every lab is different and in my experience I've found that most things will work if you put in enough effort on it.

BobH Mon Jul 28, 2008 1:38 pm #21
I just think carpet adds instability in a thing being built for stability. Carpet beneath the inner tubes just smashes and does nothing. There's very little, if any, vibration coming through the floor and making it through the inner tubes that carpet will remove. There's no friction between wood and inner tube that will rub a hole into the inner tube over a lifetime. Just use nice, smooth pieces of wood.

Table builders often focus on vibration isolation coming through the legs instead of vibrations coming through the air, or air currents. Vibrations coming through the legs only increases the effective beam ratio. Air currents push the fringes around, which decreases DE much faster. Fringe movement caused by slow stress relief of a component, or instability of a component is usually waaaay more of a problem than vibrations coming through the floor. Also true with instability of the material in the plateholder.

I recommend a thick adhesive, like concrete patching mix such as Fix-All (available in hardware stores) as the interface between the layers. Use smooth pieces of wood, not something taken from the salvage pile at a demolition site. Rub the cinder blocks together to remove burrs on the edges and little rocks that sometime stick out on the top or bottom. Keep everything very clean as you assemble the table, to make the interfaces as good as possible. Don't go cheap on your main piece of equipment.

Nothing detrimental about carpet, unless it's used immediately underneath a metal skin (which I've seen suggested before). Without justification for it, I see no reason to include it. Gap filling interfaces in legs or table are better filled with something that hardens, in my opinion.

regarding air currents; enclosures inside enclosures, inside enclosures. Leave the room to open the shutter. Provide an isolated pathway for any laser or electronics cooling air so it doesn't contact the air around the exposure system. You don't want to work in still air, the laser likes moving air, moving air is necessary for thermal stability, you just have to isolate the air over the set-up very well during the exposure. Make your set of baffle cards, walls and covers very easy to put up and take apart for easy access. Treat them as the very important piece of equipment they are.
