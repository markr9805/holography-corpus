In an earlier thread, Tip Angle, Colin suggested that two papers concerning pseudo-color reflection holography by myself and Lon Moore be posted at the Forum. At an SPIE meeting in San Jose this past January, I spoke with Dr. Tung Jeong about copyrights for the Symposium material. While copyright is still maintained by the Holography Workshops, he has kindly permitted me to summarize the two papers that were presented a the International Symposium on Display Holography in 1982. Lon Moore has read my summary of his paper and I have incorporated his suggestions. Remember that these papers are from 1982 and that our techniques evolved. Materials today are also different from what we used (Agfa 8E75HD plates and PBQ and pyrochrome developing chemistries).

Summary ofPseuso-Color Reflection Holographyby Lon Moore (Summary by John Kaufman)Proceedings of the International Symposium on Display Holography, 1982

He describes a method for non-shifting multiple colors with both horizontal and vertical parallax in a three color image plane reflection hologram using a single wavelength of laser light. The emulsion is swelled with triethanolamine to different thicknesses before each exposure for each distinct color.

He explains how a red and gold hologram is made by first exposing an unswelled plate to red laser light. The plate is then treated in a 5% triethanolamine solution, dried and given a second exposure. It is processed to playback in the color of the laser used to make it (red).

He notes the difficulty of overlaying more than one exposure on a plate and retaining good image quality. Triethanolamine is noted as complicating the process by sensitizing the emulsion (more with greater concentrations) and creating problems of uneven swelling and streaking in the final image.

He describes the making of a three color hologram, “Squiggles” that anticipates the above problems. “The purpose of the original "Squiggles" approach was to reduce the variables to a minimum to allow for easy interpretation, and subsequent correction of the results,” notes Moore today. The image is of three intersecting, colored configurations of brass wire, which was chosen for its relatively simple wavefront. Transmission masters were made for each grouping of wire and were given the same general placement and illumination in relation to the film plane. This was to give the same exposure and beam ratios in the copy exposures. The first exposure was of an untreated master, the second and third ones had increasing concentrations of triethanolamine to swell the emulsion. Plates were soaked, “squeeged”, blow dried, cleaned on the glass side, and then reloaded and exposed. All exposures were the same time. Development and bleach were the “pyrochrome” process.

The resulting hologram showed colors of varying brightness (more triethanolamine made brighter images) and viewable at discreet illumination angles. Because the masters were very similar the exposures were easily adjusted. Steve McGrew (see “A Graphical Method for Calculating Pseudo-Color Hologram Reflection Geometries”) provided the method of calculating the reference angles to use on the copy plate to get all the colors to be viewed simultaneously. Trial and error gave the percentages of triethanolamine to use for each swelling (in this case 5% for gold, 10% green and 13% deep blue). He found that color results varied by method used to dry the emulsion and the time interval between bath and exposure.

He then discusses “triethanolamining” and getting “the touch” using a mounted windshield wiper blade. After a 30 second soak the plate is sqeegeed with very even pressure across the entire surface. He suggests practicing squeegee technique by taking a processed hologram that is green color and swelling it to another color in the triethanolamine bath (rinse the test hologram in water and practice again).

Moore suggests using a brighter than normal safelight so one can see better while squeegeeing. Starting with a 100 watt bulb, he adds layers of the desired gel and examines them with a diffraction grating until only the “safe “ wavelength is viewable.

A second hologram, “Still Life” is described, a three color image of an apple, pear and a pepper with much more information to be recorded and with different images located at different depths from the film plane. It was a problem of overlaying and registering separate images, controlling the reference beam intensities and ratios, and controlling color over a large image area.

To control the amount of information to resolve in the copy, the component images were not allowed to overlap any more than necessary. Each reference beam was concentrated only on the area of its particular object.

The colors and exposures of “Still Life” were determined by trial and error. The red apple was untreated and given x exposure, the yellow pear got a 5% triethalolamine solution and half the red exposure, while the green pepper was 10% solution and given ¼ the red exposure. Since reference beams varied in angle and intensity, final exposure times were given some “tweaking”.

Good registration was maintained by collimating the masters, using the correct reference angles for the desired colors and using plateholders with no play in them.

Moore’s final note is that his guidelines should give a running start to those interested in pseudo-color reflection holography and that he looks forward to seeing better imagery and a more manageable procedure.

A Summary ofPrevisualization and Pseudo-Color Image Plane Reflection Hologramsby John A. Kaufman (Summary by John Kaufman)Proceedings of the International Symposium on Dispaly Holography, 1982

The author begins by encouraging artists who are holographers to embrace technique as a way to extend artistic vocabulary. He confesses that these are trial and error processes and apply to the artist’s specific images.

Previsulaizaton-He explains the way previsualization works in photography as a way of envisioning the completed work and the technical steps to get there (Ansel Adams describes this in detail). In holography there are many decisions, and compromises that affect the image and previsualization allows one to think through the processes with the final image always in mind.

Steve McGrew is acknowledged as having presented a very organized description of the geometries used in pseudocolor reflection holography.

The Subject-Kaufman then discusses the first part of previsualizaion, the choice of subject. He sees the recording of a number of sets of fringes as taxing the ability of the emulsion to record so much information. Thus, one might choose subjects that have less information to record. Contrasty lit subjects have less information than diffusely lit ones. Holographers are encouraged to light subjects so as to minimize incoherent light exposing the plate because it increases noise; light a smaller area or use multiple object beams.

Polarization of the Laser-Workers are cautioned to consider the subject mount positioning (side or upright) and how this fits in with the polarization of the system. Object and reference beams polarized in the same direction will eliminate wood grain reflections in the finished image and it will use the laser light more efficiently.

Collimating the Master-Collimating the reference beam is discussed as helpful for successful registration of multiple masters in the final image. Care must be taken to avoid focused “hot” spots that overexpose the emulsion. Uncollimated light will have images with more “swing” distortion and poorer registration away from the image plane.

Bleaching the Master-Unbleached masters are desired for their low noise but the copy exposure times are often too long to be practical. Kaufman uses the term “vibed out “ to describe exposures ruined by vibration.

Object to Master and Master to Copy Distances-Choosing object to master and master to copy distances through previsualization is discussed. One affects the other in many respects. Registration is best at the image plane and then decreases away from the plane. So when a subject has depth, the copy image plane should be placed where it gives the greatest sense of being fully in register.

Also be aware that registration also decreases the further off center one views. This angle of view is controlled by the object to plate distance.

The wider the angle of view, the more information is recorded on the emulsion and this can lead overexposed, dim images.

In deciding the copy image plane, avoid putting the plate at focused hot spots that can overexpose areas.

It is noted that the choices of object to master and master to copy distances are interrelated and that previsualization helps one decide which choices best match the envisioned image.

The Hinge Point-Reference is made to Steve McGrew’s described hinge point. It is noted that master to copy distance changes the lens to plate distances of the multiple reference beams for pseudo-color and one should make sure it fits on the table.

Reference Beams for the Copying- Kaufman then turns to aperture control as a way to shape the light hitting the master in the copying phase. Reducing the vertical aperture of the plate reduces information being recorded allowing brighter images. It also means registration is simplified. The subject should not be higher than the height of the aperture; be aware.

The spread and position of the H2 reference beams can be modified to illuminate different parts of the plate for different exposures. By not illuminating the whole plate for each exposure and customizing it to the image of each master, one can conserve more laser power and often avoid too much exposure on parts of the plate. Brighter images.

Ratios for the Color Copy-Ratios are noted as being almost 1:1, just a little more reference, and some hot areas going 1:1.

Swelling the Copy Plate-He notes that pseudo-color reflection holography is made possible by developer/bleach combinations that can give a playback in red when shot in red. It is combined with an emulsion swelling process such as triethanolamine that can leave the emulsion at various thicknesses between exposures. When processed, the result is sets of fringes with different spacings that reflect different colors. The higher the concentration of triethanolamine , the cooler the final color will be (1.5% for orange, 17% for violet).

Plates were soaked for 10 min. at 68 degrees. A little photo-flo was put in the triethanolamine solution. A straight wiper blade was used to squeegee the plate. He recommends only one stroke but later workers use more than one and wipe the edges of the plate before squeegee.

Plates are dried with forced hot air but still-air drying can be used (do not wait too long to use once they are dry as the plates can lose sensitivity). Hot plates are allowed sit for an hour to return to normal temperature.

Exposure Times-Exposures are then discussed. Increasing concentrations of triethanolomine make the emulsion more sensitive. It is advised to pre-soak for a red component using water/photoflo as this will also sensitize the emulsion. Usually, though, exposures were from highest concentrations to the lowest.

Exposure times are through trial and error. He notes that when colors are not mixed, the exposure times are adjusted to make each as bright as possible. When particular color mixes are desired, one must use knowledge of additive color to decide which colors to mix. Also, the eye is more sensitive to some colors than others. The eye’s sensitivity to green means that less of it, maybe just a third the exposure, is necessary in many color mixes.

A Testing Procedure-Exposure times are trial and error tested. Each of the images is tested by itself for brightness, and color . A high development temperature is used for these tests. Exposure adjustments are made for color mixing proportions. Then a small scale test is made of the combined exposures. The full exposure time is used for each exposure and a normal development temperature is used. Development time is adjusted to give the optimum density.

Development and Bleach-Development is in D-19 with times from 1-3 min achieving a density that is nearly opaque. It is then bleached in PBQ bleach. Develop for the hotter, densest areas. Good previsualizaton should mean these areas will not be development problems.

While the discussion did not cover the aesthetic aspects of previsualization, it does overlap with the technical and the holographer is advised to merge them into a coherent act.

He includes a formula for PBQ bleach:

Part A:

Potassium Bromide 30 grams

Borax 1.5 grams

Potassium dichromate 2.0 grams

Water to make 500 ml.

Part B:

P-Benzoquinone 2.0 grams

Water to make 500 ml.

Mix parts A and B just before using

Sample angles for pseudo-color reflections were 40 degress from perpendicular for 633 nm red, 44.5 degrees for 580 nm yellow, 51.5 degrees for 520 nm green and 60 degrees for 470 nm blue.
