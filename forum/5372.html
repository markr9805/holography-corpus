My thought problem is as follows.

If I set up an interferometer I can adjust the angles of the legs such that I am perfectly overlapping to get the center of the bulls-eye. The fringes are quite large. Of course I can adjust one of the legs to go off center of the bulls-eye and the fringes get smaller as the angle difference between the two beams increases.

So starting at, let’s say, a 45 degree angle difference of the two beams (of course not in the interferometer) we get a transmission hologram of the object beam and this can be recorded in a phase material, like DCG. After we process we can illuminate the hologram with a reference beam at the same angle as the original reference beam and we get a diffracted object beam at the same angle (+180 degree) as the original object beam. We then start to decrease the angle difference between the two beams until we approach no angle difference between the beams. Each time we decrease the angle we put another plate in and record the fringes, process and illuminate.

What do we see as we approach the zero angle difference, not in the fringes, I know they are getting bigger, but in an illuminated phase recording material?

What happens when the first order approaches the reference beam when we illuminate the hologram? Will we get fringes of the through reference beam and the first order beam when the angle is very small and they slightly overlap?

Tony Fri Feb 10, 2006 11:53 am #3
As the angle gets smaller, the fringes get bigger. First, you start to have fewer fringes in the thickness of the emulsion. This reduces the intensity of the diffracted beam. Eventually, the fringes get bigger than the thickness of the emulsion. When the emulsion thickness becomes a very small fraction of a fringe, the diffracted beam intensity goes to zero. The thicker the emulsion, the smaller the angle that can still produce a diffracted beam.

No math, just an intuitive answer...

Here we go, running the numbers while I hold your hands! (Only in a figurative sense!) But it wouldn't be a bad idea to print out these two files to see the sign conventions:

[http://ocw.mit.edu/NR/rdonlyres/Media-A ... loeqns.pdf](http://ocw.mit.edu/NR/rdonlyres/Media-Arts-and-Sciences/MAS-450Holographic-ImagingSpring2003/48057442-047C-40C5-AEB0-709BD69FAE2D/0/thinholoeqns.pdf)

and

[http://ocw.mit.edu/NR/rdonlyres/Media-A ... 2/0/sf.pdf](http://ocw.mit.edu/NR/rdonlyres/Media-Arts-and-Sciences/MAS-450Holographic-ImagingSpring2003/E960608A-BDF0-4177-B2E5-8A27CB9AC702/0/sf.pdf)

Let's go through them, starting with your first example,

> 45 degree angle difference of the two beams
assuming 515 nm since you're an Argon user, with both beams symmetric to the normal of the holoplate.

First we find the spatial frequency of the grating by plugging into the Interference equation:

f = (sin theta 2 - sin theta 1)/lambda

where

f = spatial frequency in cycles per mm

thetas 1 and 2 = the angles of the interfering beams as measured from the normal

lambda = wavelength of recording

f = (sin 22.5 degrees - - sin 22.5 degrees)/.000515 millimeters

f = ( .3826 - -.3826)/.000515

f = (7652)/.00515

f = 1486.14 line pairs/mm

In Benton's sign convention, an angle above the normal is positive, an angle on the other side of the normal is negative, so that is why there is that double minus in the (). The spatial frequency units are usually in line pairs or cycles per millimeter, so the familiar 515 nanometers get converted to millimeters. Also note that 2 (sin 22.5 degrees) is not = sin 45 degrees

So we get a spatial frequency of 1486+ lines pairs per mm. This corresponds to a distance between fringes of 672 nm.

To see what comes out of this grating when one of the beams is incident at the recording angle, we plug and chug into the diffraction formula:

sin theta out = sin theta in + m lambda f

The only new term in this one is m, the order number, which we shall see becomes important a little later on. We will replay the hologram with the top beam in the Benton diagram, which has a negative angle. (I told you to print out that page above!)

sin theta out = sin theta (-22.5) + 1 .000515 mm 1486 lines pairs per mm

sin theta out = -.3826+ 1 .7652

sin theta out = .3826

theta out = 22.5

Which is what you should expect, since we are duplicating the recording geometry in this replay. If we replace m with 2 instead of 1 in the above, we end up with sin theta out being > 1, which is not allowed. (sines of angles are inbetween or equal to 1 and -1)

But if we start making the intra beam angles much smaller, as in your thought experiment, like 10 degrees above and below the normal, we get a fringe spacing of:

f = (sin 10 - sin -10)/.00515 mm

f = 674 lp/mm

Going back to the diffraction formula,

sin theta out = sin theta (-10) + 1 .000515 mm 674 lp/mm

sin theta out = -.1736 + 1 .000515 mm 674 lp/mm

sin theta out = -.1736 + .3472

sin theta out = .1736

theta out = 10 degrees

But here's where it gets interesting, when we replace m by 2 in the above and illuminate along the path of one of the recording beams:

sin theta out = sin theta -10 + 2 .000515 mm 674 lp/mm

sin theta out = -.1736 + .6944

sin theta out = .5208

theta out = 31.38 degrees

This is the origin of higher diffracted orders. If m = 3 in the above, we get a sin theta out > 1, so it won't work. But if the recording beams are even more closely packed, (smaller intrabeam angle) you can support a third order. This is what I meant by getting more than what you had bargained for.

Another (ab)use of these equations is to make the gratings in the arrangement described above, with the recording beams symmetric about the normal, but to replay the grating with a beam along the normal. Then sin thetain becomes 0, which can make life simpler.

For the 45 degree intrabeam angle

sin theta out = sin theta0+ 1 .000515 mm 1486 lines pairs per mm

sin theta out = 0+ 1 .7652

sin theta out = .7652

theta out = 50 degrees

and if -1 had been substituted for m, the order number, you would see the other beam at -50 degrees. There would be no secondary order, because then again we would have a sine of an angle > 1, a big no-no.

For the 20 degree intrabeam recording angle, (10 on either side of the normal), first order:

sin theta out = sin theta 0 + 1 .000515 mm 674 lp/mm

sin theta out = .3472

theta out = 20 degrees on either side of the normal

Second order (m = 2)

sin theta out = sin theta 0 + 2 .000515 mm 674 lp/mm

sin theta out = 2 .000515 mm 674 lp/mm

sin theta out = .6944

theta out* = 44 degrees, symmetric to the normal, no 3rd order.

With smaller intrabeam angles, or same geometries but bigger lambdas, you can get more orders. You can get giddy calculating these things!

So when does a hologram stop being a hologram? I don't know, maybe when you get too many orders of things on top of each other!

Other things to think about:

If you make the grating with other than a symmetric geometry, like an intrabeam angle of 45 degrees, but one beam along the normal and the other at 45 from the normal, the holoplate sees a different count of fringes along its side than in the symmetric arrangement.

A way of visualizing this is if you have a pair of transparencies of parallel lines to make a fringe system (moire pattern in the Op Art sense) and have a rectangle on a transparency to act as a holoplate in the fringe system, as you rotate the plate through the fringes at different angles you will see the spatial frequency increases as measured along the plate edge, even though the separation of the fringes remains the same!

Another by-product of the 45 degree/normal geometry is that even with 633 He-Ne lambda, no higher orders are generated. But with a 30 degree reference and an object along the normal, there is a -1 image symmetric with the reference beam being produced! I have a circa 1966 transmission holo that was a give away in a laser magazine, and some old Holex holograms with this geometry, and you can definitely see this spurious image!

Recording a grating with the unslanted geometries (i.e. 20 degree intrabeam angle, one along the normal, the other 20 degrees off) then starts invoking Bragg or thick grating equations, and the higher orders may or may not be generated.

> What do we see as we approach the zero angle difference, not in the fringes, I know they are getting bigger, but in an illuminated phase recording material?

Isn't that the lower limit to the capabilities of a particular material and/or processing scheme? Practical hologram recording materials have an upper resolution limit, and a lower. For emample, a processing scheme based on diffusion transfer will limit DE as you decrease the interbeam angle before the material itself can.

Regardless of material, however, you're asking if the recording of a single fringe (dark and light pair) is a hologram? I'd say no. It's a photograph. Two fringes? No. More than two? If they can diffract something, I'd say it's a hologram!

Tony wrote:

> If we assume the emulsion thickness is 8 microns, then with your first example, there would be 11.88 fringes in 8 microns. However, in your second example there would only be 5.39 fringes.

In the above case, the fringes are perpendicular to the emulsion, being an unslanted transmission grating. The fringes would extend throughout the thickness of the emulsion. Look at the picture in the Benton notes, "Calculating Spatial Frequency", linked above. Fringes are formed along the angle bisector of the two beams.

Of course if the plate were rotated 90 degrees in that figure then what you are describing would happen, and that would be a reflection hologram case.
