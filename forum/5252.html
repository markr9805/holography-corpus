I wanted to "bookmark" two papers that might be relevant to some of the current discussions.

The first one, by Kubota/Ose is in Applied Optics, Vol. 18, No. 5, pages 2538-9 (1979) called "Methods of increasing the sensitivity of methylene blue sensitized gelatin." They describe two methods for providing "extra" hardening for DCG after exposure and before processing. (They also use fixer w/hardener during processing.) The first method is delaying processing after exposure (i.e., allowing the dark reaction of DCG to operate). They found that the optimum delay time was several days. The second method involved heating the DCG after exposure (which seems to be a way to "accelerate" the natural dark reaction). They used a temperature of 60 C. for periods up to 6 hours. They also mentioned that once the temperature reached 90 C. the "reverse effect" occurs (presumably lowering diffraction efficiency). This paper relates specifically to dye-sensitized DCG (which is what I'm interested in) and although I've heard of the dark reaction (or heating) procedure being used for straight DCG (no dye), I haven't heard about these techniques being used with dye-sensitized (methylene blue) DCG.

The other one, by Mazakova and others, is called "Electron donors and heat treatment: Their effect on the diffraction efficiency of dichromated gelatin reflection holograms." (Applied Optics, Vo. 24., No. 14, pages 2156-60, July 1985) Some of the statements include: "...The reduction of intermediate Cr-ion complexes in solutions is enhanced by temperature...All our experiments show that heat treatment improves holographic efficiency." They varied temperatures and time, and reported the results.

Joe Farina Sun Dec 11, 2005 2:05 pm #3
I agree, John, the time needed for the thermal treatment is annoying.

Do you know about the optical hardening method?

I have a paper by Richard Rallison called "Notes and considerations for the dichromated gelatin (DCG) holographer."

Here is a quote:

"...Developing is simply a process of reducing the remaining chromate ions and thereby uniformly hardening the gelatin. Too much hardening will give a clean, clear but weak and green result. Too little hardening will leave the hologram milky and weak and reddish or yellowish. Development is done either optically or chemically using fluorescent or incandescent lamps or any suitable reducing agent or tanning solution."

The paper includes some examples of development times, either optically or chemically. These times are short, in the 5 to 15 second range. In the optical method, he suggests a 100 watt lamp held 6 inches from the emulsion.
