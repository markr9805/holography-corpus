Gary Deal Thu Feb 24, 2005 6:02 am #3
I had good results with the "tombstone on an inner tube" design, on the second floor of a busy apartment building (16 units)

Go to your local Kmart or similar and buy an automobile inner tube. Needs to be about two feet across while flat. Talk nice to the counterperson and go when they're not swamped, in the hopes that they'll cooperate. A cheap tube should be fine.

Elsewhere in the automotive department, find a valve stem tool with which to loosen and tighten the valve on the inner tube.

Go to the garden department and find a paving stone that's about two feet square. It needs to be one big one, not four small ones. You might have to work a little to find one, and you'll want a cart to put the thing in, it should be heavy(50-70 lbs). A two foot square is about the smallest I'd want to work in and you may have to check a few places to get one.

Buy the stuff and take it home.

Use the valve stem tool to take the valve out of the valve stem on the inner tube. Blow two or three lungfuls of air into it and replace the valve. Lay it on the floor and place the stone on it (flat). If I recall correctly, the tube should end up keeping the stone one or two inches off the floor. Add or subtract air as necessary.

Voila! You now have the basic self-damping mass isolation table. Poke one corner of the stone and see how long it takes to stop wobbling. Try adding or letting out some air to see if it will stabilize more quickly (flat doesn't count). Total cost should be under $20, possibly including beer.

You can build a box to put on top (use particle board and do "fitting" between the stone and box with old cotton clothes) and fill it with coarse (not beach) sand (~150 lbs), which will need to be washed in a bucket no matter how "washed" it's claimed to be. Fine grit is bad for your components. This will add to the mass and should make it more resistant to vibration.

You can also attach a steel plate and go with magnetic bases on your compoments.

Whatever you do, set up an interferometer first, and keep tweaking the assembly to get your settling times as short as possible. The one I made like this would go from total upset (from me jumping on the floor next to it a couple of times) to dead solid in under five seconds. However, the stuff poked into the sand needed time to settle and always had to be realigned. Repeatedly.

My first hologram was done in a tiny sandbox on a bicycle inner tube on top of this setup.

If that's not cheap enough for you, see if you can get an old innertube from a junkyard, and look around for somewhere somebody's tearing up some concrete that's only a couple inches thick (bring a wagon, concrete is heavy). Hose it off before you bring it in the house. Put a piece of old blanket between the concrete and tube to keep from scuffing a hole in it - this will probably slightly increase the isolation. For that matter, a cushion from an old couch might work, as long as the concrete doesn't smash it.

Somebody's using a steel plate on some bubble wrap and that's working for them, and I recently read of a university installation involving a large slab mounted on a massive pillar set into underlying bedrock that didn't work for them - sounds like they went for stability when they should have gone for insulation (isolation), they were next to a freeway!. The more vibration you have to start with, the more you'll have to work to eliminate it.

By all means, start reading through the wealth of posts here as well as the Network 54 archive, there's a LOT of information to be found. This remains an experimenter's field, and while there's plenty of methods and instructions, not everything always works the same for everyone.

-Gary

More can be used if they are connected so that the leveling is done at three points only, as with the Newport type systems. Dinesh Thu Feb 24, 2005 5:48 pm #11
The purpose of the air is to form a cushion to absorb any ground vibrations. The ability to absorb ground vibrations, the "elasticity" of the air mass if you will, depends, among other things, on the air pressure. If you have a closed system (no leak) then any large weight placed at one end of the table will increase the force of the air under the large weight and push air around the rest of the table thus also increasing the local pressure around the rest of the table, from the fact that P=F/A and assuming no change in the overall area, A. In addition, the table will tilt. Think of sitting on a large, inflated ball. The ball squashes where you sit on it and causes the sides of the ball to bulge out. This increase changes the absorption properties of the air mass. However, if you allow leakage, then the local pressure will accomodate any local changes to the rest of the table. Putting a large weight at one end will not cause pressure variations to the rest of the table. The distribution of pressure remains constant since the local change is pressure is accompanied by loss of air from the valve. Remeber also that air is not totally inextensible. There is a bulk modulus , m

m =(delta P/P)/(delta V/V)

If you have a reservoir, such as a tractor tyre, then I'd think the reservoir may accomodate local changes if the reservoir is large enough.
