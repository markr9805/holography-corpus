Hummmm....the shadow?

I guess you mean....

We have a tranmission hologram of a sphere, let's say about 12 inches away from the plate and off axis by 45 degree. Then when we shine light through the hologram at the recording angle and a conjugate of the original reference beam and from the pseudo side, we will see the image of the sphere 12 from the plate and 45 degrees off to the side. Of couse this reconstructed image will only focus one plane of the sphere on a flat screen but we are not worried with that.

So, what will the through light look like, the shadow? Well, think of a hologram as a recoding of thousands of tiny little lenses hypothetically arranged in an array on the film. Each focusing the reconstruction light to the place where the image wants to be formed. Subtract this from that part of the reconstruced light and that will be your shadow.

Not much to see with the eye but I bet if you got into the math and physics of it you could be able to arrive at something relating to a negative fourier transform of the object depending on the divergence/convergence of the through light...lol! (Don't ask on this laser part, I made it up).

It seems impossible to physically make a model of some of the 4D Polytopes. Well, that is, it can be made at one view, but cannot be made to alter it's fourth dimension. Is that true?

if that's true, to start... we need to make a computer generated stereogram. (I have always wanted to make a hologram of impossible objects, like optical illusions) At least then we can have a 3D image of the Polytope which can be viewed or projected onto a screen. In projecting on the screen we will only see a slice of the object, which is not yet quite the "shadow" we are looking for. The parts of the image not focused at the plane of the screen would be blurry. Somehow the depth of the focused image needs to be contracted or collapsed. This can be done by masking off all but a very small area of the hologram and illuminating it. It will lose the 3D and retain the image "shadow" from that particular perspective. By masking off different parts, different "shadows" of the polytope can be seen. Better yet, just illuminate part of the hologram with a narrow laser beam. As you move the laser beam up and down, the polytope "shadow" will rotate up and down and as you move the laser beam back and forth, the polytope "shadow" will rotate back and forth.

So I believe the end result can be done.
