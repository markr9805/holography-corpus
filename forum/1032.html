OK, thanks! Here are my "first impressions" on the kit...

Oh, here is a link to a short video of my very first hologram:

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QnyYqDF8mMY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QnyYqDF8mMY)

Note that I took it with a small camera and bad lighting, and it wasn't possible to get a good exposure or color balance. The hologram looks much better in real life.

Anyway, a small background: I have always been fascinated with holography (and lasers, for that matter). I started studying the physics of lasers when I was in high school, way before Laser diodes became available. So all my interest remained pretty much "theoretical" for a long time. The same happened with holography: I loved holograms, but I thought that the setup needed to make one was extremely complicated and expensive. That's why, when I saw the LitiHolo website, it almost seemed too good to be true: instant holography with a basic, cheap setup, and even no processing? Wow.

I also knew about the Integraf kit. They have larger plates, and regular (chemical) plate processing, which meant more work but seemed like a more "tested" process. Long story short, I decided to buy both; but I haven't actually used the Integraf kit yet so I'll only talk about the LitiHolo here.

To sum it up in two words: the kit does what it promises - it lets you create a real hologram of good quality in less than an hour. So: would I buy it again? Yes. But not all that glitters is gold, and there are some quite disappointing issues.

First of all: customer service. It's virtually non-existent.

Before committing to buy, I sent them a couple of emails, asking questions about shipment and a couple of technical questions. All of my emails were totally ignored.

After a few weeks, I sent another email asking, basically, "Are you still in business, and ready to ship the kit right away if I order it?" And to this message, they replied immediately, guaranteeing immediate order processing and shipment. So I went ahead and ordered the basic kit plus the "reflection upgrade kit". Due to a glitch with the order, it went through as two separate orders, with separate shipments. Again, I emailed them to check if it was possible to "merge" the orders so I could pay a single shipment. And again, my message was ignored. So I tried to actually call them on the phone. A very polite and friendly girl answered, and told me it could be done... but in the end they didn't, and I ended up paying double shipment anyway. Again, attempts to ask for the reason via email failed.

Anyway, the kits arrived. The box looked great: flashy and colorful and attractive-looking. Obviously targeting the general public rather than lab people. The Integraf kit by contrast was more "bare bones" - a simple white cardboard box. But the documentation inside the LitiHolo kit, though colorful and "solid", was much less interesting than what came with the Integraf kit (which was just a bunch of sheets of paper printed in b/w and stapled together, but much more detailed, with quite some theory and lots of experiments to try).

The kit comes with several plexiglass parts that need to be assembled (snap-fit, no glue required) to create the correct geometry for the exposure and display of the hologram. The very first thing I noticed, when trying the assembly, is that the actual material is way too brittle and delicate. Just trying to disassemble it the very first time, two of the "tabs" broke clean, making it impossible to assemble the "reflection tower" in a solid way without gluing it together. And a couple of days later, while moving the laser and battery holder, its main "rib" snapped in two. I didn't drop it: I just lifted it, and it broke. And a week later, also the plate holder suffered the same fate. Luckily, it's a material that can easily be glued with cyanoacrilate glue.

Anyway, I tried again to contact them, asking if they would sell me replacement parts for those that broke: do you think they answered my message? Right: they didn't.

Now, moving on to more "important" stuff: the laser. It's a simple, honest red diode. I don't know the exact power, but it's decent. It's not annoying if you glance at the beam while reproducing a hologram, but it produces very bright holograms. It comes with a small heat sink which is basically a metal "clip" that you clip onto the diode barrel. This heat sink isn't well thought out, because if you aren't extra careful, it's very easy to make it contact with the exposed components on the laser's pcb, creating a short circuit, or even physically damaging the circuit, since the spring of the clip is really strong and the "jaws" are pretty sharp. But I have to say that the diode seems quite stable even without the heat sink, at least in wintery temperatures.

The laser beam is very "clean" and "even", with only a negative side: with the geometry formed by the kit, it can't fully illuminate the whole holographic plate. A small strip at the top (or the bottom) will be less exposed than the rest. This is not a problem for the actual exposure, since these plates use a weird process where it's impossible to over-expose: you can just keep the laser on for longer, so even the top strip gets properly exposed. But the problem reappears when using the same laser to reproduce the hologram - a small strip will be darker than the rest.

Now, on to the plates. Physically they are made of a piece of glass, to which the actual holographic film is bonded in some way (not glued). The glass has a pretty rough cut; the edges are rough, and the cuts aren't always parallel or even straight. But this is a pretty minor problem, since even if they aren't perfectly aligned, the same mis-alignment will occur when reproducing it, so all will be well. But it just looks "unprofessional". After exposing the hologram, it's possible to "peel" the film off the glass, because it's not glued on. But of course if then you try to stick it back on, it's almost impossible to do it without getting air bubbles trapped between glass and film.

One downside of this is that even if you don't peel the film off, if you're not careful when handling the exposed plates, they can start to peel at the edges. So, no rough handling. This is especially true on some plates, where the film is very slightly larger than the glass, so that there is a small part of the film "protruding" over the glass. With these plates, it's very easy to inadvertently initiate the peeling.

The actual exposure is an amazing process. I don't know how it works, but it's extremely easy. You just set up all your geometry using the provided "fake" plate (which is a simple piece of glass without the holographic film) and, when everything is ready, you turn off the light, take a plate out of the box, put it in place of the fake plate, and remove the shutter. Then wait. I got good results with 3 minutes of exposure, but even overexposing up to 15 minutes nothing changed. Basically, the plate "feels" when the exposure is right, and after that point, the process ends; from then on, if you keep the laser on, you are basically just reproducing the hologram instead of exposing it. The very first hologram I made, using the provided car model, was an incredible experience: I was looking at the car through the plate, and when the timer beeped at the end of the exposure, I just went and removed the car, without turning off the laser. It looked exactly as if I had removed a "ghost" of the car, and the actual car was still there. The detail was amazing: even a few motes of dust that were on the roof of the car, were recorded and faithfully reproduced.

I think the tolerance to vibrations is very good. I didn't do any strict testing about this, but on my first reflection experiment, I had set it up with the plate resting on top of a bowl containing the objects to be exposed. When I actually started the exposure, I could see fringes moving on the plate. It turned out that the plate was slightly "shaking" on top of the bowl. I thought that the hologram was compromised, so after no more than 4-5 seconds, I moved the plate a little, looking for a more stable setup. Well... the hologram turned out anyway. Maybe not as bright or detailed as it could have been, but still much better than a total loss. I don't know if in those 4-5 seconds the exposure had already completed, or if the second part of the exposure (several minutes) "overshadowed" the first part; but anyway, I think it was pretty impressive, especially since I obviously introduced many vibrations while touching the plate and bowl.

So, to sum it up: this kit lets you make some pretty amazing holograms. Bright and detailed. Especially the transmission ones are really good, in my opinion. And for the price, I really think the kit is a good beginner's choice. It comes with clear (if a bit generic) instructions; it includes a small safety light to help when setting up for the exposure; it includes a cardboard shutter, a "fake" glass plate, and even a white piece of cardboard to test if the plate is evenly illuminated by the laser light. AND a small car model. You don't need to add anything except two batteries for the laser.

BUT - my main complaint is the non-existent customer service, both pre- and post-sale. The materials for the spacers and holders is way too brittle, and incapable of resisting normal handling. And some components, like the heat sink clip or the actual glass plates, look "rough" - a little more care to the details there would have helped, though these aren't show-stoppers.

I still have to make more experiments with exposure times and with different lasers; luckily I still have about 30 plates left, so I can afford to make some bad mistakes if needed! I'll report here any new findings...

Cristian

Excellent report! Thanks. The material they use is a photopolymer made by Bayer. It's stuck on by the stickiness of the material itself before exposure, then after the exposing light hardens it, it is easily peeled off the glass.
