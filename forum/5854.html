> I bought a He Ne Laser-632.8nm @ 10mW O/P Power., But the laser was 'randomly polarising'and i heard from books that randomly polarised laser wont yeild better results.I have polariser rotator with me. Does it will make the laser linearly polarised?

A random polarized laser just means the polarization is uncontrolled, so it can be in any (MULTIPLE?) rotations. A polarization rotator just rotates this, it doesn't clean it up. You really need a linearly polarized laser.

> Some time back i had bought the Integraphs Kit., with which i had tried out the Denisyuk Hologram with coins, but the result was not clear. (I believe it may be because of I used pipe water instead of distilled water!).

Tap water can cause a milky whitish deposit on the film due to reaction with the bleach. This can be gently removed with a paper towel when wet. You can also use bottled water which doesn't contain chlorine. There's a good chance the laser might be the cause of the fuzziness.

> Recently i heard from Color Holographic that plates need to be resensitised to get better results. Is it compulsory? Even the plates from Intgraph too needs it?

Resensitization BB-640 just increases the sensitivity. If you are getting a good density (around 2 for reflection holograms) you don't need to resensitize. You don't need to resensitize other types (you didn't say what type you got from Integraf).

> Now I am really confused., where to start and what to do? There are many designs avaliable in net for doing holography.Anybody please guide me the right way.

Start by making simple single beam reflection holograms until you get your exposure and developing techniques working well. Then you can try a transmission hologram.

> Also Integraph suggested some different combination for developing and Color Holographic advises us different one. Which one is right?

All the emulsions are different, and need different exposures and chemistries for processing. You should pick one and use it exclusively to reduce the number of variables going on. In the beginning I would recommend something inexpensive, like PFG-01 film and JD-2 chemistry.

Good luck!

> When we rotate the Polariser, the intensity decreases and then increases. So at which point we can say that the laser is linearly polarised?

I seriously doubt that what you have is a real "polarization rotator". No waveplate would affect the intensity of an unpolarized laser beam as it is rotated in the beam. Neither would a polarization rotator (which works by optical activity instead of wave retardation).

It sounds like you really have a polarized laser, and are rotating a polarizer in the beam. If this is true, the intensity of the beam will go from maximum to almost nothing as you rotate the polarizer through 90 degrees.
Hans Thu Oct 26, 2006 5:19 pm #4
> I bought a He Ne Laser-632.8nm @ 10mW O/P Power., But the laser was 'randomly polarising'and i heard from books that randomly polarised laser wont yeild better results.

You can make holograms with it, but the result will be unpredictable as the direction of polarization of your laser will vary in time. I think you should be able to correct for this with a circular polarizer (quarter wave plate) combined with a linear polarizer. Fortunately these things can be bought very cheaply as add on lenses for digital camera's. If you ask for a circular polarizer for a digital camera, it will always be combined with the linear polarizer.

> I have polariser rotator with me. Does it will make the laser linearly polarised?When we rotate the Polariser, the intensity decreases and then increases. So at which point we can say that the laser is linearly polarised?

This should not happen whith a polarization rotator (half wave plate). But the polarization rotator will not do you much good, unless it is the add on filter I just described to you. This is because when you just rotate the polarization direction of your laser. The randomly polarized light will still be polarized in a random direction after it has been rotated by a fixed direction.

But as you describe that the intensity changes as you rotate the ROTATOR, it makes me believe that what you are using actually IS a circular polarizer that has been made for photo camera use. In that case, you are using the filter in the wrong direction (linear polarization first, then circular). Try to shine the laser through the filter from the opposite direction. Now, as you rotate the filter, the intensity should not change. In that case it will work. As the light enters your filter, it will first be circularly polarized, and then linearly polarized by the subsequent linear polarizer. The light exiting your Rotator will then be constant and with fixed direction of polarization. The downside of this setup is that your filter will sacrifice a few mW of laser light.

> But as you describe that the intensity changes as you rotate the ROTATOR, it makes me believe that what you are using actually IS a circular polarizer that has been made for photo camera use. In that case, you are using the filter in the wrong direction (linear polarization first, then circular).This is not true. Shocked Surprised ConfusedIf you rotate a linear polarizer in a randomly polarized beam, you'll make linear polarization but the intensity will remain constant as you rotate it. If a quarter-waveplate is attached to the linear polarizer, the intensity transmitted as the assembly is rotated will also remain constant regardless of the orientation of the assembly.

If you take a linear polarizer and rotate it in a beam in which the polarization direction changes slowly with time (which I believe is the case in a laser that is not polarized: Saxby, Practical Holography, page 28 ), the exiting beam will not remain constant.

This holds also true if a Rotator is attached behind the linear polarizer in this order: LaserBeam->Polarizer->Quarter Wave PLate. What exits the Rotator now is circularly polarized light that changes with intensity in time and depending on the rotation of the Rotator.

If the Rotator is now flipped, the light is first made circular by the quarter wave plate, and thus will not vary with intensity depending on rotation angle and polarization angle. If the quarter wave plate is then followed by the linear polarizer, the exiting light is then again linearly polarized. But now constant in direction and intensity.

The thing to remember here is that in a laser that is "randomly" polarized, it is most often the case that the light IS actually polarized, but the direction of polarization is not constant with time. It can vary through great angles with time. This is a significant difference with true randomly polarized light, such as from a light bulb, that has no specific direction of polarization.

A very simple experiement to check your laser for polarization is to go out and buy a $5 pair of Polarized sunglasses (this can be a baseline as we know for sure it is a polarizing filter). Place one of the lenses in your expanded beam and rotate. If the light passing throught the lens goes from near 100% to near nothing then you laser is polarized. If you rotate the lens in the beam and the light does not change then most likely the laser is not polarized. If the light does seem to be polarized, mount the lens such that is can be in the beam for some time, either passing or filtering the light, and observe the through beam over time to see if it changes intensity.

> holography4fun: Now i have procurred some mounts, tables and optics which are essential for doing Experiments in Holography.


Of course you could set up a Michelson Interferometer and learn very much about the stability and nature of your laser, optics and table. Just expand and project the fringes on a wall and set a nice comfy chair away from the table (to minimize air currents) and view those interferometer fringes for as long as you need to. This is viewing the creation of a hologram in real time.
