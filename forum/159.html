I'm afraid it's just as much nonsense as Luton Airport!

The implication is that the "interference fringes" are somehow being drawn on the monitor/TV set and then a reconstruction light is used to reconstruct the hologram (from where?). The implication is also that this set of "interference fringes" are being refreshed at video rate.

Firstly, no TV or monitor can possibly resolve even a modest holographic fringe density of 1000 l/mm or structures of about a micron, or about 25,000 dpi. This frequency is what the film records when you make a transmission hologram with 514nm at about 30 degrees. Note: transmission hologram, not the obvious reflection holograms they were showing. Even if they could get a monitor to show 25,000 dpi and they were reconstructing a transmission hologram, there would be severe dispersion - all you'd see is a colour blur! To show a reflection hologram, as they seem to be doing, you'd need approximately 3000 l/mm or 75,000 dpi, and this is for image plane holograms. To show an object in free space several inches away from the screen, you'd need resolutions of several hundred thousand dpi.

Secondly, it's simply not possible today to transmit 100,000 dpi of organised data at 30 frames/sec through space to several TV sets. The "source" or "camera" would have to calculate the FT of an image, transmit the fringes through space, have the "TV" draw out the fringes (or display them simultaneously by an addressible set of pixels) and then do this again 30 milliseconds later!

You don't need "head tracking" to see a hologram. The eye position does not have to be fixed in any relation to the image, since the image field is an optical wavefront. All of you who make holograms I'm sure don't pass around "head tracking" hardware before you show your holograms to your friends!

What this is, probably, is simply an auto-stereoscopic display with head tracking. The left eye and right eye images are focused in space (possibly via a HOE - which is why they're saying it's "holographic" ) and the focused pair of spots is then moved according to head position by the head tracking equipment. This solves two problems:

1. You can have multiple pairs of auto-stereo positions

2. You can watch the auto-stereo signal even if you move your head.

One problem with auto-stereo is that the eye positions are fixed in space. If you move out of the focused, virtual eye positions, you lose the 3D. Your eyes have to be within the virtual apertures. This is one way they've been working on to cause "real time" focused auto-stereo pairs.

But, it ain't "holographic TV".

dr = 1/2{lambdan/f)^(-1/2)df + (fn/lambda))^(1/2)d(lambda)}

Several methods:

1. Coat a plane piece of glass with dcg. Shoot a hologram on this pane glass, one beam normal and collimated, the other diverging from some point from the other side. Now you have a glass lens that focuses at f_1 and a holographic lens that focuses at f_2. 2. Coat one surface of a lens with dcg; tricky because of coating on a curved surface but possible, again we've done this. Now hit the coated side with a planar beam and the other side with a diverging beam from any point other than f_1, but closer to the lens than f_1. Calculate the entire object wavefunction

Calculate the entire wavefunction but leave out the "dc" term. That is, leave out the I_1 + I_2 term, since this does not contribute to the phase data.

Calculate bits of the object wavefunction.

ab = |o(x,y|^2

The displacement of the window at any given point causes a displacement of the optical path that exactly corresponds to a recovery of the phase of the object wavefunction at that point. Once all the cells are "written", then the entire hologram is illuminated by a reconstruction beam to reconstruct the hologram

the cells are not the same size but depend on the intensity of the object wave

the displaced windows are not in the same position, but depend on the phase of the wavefront

The grating equation for two off-axis input beams is:

m(lambda) = d(sin(phi) +/- sin(theta))

where d is the slit width (the 'pixel' size if you will, although 'pixel ' may be vague in this context since electronic digital displays do not obey the grating equation), m is the order and phi and theta are the two beams impinging on the grating. These days the 'm' is never used because holography rarely goes past the first minimum. Back in the 60's the holograms were very, very thin and so you generally went past several of the sinc minima. The +/- is dependent on whether the two beams are on the same side of the plate normal or not (use '+' if they're not). If one of the beams is on-axis to the plate normal, then one of the angles is zero (let's say phi). In this case, the grating equation reduces to the familiar:

m(lambda) = dsin(theta)

In which case, the diffraction angle is:

theta = arcsin(mlambda/d)

If both beams are off-axis, then, assuming you're interested in the theta deviation:

theta = arcsin{m(lambda) -/+ d*sin(phi)}/d

There's an extra term due to the off-axis geometry.

In either case, the relationship between d ('pixel' size) and wavelength in the most general case is the diffraction angle(s):

lambda/d = sin(theta) +/- sin(phi)

Ok, I went to their site and read this (Holographic 3-D Displays - Electro-holography within the Grasp of Commercialization (SCIYO.COM) ) paper. There are a number of standard misconceptions. They say, for example, that every "pixel" of a hologram contains the entire image. That's not generally true. If you break a single beam Denisyuk into tiny pieces (eg 1mm diameter shards) and then illuminate one of the shards, you won't see the entire image. This idea is a hangover from the days of laser transmission holograms where the object was a long way from the hologram plane. When the object is very near the plate, no small piece can show the entire object. They also say that the hologram has a "large diffraction angle", then give the space-bandwidth product as a measure of the object beam divergence (from Nyquist) , not the diffraction angle. Now, of course, a large object will give a large object beam divergence and so the extremes of the object field will have large diffraction angles. However, I do get the feeling that they're confusing the diffraction angle (ie the angle of the reference to the plate) with the beam divergence of the object (the angle subtended by the object at the plate). There are a number of other instances, but these caught my eye straightaway.

Now, the problem they're addressing is: If the 'hologram has a large diffraction angle' (not clear what that means), then you need a high spatial frequency (true). However, conventional electronic systems don't have the necessary resolution (true, up to a point. A continuous set of fringes is not quantised and so the concept of "pixel" may not be valid). Their solution is to limit the viewing zone of the hologram by computing the eye position and then to create a hologram fringe structure with a low enough spatial frequency over a narrow section of the display (small holographic aperture) such that the diffracted image field also has a narrow aperture and is directed pretty much to the eye (almost a Gabor geometry with a very small film). As the eye moves, the eye is tracked and, at the new eye position, a new hologram is computed and generated over a small aperture with a narrow viewing aperture. These, constantly created, low aperture holograms are referred to as "sub-holograms, except that these sub-holograms don't exist simultaneously but are created in situ - real time, as it were ("apreture" here meaning the aperture over which the fringe structure is created - not the viewing aperture). The idea is that as you move the eye, the part of the hologram that you would have seen, had it been a 'conventional' hologram would be created as a "sub-hologram" subtending only that part of the image at the eye. I'm not sure how they deal with peripheral vision, but we'll let that pass.

It's a clever idea. There are however a number of issues with that. Firstly, this is clearly a transmission geometry. The light source is behind the display. The light source illuminates the display and so reconstructs the(momentary) sub hologram. In this situation, there is going to be considerable dispersion, unless they use a laser source to reconstruct. Even with a laser source, the angle of diffraction is pretty small, which means that will will be a large number of diffractive orders. No hologram is ever 100% in practice. In this case, the hologram is probably a amplitude hologram in transmission, which implies a 30% efficiency at best. This means there will be a zero order beam. Due to the narrow diffraction angles, the zero order beam is probably very close to the viewer. In a Gabor hologram, it went directly into the viewers eye. The narrow angle of diffraction will also superimpose the conjugate image very close to the primary image. In a Gabor they're right on top of each other. In this geometry they won't be right on top, but will superimpose slightly. If they're using an LED to reconstruct, then there will be dispersion of the LED itself (the zero order beam) as well as dispersion the object. delta( lambda) = dcos(theta)delta(theta)

with delta lambda being 50nm. theta is small, cos(theta) is large and so delta theta is also large.
