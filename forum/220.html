Dinesh Mon May 09, 2011 12:54 pm #2
Jeez, Jeff! Talk about cat amongst the pigeons! Asking "what is pseudo-colour?" is a bit like whether McDonalds tastes better than Burger King! The whole question is mostly subjective, one person's "pseudo-colour" is another person's "true colour", especially in these rainbow holograms.

Here's my take, for what it's worth. Let's start with what colour is. Colour is a sensation on the eye, interpreted by the brain, of the natural world. You may remember that essay I did on colour perception (which is apparently all over the web - I'm surprised that non-holographers would be interested in that at all!). Anyway, continuing, the real world produces colour by means of natural pigments created out of proteins, atomic structure etc. In this sense, colours in the real world cannot be "wrong". If you see real blue grass, then the colour of the grass is not "wrong" because grass is supposed to be green, this is just a variant of grass.

However, when it comes to reproducing colour in some medium, then the job of the reconstruction process is to produce a colour that is recognisable by association to the real world. So, if you print a picture with pink grass, then we all "know" that grass is "really" green and the pink grass is "art". The observer of the picture cannot know whether the photographer really found pink grass in nature. But, there a gazillion real colours in nature, so we use a reduction process which takes advantage of the fact that we have only three colour receptors in our eyes - red, green and blue (the exact wavelengths of these receptors in the eye is in that essay I mentioned). We use three basic primaries and reproduce all the colours of nature with these primaries in various proportions - out-of-gamut and negative-coefficient colour notwithstanding for now. However, we choose the proportions to give us a deliberate copy of nature - green for grass - or an exaggerated unnatural effect - pink for grass. We can pretty much make any object any colour by simply changing the proportions of the primaries. We can even alter the inputs from electronic equipment to alter real-world colours to bizarre unnatural colours by simply changing the white point in the camera.

In holography, the colours of a hologram are generated by fringe separation or by diffraction angles, or both. In fact, you could argue that both these methods are actually the same method. So, colour is not an intrinsic property of the medium, as it is in print media or film, but a by-product of a physical process. You can control this physical process in a number of ways:

1. Swell the emulsion

2. Change the angles of the reference

3. Expose the film with different wavelengths.

All of these methods alter the physical process of the playback and generate "colours". But, do they generate "real" colours, ie do they generate the actual colours of a real-world object or scene.

You could argue that the reproduction of "real" colours are those that are captured directly from a real world object or scene by some capture method (camera etc) and reproduced in a medium (eg film) with no alteration of or by the medium and by the capturing method. So, a photo of grass focused onto colour film by a camera is "real" colour" when there are no filters on the camera and the colour on the film is a faithful reproduction of the colour of the grass, albeit artificially reproduced by dyes in the film.

If the colour of a real world object or scene is altered by some physical device (eg filters) or by some processing scheme on the media (eg non-standard development), then the "colour" is not a real world colour captured faithfully, but imposed onto the medium by a physical or procedural process. In this case, you could call the the resulting colour "pseudo-colour" - 'pseudo' because it's not the actual colour of the real object, but a false colour imposed by your process.

In holography, a faithful reproduction of colour would imply that the process whereby a hologram is both recorded and reconstructed would capture colour information faithfully and reconstruct it faithfully. But, holography is a diffractive process. What comes out of a hologram is not "colour", but a reconstituted set of pure wavelengths given by the fringe separation. Our eyes do not see pure wavelengths because the receptors in our eyes are not pure red, green and blue; there's a massive superimposition of the wavelength peaks (again the exact wavelength sensitivity of the retinal dyes are in that essay). So, whatever fringes are recorded in the hologram, they must diffract the exact same wavelength combinations that are recognised by the eye brain system as "true colour". In holography also, the colour of the source is very impportant; if the colour in the source does not contain the colour in the hologram, it ain't going to diffract. So now the reconstruction process involves not only the medium, but the reconstruction of that medium.

So, pseudo colour in holography is generally considered to be swelling the emulsion - an artificial manipulation of the colour reproduction system by artificially altering the fringe separation. In your case of rainbows, that too would be pseudocolour because you're artificially generating a specific colour(s) by manipulation of the reference beams - you decide which reference beam angle you want to impose a particular colour rather than let nature and an unaltered capture system decide on the colour. You can make grass pink if you chose to, by simply altering the reference angle. However, I could argue that all colour holography is pseudo-colour because even "true colour" holography with three (or more) lasers is still manipulation of the medium to get the "right colours" - in this case getting the chemistry right.

I personally like the following definitions:

"Pseudo color" in holography is the color that is not related to the reflection spectrum of the object and is created solely by special processing using single laser wavelength. Much like painted b&w photograph.

"Real color" is the color caused by different reflection of different wavelengths by the object. Here we have to distinguish between "just real color" (using 3 or sometimes 2 lasers) and "true color" that represents the full spectrum (like Lippmann photography). Looks like "true color" is impossible in holography.

> PinkysBrain: Colour holograms tend to be RBG reconstructions, so it's more about white point than temperature.

The association of colour temperatures to a white point is common in digital photography to take account of ambient conditions. The white point is that point where the coordinates, x-bar, y-bar and g-bar for example, are equal, so a specific white point is defined with respect to a specific colour chart - CIE 1931, CIE 1976, CIELAB etc. However, an absolute colour temperature regardless of any specific colour chart can be assigned as the radiation emitted by a black body at a given temperature. In this case, the colour temperature would be follow Planck's Law, or Wien's Law, or the Rayleigh Jeans Law, depending on the range of temperatures over which the source emits.

Whether or not a colour temperature can be assigned to a LED is problematical, since the LED does not emit Planckian radiation. The same is true of fluorescent light bulbs, but they've gotten around that by assigning a colour temperature to the fluorescent source as to a Planckian source giving off the same radiation

The so-called color temperature of fluorescent lights is a trick pulled by the manufacturers. A color temperature meter, like those used in photography, is not a broadband spectrometer. It is simply red and blue filters in front of two separate light meters. The ratio of the red to blue readings gives a color temperature by looking at two points on the black body radiation curve.

Take a look at a color temperature rated fluorescent tube through a grating, or bounced off a CD, and you will see that distinctive strong green line indicative of the mercury emission, which might be ignored by the above-mentioned device or lumped in with the red reading.

Dinesh Fri May 20, 2011 12:08 pm #36
> PinkysBrain: Any way ... my point about white point was that the hologram itself does not emit a smooth spectrum. A light with R/G/B leds at appropriate wavelengths might have poor CRI, but as long as the white point was right it would be as good as any lamp with a continuous spectrum to replay a hologram.

Well, actually, a hologram does not emit anything. It diffracts light from a set of interference planes at specific frequencies with specific strengths which are proportional to both the efficiency of the medium and the Bragg plane strength; in other words, a pure phase hologram in, say, dcg with a particular index modulation will have an output intensity that's a particular fraction of the input intensity. The same phase hologram in silver will have a different output intensity for the same input intensity because the scattering profile from point scatterers of reduced grains is different from the scattering profile from a continuous medium.

This may seem overly pedantic, but I mention this because the discussion so far has focused on some sort of objective measurement of the frequency as a metaphor for "colour". However, colour is not a set of wavelengths, it's a subjective impression. As such, we need to take into account the photopic curve over which we interpret colour. For example, if our equipment reads a "blue" at 457nm of m units (say, candelas), then how do we perceive that "blue" (we actually can't, since a single frequency cannot be seen) ? If we measure a band of frequencies from 457 to 488 and at b units, then again, how do we perceive this "colour"? What colour is a yellow brick, as opposed to what are the band of frequencies, and with what strengths, can such a yellow brick be measured?

Also, in terms of colour, there is a statistical component that I don't think has been appreciated yet. Let's say, for example, that your "red" fringes fall off as a Gaussian centered at 633 (the frequency cannot be a Dirac spike simply because of differential swelling, which cannot be avoided). In this case, a flat white source - one that a constant intensity across the visible spectrum - will diffract a "red" intensity according to the sigma on the Gaussian. This diffracted "red" will then have to be convolved with the scotopic curve to determine what "red" the observer actually sees. If you use a led, then the spectrum of the led will have to be convolved with the Gaussian of the fringe frequencies (which will depend on the sigma of the response curve) and then convolved again with the scotopic. All of this is again true of the other sets of fringe frequencies.

Remember also, the cone response functions don't peak at specific wavelengths. There's a great deal of overlap, resulting in, for example, wavelengths surrounding 600nm giving a large "green" response at the cones.

> BobH: My guess is that the CRI of all reflection holography would be pretty low.

My guess is that the colur rendition is pretty low because the Gaussians of the three frequency components overlap. If you look at the cone responses, a large spread (large value of sigma) in the "red" components will give more of a green response and make the red appear yellowish.

> Joe Farina: Of course it will never be absolutely "true" or "real" color, no matter how much technology is involved (at least, I can't imagine this).

Why not? Is TV "real" or "true" colour. No colour reproduction is ever "real" because we have no idea what the original colour was. We have a mental picture of what's being portrayed and let our imaginations (or memories) do the rest. If you see a field of green grass, for example, you'd think that it's "true" colour because that's what the colour of grass is. But if you take you "colour-meter" and measure real grass and then take that same meter and measure the picture, you'll probably see a significant difference. The interesting thing is that if there is a human face in the picture, we tend to center everything around the colour of the face. If the face is wrong, everything is wrong, if the face is right, then everything is right!
