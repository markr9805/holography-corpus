> d paul: What I tried was to put a thin strip of rubber cement around the top edge of the plate to make a fence or dam around the perimeter. That made it easier to control the puddle when you move it around the corners.

Like John, I once tried to set a puddle of gelatin on a flat plate, and it simply didn't spread out. That actually sounds like an interesting method, i.e., to manually tilt the plate until it is wetted over the whole surface, with some type of dam at the edges, then allow it to set by gravity. I would think that the gelling time should be delayed enough, though, so that gravity has a chance to do its job completely. Plus, the laminar flow or dust-exclusion system must be in top working order, becasue the gelatin layer will be a dust-magnet. This method may involve little wasted area at the edges of the plate, unlike bar-coating.

But I still favor a bar for coating, because thickness changes are simplest and most direct with this technique, in my opinion. One just needs to change to a different bar with different windings (or one with different size grooves in the case of a "formed" bar instead of a wire bar). The veil method is not capable of such direct thickness changes (the gelatin concentration would need to be changed, or multiple coatings done). The molding method would be excellent if one does not have a laminar flow area, but if one already has this, then it seems less predicatable and repeatable as compared to bar coating, more work, and less amenable to producing a large number of plates.

Hey John,

Your on the right track regarding the fence technique. If the plate is warm (90F) you can pour the metered amount of gel in and tilt it in a slightly circular motion until the surface is covered, then place it on a flat warm surface, the gel will settle. The key is to keep the gel liquid until it settles down to the flat surface, that's why thinner films is useful. Spinning is not neccessay, but when I first did it I used a 45RM (had to remove my Bee Gee's record ). The reason was with small plates that I used 3x3 it would form a bow and need some small amount of centrifugal force to over come it. Larger plates are less problematic. If you really want to know how I first did it, I used a 3x3 PVC pipe with RTV as a gasket and a wieght for compression on top just to prove the concept. Worked good and was cheap it make. Also if the plate is super clean, surface tension is be minimal.

You will get a slight meniscus on the edges where the gel rides up on the fence.

One important factor in the cleaning of my plates, which I have stated but I am sure most do not follow through with is ....... well let me give you history on how I know this is important, then I will tell you....... I am one of those guys that does everything, I mean I try to help with everything around the house. One of the things I don't mind doing is cleaning the windows of the house. Now, if you have ever cleaned window with Windex or even just vinegar and water and a paper towel or newspaper you will see something very interesting. You have to remember cleaning is nothing more the a dilution of dirt.... all cleaning. Nothing is ever clean, the "dirt" or contaminant is just diluted to an acceptable level. So back to the windows. Rarely can you clean the windows as described above and get them perfectly streak free unless you continue to use a continually, from wet to dryer paper towel. If you simply wipe the window with the wettened (with cleaner) paper towel, when the glass dries you will see streaks. If after you wipe the window with the wet towel and then it dries completely before using a dry paper towel, the streaks will remain after wiping with the dry paper towel. The only way to really get a very clean window is to first wash with wet paper towel, then while the window is still damp us a dryer paper towel. You will see the friction increase when the exact combination of wetness and dryness is achieved. Too wet, not much friction, too dry, not much friction, just right, very great friction.

So, what's my point. In all of my cleaning process posts I have stated that after I soak in HCl (only about 5%), scrub in HCl, scrub in hot water I place the plates in a rack. Now the important part. BEFORE the plates are dry I take a paper towel and while having gloves on, I wipe the plate and turn the towel until just that right friction of cleaning is felt. I mean I really wipe and rub it. I usually have to turn the towel two or three times to get that good friction and then rub the entire plate with that exactly correctly very slightly damp towel. I use gloves because I am sure that if did not the oils from my fingers would get on the towels and thus when I turn the towel, be smeared on the plate. Then a blow of air to rid of any towel particles.

Does this make a difference? It does when I clean my house windows.....
Joe Farina Thu Oct 04, 2007 11:19 am #30
I also think the grease or oil problem has been underestimated when there are adhesion problems. In my opinion (and based on experience), if the grease issue is definitely under control, then a normal DCG layer should adhere pretty well to the glass. Dave has provided a very useful approach (by the way, thanks Dave) in his use of TSP. This is extremely effective as a degreaser (I had used it previously for this purpose, but didn't think of using it for cleaning glass for DCG). It's now part of my standard procedure, and I have very good adhesion. When I scrape the edges with a razor blade after the hologram is finished, it's quite tenacious, and it takes considerable effort with the blade to remove the gelatin.

All that I do is mix the TSP according to the directions, and pour a couple gallons into the bottom of my plastic sink (or a plastic tray could be used). Then I place the glass under the liquid, and scrub both sides with an ordinary sponge. Then I let it set under the liquid for another 15 minutes, then scrub again. Then rinse the glass well under running water. All this is done wearing latex gloves, and the glass is dried using paper towels.

Not the safest thing to handle, but to clean any large organic molecule I use a mixture of sodium hydoxide (5%) and sodium hypochlorate (about 2 oz in a gallon). At 140F it provides an excelent cleaner and grease does not survive. This solution take a lot of water to rinse, use Deionized water. Then a quick rinse in very hot DI water. Do not get this on your skin of in your eyes!

Note: TSP is extremely difficult to rinse and I use lots of DI water for it as well, if it feels slick then it has not been rinsed enough.
