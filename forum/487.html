> HoloM: I assume masters are mostly done on a photoresist, and then are metallized for embossing. Which companies do this?Which software is used?
The main problem here are the words we use. They mean different things to different people in different fields. Embossed holograms are not digitally generated, if, "digitally" you mean some kind of software that outputs some kind of file. They are "digital" in that dot matrix machines are run by computers that run specific software, but the software runs the machine, it does not record the hologram. The lasers still record the hologram. To the best of my knowledge, there is no commercially available software that can make holograms. Companies and universities that make digital holograms write their own software.

In the holographic world, there are three basic kinds: embossed ("mass produced", "security" etc etc), HOE/DOE, classical display.

In the first kind - "embossed" - a hologram is made by a variety of ways. This hologram is then electroformed to create a "shim", which is then embossed onto a substrate, or hot stamped onto foil. The actual embossing (or hot stamping) is usually not done by holographers, it's usually done by a separate company such as Novavision or Wavefront. Companies such as American Banknote (now JDSU) and Crown Roll leaf still do both the holography and the embossing (and many other things!), but this is not now any longer true. Nowadays, the actual holograms are made by individual companies or people such as Pacific Holography or (as Ed mentioned ) Jeff Weil.These holographers get their work from embossers who deal with the client and then "farm" out the holography. The holograms made by holographers or holographic companies are called "Glass Masters". After electroforming, they're called "Silver Masters". If you talk to an embosser, "master" means the product after silvering and electroforming.

There s another form of "master" made by a technique called "lithography". In this technique, the pattern that a traditional hologram makes is etched directly onto the substrate to create a surface profile. There is software that calculates a lithographic pattern, which can then be impressed on a substrate (eg resist). These techniques are sometimes known as DOE's (Diffractive Optical Elements), because a diffraction pattern is calculated by the software for mostly technical optics. Another technique is known as "e-beam", where a beam of electrons etch into a substrate. There are machines and software used for controlling the electrons and making e-beam "holograms", but these are not traditional holograms. Some display embossed holograms use this lithographic-ebeam technique to produce masters which are then embossed. I believe De La Rue uses an e-beam method ( [http://www.delarue.com/ProductsSolution ... olutionsf/](http://www.delarue.com/ProductsSolutions/Holographics/BrandProtectionand/HologramSolutionsf/) ) E-beam technique are also lithographic, but to a much finer scale.

On the other hand there are "digital" holograms that are made by software, but these are not embossed. The diffraction pattern of a hologram is calculated and the pattern is then etched directly onto the medium using a laser and lenses. Usually, the companies doing this have their own software. Zebra comes to mind. Zebra has some software on their site that you can use to make your own holograms, called "Zscape" ( [http://www.zebraimaging.com/](http://www.zebraimaging.com/) ).

There are techniques are known as "CGH" (Computer Generated Holograms) and there is a lot of interest in them from academia. CGH is a mostly mathematical technique that calculates the hologram that would have been made by a conventional laser and then directly records the pattern onto a substrate. There is a great deal of academic interest in this technique but it's not usually seen by the public, as far as I know.

There are holograms made directly from CGI data, or data generated by equipment and then processed using CGI technique. On a display level, companies such as Liti ( [http://www.litiholographics.com/](http://www.litiholographics.com/) ) will take your video sequence, apply their own software and make a hologram. Holorad (formerly Voxel) takes CAT scan data generated by a CAT scan machine, procceses the raw CAT scan data into image data and then records a hologram composed of many individual CAT scan images. Holorad have their own propretry software to do this. We (Triple Take Holographics) have our own software that can also take imagery from any video sequence to make our "floating image" display ( [http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zgcKKgQPjuw](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zgcKKgQPjuw) ). The point is: any hologram company tha makes display holograms usually have written the software themselves, there's nothing off-the-shelf.

As for books, it depends on your technical level. A good series of books for the non-technical person are the "Holography Marketplace" by Ross Books ( [http://www.amazon.com/Holography-Market ... 0894960253](http://www.amazon.com/Holography-Marketplace-Alan-Rhody/dp/0894960253) ). These books, eight volumes in all,are a good source of undersatnding the different types of embossed holograms, the companies involved and the techniques. A more technical book is "Digital Diffractive Optics" ( [http://www.amazon.com/Digital-Diffracti ... 873&sr=1-1](http://www.amazon.com/Digital-Diffractive-Optics-Introduction-Technology/dp/0471984477/ref=sr_1_1?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1330648873&sr=1-1)).

As for papers, the SPIE Proceedings of the Practiacal Holography meetings are full of papers on CGH. The new one for this year's convention just came out ([http://spie.org/x648.html?product_id=897088](http://spie.org/x648.html?product_id=897088) ). At a (much!) higher technical level, look for papers by Moharam, Gaylord and anything on Rigorous Coupled Wave Analysis (RCWA), for example: [http://www.opticsinfobase.org/josa/abst ... m?id=58041](http://www.opticsinfobase.org/josa/abstract.cfm?id=58041)

> PinkysBrain: To directly record fringes near <1/2 visible wavelengths e-beam seems to be the only game in town,

Not so. Cymer ( [http://www.cymer.com/](http://www.cymer.com/) ), here in San Diego provides lasers to the electronic industry for nanometer tracks on circuit boards. MIT's holo-video uses acousto-optics to create momentary Bragg planes in a crystal.

You don't have to record near < lambda/2 because the spatial frequency deos not have to be lambda/(2*n). This is the spatial frequency of two collimated beams hitting the plate from opposite sides of the film on-axis. Most glass masters for embossing are usually about 1000 l/mm, mostly because the response function for resist drops rapidly at about 1500 l/mm. However, 1000 l/mm creates fringes with a 1 micron separation. For recording CGH's with, say the Binary Detour Phase methods, a laser can be used.

Dinesh Mon Mar 05, 2012 8:42 pm #13
> PinkysBrain: The dispersion is just distortion, nothing more.

Well, it depends on what you mean by "distortion". Dispersion is not an aberration, such as coma or astigmatism. It's dispersion that allows you to see the hologram, if the hologram had no dispersion, the bandwidth would be too thin to register on the retina. Colour is generally a dispersion effect, where the degree of dispersion gives the saturation (the hue being given by the centre frequency). So, if the diffraction from the hologram were to center around, say, 500nm +/- 10 nm, then the hologram would appear to be a "sharp" green. With no dispersion, the hologram would diffract at 500 +/- 0nm, which is impossible (it violates theTime-Energy uncertainy, since a single frequency means you measure an effect that occurs over a zero time interval))

> PinkysBrain: (strictly speaking you record trichromatic 2D light fields for a lot of points on a grid, but comes down to the same thing

Only in a photograph. In a hologram, you're recording phase information relative to the reference beam, so you're recording the magnitude of the E field (the intensity), the angle between the E field of the Object and the E field of the referene beams (the phase) and the separation of the peaks of the E field over a period of time (the frequency). You can think of this as a three dimensional phase diagram (here, "phase" refers to the system state, as opposed the optical phase). To make the recording possible, the E field across the entire aperture must be constant and stay constant over the period of the exposure, but this is not really a phase state, as much as it's an "existence condition" or "existence theorem".

> PinkysBrain: It's unwanted and adds nothing useful to the image on replay ... the extra brightness you get from accepting a wider spectrum is wanted of course, but not the dispersion.

Colour is not brightness. The point is that without hue and chroma there is no hologram. Brightness is defined as the luminance is any given wavelength range for grey scale or for tristumulus values. There is no "increase" in brightness from a "wider spectrum" (you mean presumably a larger bandwidth rather than a wider spectrum, since our eyes cannot see a wider spectrum beyond, roughly 400nm to 700nm). The sum total of intensity is also different from the concept of brightness, since now you need to take into account the scotopic and photopic response functions. Without any colour, the image would simply be a greyscale, like an open-aperture "achromat" transmission. I would suggest that there's a difference between colour, the variation of colour and the tonal range of any given colour. All three of these are dispersive effects and encompass a given definition of delta lambda, but tonal range is a function of the integral of the photopic (or scotopic) function over lambda.

> PinkysBrain: I can see laser light just fine ... energy is important, not bandwidth.

Actually, you can't see laser light. In fact, you can't see any light at all! What you see is an interaction of light with matter that produces a visual stimulation. If, and when, you "see" a laser beam across a room, what you're seeing is the light reflected from dust. If there was no dust, you'd not see the beam. Certainly deep space is not permeated by an infinite amount of light a la Olber's Paradox.

Again, the bandwidth defines energy. Without bandwidth there is no energy! The energy in any electromagnetic field is the integral of the energy density function over the wavelength. If you allow an "S" for an integral sign, then:

E = S<E(lambda)d(lambda)>

You have to put in the limits over the bandwidth from lambda(1) to lambda(2). However, without bandwidth

lambda(1) = lambda(2)

and so

E = 0!

Also, laser light does have a bandwidth. You cannot have zero bandwidth! Consider what is meant by an item of physical reality outside of the purview of the EPR paper. In order to determine the objective reality of a phenomenon, you need to take a measurement, since, without a meausrement there is no physical reality. However, in order to measure something, you need to use a measuring instrument. However, all physical measuring systems have an inherent uncertainty. So, all measurements have an inherent uncertainty. So, in order to measure the speed of a car, you need to track the car over a specified distance and time it over a specific period. If the distance moved or the time interval is shorter than the uncertainty in measurement, then the physical reality of the car actaully moving is brought intop question. If you were measuring a frequency phenomenon, you need to time at least one complete period of the phenomenon. But, if the frequency is a Dirac delta - no bandwidth, then you would have to measure this frequency over zero time. In order to measure the frequency, you'd have to employ the Shannon criterion. But, measuring something over a zero period imlpies infinite uncertainty.

Dinesh -

The "light field" is a construct utilized in a hot field of computer graphics / optics. 4D is just referring to a particular parameterization of rays that go through a 3-D scene (see, "plenoptic function.") You're probably already up to speed on this stuff, but one 4-D parameterization considers each ray as passing through two 2-D reference planes. If one plane has axes (u,v) and the other (s,t), then the properties of a ray through the volume is described by 4 position components: L(s,t,u,v).

Anyway, if this isn't something you've read about I think you'll get a kick out of it. Light field-thinking has generated some neat advances in computational photography.

The appearance of continuity in a discretized image is just a question of resolution ... also traditional dot matrix interferes a couple of beams, the object beam is not generally a highly converging beam modulated with a hemisphere intensity pattern appropriate for the dot.

It's dot matrix on steroids.

> Surely this is a contradiction? Another point is that I know that they take a ray from the scene from various angles, but how do they determine the direction of the ray?

The virtual hologram is simply a window in the synthetic 3D scene, pick a spot on it, any spot ... at that spot they need to ray trace in all directions of a hemisphere (if you want real images in front of the hologram plane you have to start the rays behind the hologram plane and trace towards and through it). Then add some discretization.

They don't pick the direction, they take them all. Every ray on the hemisphere represents one pixel on the SLM you use to modulate the object beam for the hogel.
