Dinesh Wed Oct 06, 2004 4:09 pm #6
Two laser cannot interfere because they're not mutually coherent either spatially or temporally. When you do two beam holography, the beamsplitter makes two identical copies of the wavefronts. These identical copies go off in two different directions, rejoin, and, because they're identical, interfere. If you tried to interfere two different lasers with no beamsplitter, the phase difference would change. Note, the difference in phase. In your paper analogy [quote:f348b005fa]I overlaid the clear set on the paper set at an angle. I observed the almost moiré pattern. If I advanced the angled set of waves on the clear plastic in the direction they are facing, I still get a moiré effect just different.[/quote:f348b005fa]

instead of keeping either paper still, advance both papers simultaneously. If the two wavelengths are the same you'll notice that the moire pattern stays constant as you move both papers. If the wavelengths change, the moire pattern itself changes as you move the papers. The film is trying to record the moire pattern. If, as the wave travels, the moire pattern does not change, it can be captured. However, if the moire is constantly changing, the film can't capture it. You migt be tempted to say, "What if I get a film fast enough?" Well, move both papers at 186,283 miles per second and see how fast the moire pattern is changing.

These are actually pretty frequently asked questions. The "Where does the energy go?" question is as old as interference itself and still not easy to answer completely. Young's two-slit interference, and it's modern counterpart in Hidden Variable Theory, is one of the most complex and connfusing aspect of Physics.

OK, Let's try anyway! On a pure wave theory it's pretty easy. You can see this in any wave machine. I don't know if they still do this, but when I was learning Physics in high school they used to project a wave machine on a screen and demonstrate interference. One wave pushes the water up and the other pushes it down. If both waves arrive at the same bit of water simultaneously, the water don't move! Light as a wave theory is also easy. Remember that the "height" of a wave in a standard depiction of light waves is an electric field. The "height" is actually a potential measured in volts/cm. A while back, answering one of Mark Cavin's posts, I used a parallel plate capacitor analogy to show the behaviour of light. So-o-o, One light wave comes along. This light wave has a frequency of 10^14 Hz, in other words it goes through a complete cycles in one hundred thousand billionth of a second. It has an amplitude (peak) of 10. You have hypothetical voltmeters with zero dimensions and infinite resolution and infinite range. Also, it has a centre zero. In other words it can read negative voltages. You place these voltmeters all over space, or, let's say, you place them along a light path. Look at any individual voltmeter and, let's say, you happened to look at it when it read 3.6V. As you watch, it rises to 10 volts, falls to zero volts, goes the other way, goes up (down?) to -10 volts, goes the other way back to zero, continues on to +10 volts and now starts on a downward journey. Soon it's back to 3.6 V. You've just watched one entire wave of light as it goes up, down and back up. This entire sequence from 3.6 V back to 3.6V has taken one hundred thousand billionth of a second. The initial value, where you happened to catch it, is called "the initial phase". It's obviously pretty arbitrary, since you could have picked any voltmeter and it could have been reading any voltage when you first looked at it. Take a picture of these voltmeters strung out. Each one reads a slightly different voltages. In the direction of travel of the wave each one is a little advanced in the sequence I mentioned - one reads 3.6V, it's neighbour reads 3.8V, a little way down the roasd one reads 10V and a little further on one reads 9.8V etc. You see a light wave "stopped in time". Now bring on another wave. If the other wave is coherent with the original wave, there will be points where the voltmeter needle will be pulled one way by one wave, let's say trying to read 6V, while being pulled another way by the other wave, lets say trying to read -2 V. The voltmeter needle ends up reading 6-2 or 4V. Further down a little, one wave is trying to get the needle to go 10V while the other is trying to get it to go -10V. Result: the needle don't move! the resulting voltage is zero - destructive interference. At still another position, one wave is pushing the voltmeter needle to +10V and so is the other wave. Result: the needle reads +20V - constructive interference. If the two waves are mutually coherent, the needles all along the areas of intersection of the two waves will remain the same. The one reading 4V will continue reading 4V, the one reading zero volts will continue reading zero. Now, you're an emulsion. You go along and read the voltmeters. You decide that you're going to paint yourself a greyscale according to the voltmeter readings. But wait, how do you paint a negative colour?! Aha! being the clever mathematician you are, you say, "If I square all the voltmeter readings, I can't get a negative colour" Bingo! So you have a range of voltages from zero to 100 (10 sqaured). You decide to, say distribute 50 greyscale values over the 100 possible values of any voltmeter. As you walk along the voltmetrs, you notice that a pattern emerges. You've found that you've painted a sort of zebra-striped set of lines that start from light to grey to grey still to pitch black to grey to less grey. In fact, this variation of grey follows a sinusoifdal pattern.

However, what if you have two lights from two different lasers, not coherent. Then you try to record the voltmeter reading, but you find they're changing too fast! You can't actually write it down because thge voltmeter needle is swinging wildly back and forth!

Now, being the smart Physicist you are, you say, "OK I'm going to try something. I'm going to try and displace on of my two interfering waves back without moving the other one. What happens to my voltages now. Well, you move one beam back, let's say an inch. Nothing. The voltages remain as before. Move back more, two inches, still nothing. Continue, three inches. Now you notice that some of the needles aren't reading the same anymore. The needles are beginning to drift. You can still write down the readings, but by the time you've written them down, they've changed. Four inches, now the readings are changing too fast to write down. Can you can guess the coherence length?

Photons is another and far more difficult story. Photons are models. It has no physical substance. It carries no charge and it has no mass. If two photons "bump" into each other, they pass right through each other. Photons don't "feel" other photons. So how do they interfere? Well, they actually don't. It's very difficult to understand interference (or diffraction) in a photon theory. You can sort of set an "internal voltmeter" inside them that constantly changes as they move forward. When two photons meet, they can "compare" their internal voltmeters and "decide" on something that "may" be interpreted as a phase (I forgot to mention that the voltage readings on the voltmeter are the called "phase"). Feynmann used this analogy a lot, except instead of voltmeters, he called them "stopwatches" somehow "attached" to the photon. It sort of ties the photon theory to the wave theory, except I find it a little ad hoc.. You can use one theory to explain one set of phenomena and another to explain another. Interference cannot be explained by photons and the Photoelectric Effect cannot be explained by waves. But then, how do single photons interfere? This was known back in the sixties. The Standard Answer, the so-called Copenhagen Interpretation, is to simply say. Foggetabahtit! Basically, if it cannot be measured, it's existence makes no sense. Understand that this doesn't answer whether or not it exists. The concept of "existence" becomes meaningless. Sort of like asking how high an elephant could fly if you trimmed it's wings. Since the single photon only interferes if you don't try to "track" it's movement, no measurement can be made on single photons that interfere. Once you attempt any measurement, they won't interfere. Existence is predated on measurement alone. Some (me, included) found this a little unsatisfactory. In fact Professor Bohm (who I had the privelege of studying under for one course) "resolved" it by talking of a reality underneath our observed sense of reality which "really" drives these phenomena. he called this Hidden Variables. Most Physicists were very polite and just as dismissive back then. Today Bell's theories have made Hidden Variables sort of respectable. But single-photon interference is still, as far as I know a Big Mystery. I haven't done any Theoretical Physics since about 79, so I have no idea what the state of knowledge on this is now. Perhaps the String people have an answer.

> Dinesh: Young's two-slit interference, and it's modern counterpart in Hidden Variable Theory, is one of the most complex and connfusing aspect of Physics.

> Dinesh: Today Bell's theories have made Hidden Variables sort of respectable.

Actually, Bell's inequality was used to prove Hidden Variable theories are wrong. Alain Aspect[1] showed it, and later it was shown without inequalities by using triplets of entangled particles.

For those following at home, Hidden Variables (see the famous EPR paradox[2]) says that two entangled entities (say photons) are 'entangled' in the sense that they were programmed with the same properties (say polarization on X, Y, and Z) from the start. So, you measure the same polarization when you measure the same axis on both photons (say, X). But, if you measure one (say, X), on the first particle, and then another (Y) on the other, you've violated QM because (and this was the assumption), you could infer that both particles had the same X and Y polarizations. (QM says you should only know one property with certainty, not two.) The trouble was, there wasn't an experiment (then) which could prove this.

Now, Bell comes along and he says something like this: if there are hidden variables, then statisticly you shouldn't violate a certain inequality (Bell's inequality [3]) when you measure different combinations of XYZ of both particles over a statistical average. For example, again, take our two particles and measure when the polarization matches if you have 1,1,-1 for XYZ spins:

particle 1 spin, particle 2 spin, match?

X,X,YES

X,Y,YES

X,Z,no

Y,X,YES

Y,Y,YES

Y,Z,no

Z,X,no

Z,Y,no

Z,Z,YES

That's 5 out of 9. Notice that if we chose hidden variables 1,-1,1 or -1,-1,1, or -1,1,-1 that you get the same. If they were all '1' or '-1', then you'd get 9 out of 9 matches, therefore you get the inequality:

matches you should get > or = 5/9, if you measure random variations over time.

In experiments, this turns out to not be true. This is what Einstein called "spooky action at a distance", which he couldn't accept. But, it happens. As soon as one particle is measured (like X spin), the other particle's X matches, and all other properties which could have matched if measured are 'poisoned'.

Disclaimer- I've left out a lot of details, and probably got a couple wrong. ;') For example, entangled photons have opposite spins not really matching, but it's easier for discussion to say they are the 'same' (i.e. predictable). I'm an armchair physicist and reciently read Brian Green's "Fabric of the Cosmos" (good book!) and Amir D. Aczel's "Entanglement", so I've got this stuff still floating around in my head. Very interesting stuff, I think!
