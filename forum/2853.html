Maybe I am wrong but with regards to gelatin swelling, I guess TEA basically

behaves like glycerol. First and foremost the swelling of the layer is

caused by incorporating water. TEA, glycerol, glycols etc. form a

very strong binding with water. I believe with glycerol e.g. it takes

some 250°C or so to get rid of the water. In other words, at the end of the

pre-swelling step (when the layer has been dried), the layer not only

contains TEA but also a significant amount of water.

Higher concentrated glycerol/water mixtures (say at 1:1 ratio) may take an eternity to stabilize a silver halide emulsion. Agfa and Ilford emulsions

treated with such mixtures (never tried that on PFG emulsions) used to

reveal a rather strange behavior: they seemed to have two different levels of swellling, leading thus to the recording of two gratings simultaneously. So one might have got both a red and a blue image on the same emulsion.

I don't know what actually causes this strange swelling behavior - maybe it relates to the "history" of the gelatin layer, to things like hardening etc.

Maybe it's linked to the specific architecture of the emulsion hence

subbing layers etc.would play an important role.

The question of broadband replay we may have to consider along those two basic paths:

- the thickness of the recording material - the thinner the recording layer,

the broader the bandwidth;

- bandwidth of the recording light source. That's where Lippmann photography is paramount.

Dinesh Sun Oct 31, 2004 2:57 pm #9
[quote:5c9bfe9b22]What is HSB?[/quote:5c9bfe9b22]

It's a different color space. It's usually called HSV("Hue", "Saturation", "Value"), but Photoshop uses HSB, substituting V for B ("Brightness").

Color is usually specified, and/or broken down, in terms of a coordinate system. This is because it can then be codified for computer graphic applications, amongst others. Usually, most people use either RGB or CMYK, the former for additive colors such as TV and the latter for printing because ink on paper is a subtractive color process. However, if you want to describe a color in terms of other constituents, you may choose a different set of coordinates more appropriate to your application. Artists, for example, may not care exactly what the RGB or CMYK components are so much as hue and brightness. They would then use a color space where the coords are these values ( [http://developer.apple.com/documentatio ... CI-48.html](http://developer.apple.com/documentation/mac/ACI/ACI-48.html) )

In my case, I'm more intersted in describing the colors of a color hologram in HSV because I think this space is more attuned to the eye as an intuitive representation of real color on a real object. I'm sort of vaguely trying to grasp what "color holography" actually means. Certainly you can always get a "color hologram" simply by using two or three colors on a panchromatic emulsion. But, in display holography, the word "color" in an "artistic" or display sense is not stressed as much as the RGB components, ie the gamut. I'm trying to somehow draw the viewer into the hologram, rather than have it stand as some abstract thing. The colors of a color hologram have mosly seemed "wrong", like early color TV's, except for some of Hans' work I think that a four color gamut is a step in the right direction, but I also think we're focusing on the wrong color coordinate system.
