Well here is another thought based on my initial response.

My initial response:

> I would have to say it is due to properties that gelatin hardens with light. Thus with longer exposures the gel is hardening as it is being developed. With shorter exposure times, all bonds are pretty much make at the same time without the gel changing properties too much during exposure.

The key here to remember is DCG exposes quickly in the beginning and more slowly near the end of optimal exposure.

Another effect is based on looking at the light struck fringe and the supposedly non light struck fringe. We have to assume we are using a diffusing, randomly polarizing object. We also have to keep in mind that as we expose the AmDi is being used up and a lot of crosslinking happens in the beginning of exposure then just a little toward the end. Near the end of the optimal exposure when most AmDi is used up and most crosslinking has already happened we get a very slow reaction (hardening).

Now, with a short exposure, the optimal exposure time is reached for the light struck fringe while keeping the incorrectly polarizing light that hits the destructive fringe from hardening it too much. Remember, the destructive area is getting very little light that is not amplifying at all with the reference beam. So not much hardening has happened there. At least not in a short time period.

With a long exposure, we are allowing the incorrectly polarized light that is hitting the destructive fringe ample time to harden it. In a 120 second exposure, the last 60 seconds of exposure is needed to get the constructive fringe it's last 20 or so optimally hardened amount allowing the destructive area ample time to use up more AmDi and get more hardened because there is so much AmDi still left and it is still in the beginning stages of crosslinking relatively quickly.

Does this make sense? If not, I can try to plot it I guess.
