JohnFP Thu Feb 15, 2007 2:19 pm #2
4fun, why do you want to make a hologram of something flat? Or do you want the flat image deep in or projecting out of the plate?

The best answer to your question about a diffuser is...take a piece of mirror roughly the size of a coin and make a hologram of it. Make sure when you do this that the light going through the plate and hitting the mirror reflects the light back to the plate, if it's reflected light misses the plate you will get nothing. Then take a painted white coin (flat white not gloss white) and make a hologram of it, there will be no need to insure it's reflections hit the plate as the diffused light should hit the plate everywhere. Make two separate holograms as ratios will be quite different and thusly exosure times may need modifications. Now compare the two properties of the two objects. It should answer all your questions. The hologam of the mirror will only be visible within the reflected light window, whereas the hologram of the white coin will be visible throughout the entire hologram.

Hope this helps!
BobH Thu Feb 15, 2007 3:36 pm #3
The grain size of the diffuser will determine how well it diffuses. Large grains will diffuse less and have more speckle. Small grains (the extreme being flashed opal) will diffuse more (and waste more light) and have less speckle.

Most diffusers will depolarize the beam. You can either readjust the beam ratio (as JohnFP suggests), or put a sheet of polarizer after the diffuser (if you have enough light left for exposure).

> why do you want to make a hologram of something flat?

Perhaps because a flat object carries information, is still a three dimensional object, and/or is a stepping stone to more complex projects?

Well, as I dust out the cob webs I shall attempt to explain what you are asking. I think it's best to draw it also to help express what I am saying.

It seem you want to make 2D 3D holograms. I have done this in the past so here it goes. I used maked collimated beams instead of cylindrical lenses.

For your tranparencies use litho or somthing similar. You want light-inpenetrable blacks. Mount this transparency to your diffusing plate and set this at a distance from the H1 plateholder that will allow correct geometry for the H2 copy. The litho should be on the holographic plate side and the diffuser on the other side. The light goes through the diffuser and is masked by the litho. This light does NOT have to be collimated.

The H1 is usually a plate cut to the width of the number of channels you want. So in this example, let's say it was and 8"x10" plate. It should be cut to 4"x10" allowing about 2" for each slit width and 10" for the slit length. The H1 in this illustration is viewed from the top thus looking 4" wide. The refence beam hitting this plate directly should be collimated. The collimated beam is 12" in diameter covering the entire length evenly and block to the 4" width. Half of the width of the the plate is masked off allowing a 2" slit to be made while the other half of the plate is unexposed.

After the exposure the Litho mask is changed and the H1 slit mask is moved to the other side and the second exposure is made.

![Image](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/2D3DRainMast.jpg)

Now for the copy. Because both slits are in allignment and on the same plate a simple H2 Tranmisison copy is done as illlustrated below. Both channels are exposed at the same time. Thus you get the two channels, diplaced from one another, changing different colors with respect to one another.

In viewing the final hologram, the one channel hologram may be green while the other is yellow, then as you move up the green channel turns to yellow and the yellow channel turns to red. Thus the two images are rainbowing different colors at the same time at the same viewing angle.

Hope this helps!

Here are some clarifications.

For a transmisison copy I would trybetween 5:1 or 10:1 light ratios.

Yes, you can use silver halide for both plates.

If the transmission H2 is created from a "slit" master then yes it will be a rainbow hologram and it will be visible with the naked eye.

I have not personally made a relief hologram with silver halide. We always used resist.

Concerning your other continued post.

I always made a large H1 in relation to the smaller H2 such that the hologram would provide a large viewing window. My 2 channel H1's were 4" by 16" and copied to a 4x5 resist H2.

Yes, you change the litho after shooting the first slit such that the two channels are different (forground and background for example).

If the distance from Litho to H1 is the mastering is the same as the distance from H1 to H2 in the copy then the litho plane (image) will be right at the hologram surface.

If you illuminate the entier H1 plate instead of a slit then you will not get a rainbow, white light viewable hologram. You will get a transmisison hologram which will have to be illuminated with a very narrow wavelength of light.

I think it is mostly preference over blocking for a slit and using a cylindrical lens. I can think of pros and cons for each.

No, the angle use to create the H1 has to be the same as the angle that illuminates the H1 in the copy set up (provided no film shrinkage and the recording and replay wavelengths are the same) but can be completely independent of the H2 angle.

And finally:

> 6) How we create H2 without H1?


Well, you could use an object and focus the image of the object with a large lens or mirror directly onto a plate. If you want a white light viewable tranmission as the final hologram then take the large mirror or lens and mask it to a slit. This will give you a single step, white light viewable, rainbow tranmission hologram.
