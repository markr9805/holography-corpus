Tommy Tue Oct 05, 2004 1:24 pm #12
The mental model I've used for optical tables is that they are filters. The big mass of the table on a spring will want to oscilate at a low frequency. Then the rigid optical mounts on the top of the table will want to oscilate at a high frequency. So movement from the ground adds energy to the entire table but only low frequency motion results, which the rigid mounts don't care about because they oscilate at a high frequency and the coupling between the two is just too inefficient for much to happen.

So in that model, a stable table would have the greatest difference between the low frequency and the high frequency.

Pondering Colin's point, would the optimal air pressure in the inner tubes be the weight of the table divided by the area the tubes cover? Any greater pressure would simply stretch the rubber, and increase the spring constant. And greater spring constant means higher frequency. (And any lower pressure would not be possible, the weight of the table alone sitting on otherwise atmospheric pressure inner tubes would create the minimum pressure.)

Dinesh Tue Oct 05, 2004 6:20 pm #14
[quote:910a86bf86]Any greater pressure would simply stretch the rubber, and increase the spring constant.[/quote:910a86bf86]

Wouldn't that depend on the bulk modulus index of air,

B= (delta V/V)/(deltaP/P)

or the fractional change in volume for a certain fractional change in pressure? Not sure what B is for air at STP, but it's probably available somewhere? Of course the limit would occur when the pressure inside exceeds the rubber's capacity to hold it, in which case, the tube explodes. However, before that limit is reached, doesn't the pressure keeps increasing. It seems true that the increasing pressure will stretch the rubber, however. So it seems as you increase the load, up to a point the air pressure increases. After that, it seems some of the energy goes to stretch the rubber and so the increase in pressure is slowed down.

Ken Dunkley's paper gives a nice analysis of it all:

"The Interferometric and Vibrational Performance of Inner Tube Isolation Tables"

Kenneth J. Dunkley, SPIE 4296, pp92-101.

My only criticism of the paper is that he assumes a flat profile for the squashed inner tubes, while I beileve it's torroidal. The difference is probably very small and I'm probably splitting hairs.
