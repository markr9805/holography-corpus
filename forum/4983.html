> fkienast: I tried making a transmission hologram of some white dice, thinking they would be a good demonstration of a 3-dimensional object. I used a simple single-beam setup, where a mirror reflects the center of the spread beam onto the dice and the plate facing the dice is struck by the side of the beam.After I developed and dried the hologram, I used a green laser pointer (with spreading lens) to look for the dice. Instead of dice, I was surprised to see several green dots. As I looked more closely I realized I was seeing the black dots on the dice! I took a closer look at the dice and discovered that although they are white, the "white" is actually a bright gray. The dots are painted with black paint, but this paint is reflective despite being black. Thus more light was reflected from the black dots than from the "white" surface, making the dots the only part of the dice visible in the hologram.

I wanted to comment but also respect the request not to discuss posts in the "Tips and Tricks" section. So I copied it here.

Frank, could you see that the dice were near black when illuminated by the laser in the set up pior to exposure? It could be that that "color" white does not reflect your particular wavelength whereas the "black" dots were shiny and reflected more light at particular angles. Illuminate the dice with your laser and if what you say is correct the dice should look black but as you move your head back and forth the dots reflect very bright spots. I did some studies of different paints with different wavelengths and found "colors" are very deceiving when using pure wavelengths.

It may also be that your exposure time was not long enough for the diffuse reflection of the dice but was long enough for the spectral reflection of the shiny black dots. Are the dots mostly visible and particular viewing angles and dim out when you move your head back and forth or up and down when viewing?

The reason I question and comment is I have, as well as many others, used dice and they seem to be a very good object. You many need to change your dice.

JohnFP Fri Aug 19, 2005 9:10 am #4
Well, optimally you want a diffuse reflection. That way each part of the plate sees a particular point on the object. You don't want the object to be shiny unless that is the effect you are looking for, not to mention polarization becomes an issue. In the case of your shiny dots, in the final hologram you will only be able to see the dots at the specific angles they are reflecting light to. Think of a point in a diffuse material acting like a convex mirror that reflect that point of light to all parts of the plate and a point in a reflective material acting like a flat mirror, reflecting light back to only one point on the plate. Yes you are right in that the reflective points are putting more light back to the plate at that point.

As you are using the center of the beam to illuminate the dice in a simple single beam transmission, I will have to stress at this point that your exposure time was not long enough. Increase you exposure and give it another go. I will go out on a limb and say at least triple the exposure as the reflective areas were visible.

You were actually lucky to have the dots shiny and see them as visible indicating a longer exposure time was needed. In the case the object did not have the shiny dots you simply may have not gotten a hologram at all and that ever lurking question would be, "What did I do wrong?"

I also bet if you look real closely at the hologram at an angle in which the dots on the dice do not show up, you will be able to see the dice very faintly.
Dinesh Fri Aug 19, 2005 4:10 pm #5
Be careful also of beam ratios. This whole field is peppered with statements like "be careful" of this or that. This is basically the point of experience. You start off simply and progress. If you tackled everything at once, you'd be swamped! I'd suggest shooting a whole variety of materials, from soft cloth to hard metals and plastics and wood. Each material will show up differently and you'll get a "feel" for what's hologenic and what's not. Also, bear in mind that the advice of experienced holographers is based on an intuitive sense of what's hologenic. Holographers will tell you that stability is not important. To some extent this is true. But only if you have a feel for "stability". The best way get that feel is to play with it. Again, some swear by overhead references and some by side reference. This is purely a matter of taste, there is no "better" or "worse". In the end it's a lot like photography. The lighting, the subject and the setup varies from photographer to photographer. There is no "golden rule" or "this is the best way".

Dinesh Fri Aug 19, 2005 7:14 pm #7
Yes, I agree about shooting soft cloth. My thinking is to bracket the parameters from <definitely does not work> to <will work in the middle of an earthquake>. In this respect, if you know what definitely does not work, you can figure out why it doesn't and then play with appropriate effects. Creating black wheels in the tyres of a car, for instance, by putting soft cloth around them.

I think the one problem with single beam transmission, and reflections, is that just a bright object beam is not enough. The object and reference have to superimpose. This is usually not well explained, so that you se a bright reflection off the object and assume it'll work, not noticing that the object beam and reference beam don't superimpose anywhere. Too bright an object may cause burn-in. You then have the effect that an apparently dim part of the object seems bright in the hologram while a bright part of the object sems dim. Usually, the knee-jerk answer is polarisation, but it could quite easily be the beam ratio. Also, a too-bright part of the object (usually the center) ends up black in the hologram. This is usually interpreted as motion (black = motion, right?). Again, this is sometimes caused by too much ratio.
