Justin W Sat Feb 19, 2011 9:13 am #1
Hi again my friends,

Those that are familiar with my most recent posts are aware that I've been fartin' around with a DLP chip lately (loosed from a silly Pico projector in the hopes of using it for stereograms).

The on-board optics were a joke; it became almost immediately clear that I needed to collect and focus the image myself.

-Talk about a crash course in beginner optics.-

The DLP chip is now Au natural - unfettered by plastic lenses and prisms.

What I think I've learned so far is that a lens of my own placed at its focal length away from the laser-illuminated DMD will focus the image seemingly at any distance. I think I can also note that a second lens a bit downstream will further expand the projected image, with its distance from the first lens determining the amount of "zoom" occurring.

Another thing I've noted that may be purely coincidental and possibly problematic is the fact that my zero-order reflection (the brightest in the center of a cluster of possibly hundreds of reflections) comes into focus right where my lens also re-aligns it with the nearest diffracted images the lens also collected. A focusing lens with a short-enough focal length to make an effective showing as a projecting lens needs to be positioned quite close to the DMD - and at that point all the diverging higher-order diffracted images are still overlapping the zero order and are impossible to card off. The result is that the focusing lens collects a bunch of images then focuses them all back to the same spot.

I'm wondering if there's really any harm in this? I'm certain it makes for a brighter object beam, and that seems good to me. However, I have to wonder if this may result in some sort of unwanted interference or possibly something else bad that I probably don't even know the name of...

Any and all insight is appreciated.

Thanks, guys

PinkysBrain Sat Feb 19, 2011 4:11 pm #4
> Justin W: What I think I've learned so far is that a lens of my own placed at its focal length away from the laser-illuminated DMD will focus the image seemingly at any distance.

Ignoring the non zero diffraction orders the beam is always purely converging or diverging, the distance of the lens or the projection plane doesn't matter ... it's always in focus. As was said before, if you focus the beam to a point you can clean up the diffraction orders with an aperture. You can also image the DLP like you are trying to do to line up all the diffraction orders, but changing the magnification becomes more involved then than simply shifting the projection plane in that case.
Justin W Wed Apr 13, 2011 2:04 pm #5
Test shot successful!

I piped a jpeg via VGA cable w/ adapter to the skeletal remains of the DLP controller board and burned a real nice bright tranny holo of said image.

As it turns out, I was mistaken about the focus of the image - when projected by a single achromat - being automatic. I fortunately had the foresight to purchase with the lens a suitable lens tube and a threaded mount. With a little bearing grease on the threads to smooth the action, I am able to focus the projected DLP image on a sheet of home-frosted glass, and in turn make a real nice bright hologram of it.

As it turns out - at least when projecting gray-scales, a Texas instruments DMD is stable enough for holographic use. MIT already knew this, but I needed to see for myself. I am using the projector guts in purely stock albeit stripped-down for racing configuration. The only modifications are the removal of the LEDs, and the removal of the DMD from the OEM optical module. So far there seems to be no necessity for reprogramming duty cycles or anything involved like that.

For (hopefully) improved resolution I will when I can afford to do so change out the long-a* VGA cable leading into my lab with a similarly lengthy HDMI cable (plus splitter and HDMI-to-mini HDMI adapter for a total of about a trillion dollars).

Next it's on to polishing my blueprints and saving toward machine shop work for the capture camera rail and moving slit mechanism. sigh*

An LCD "slit" would be absolutely awesome.

I reckon the edge blur would have a lot to do with the thickness of the LCD screen itself... An LCD with the liquid crystal on one surface that you could place against the film seems like it should eliminate the gap between the opaque masking area and the emulsion, but the little LCDs that I've had a chance to fool around with sure seem to be some sort of sandwich with the liquid crystal between. Though the glass sandwich does seem to be made from fairly thin glass...

Another (huge) concern is the polarization. To my limited knowledge, they all seem to be polarized on some diagonal, and I bet this weird polarization would eat up about half the light trying to get through. I guess you could rotate your polarization to wherever you got optimal transmittance, but that would almost certainly blow Brewster's to hell.

If an LCD slit mask could be worked out, it would certainly be a fantastic solution - no moving parts, settle time eliminated, very easily computer-controllable... Would be massively cool... I've considered experimenting with this, but here at my silly little home lab, it's doubtful that I'll make any real progress. I wish there was info out there on how to make this work.
