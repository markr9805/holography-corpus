One thing occurs to me. The LCD relies on the input light being of a particular polarisation, which is then twisted in a particular way to get an out put light. For instance the input polariser is set to polarise the (unpolarised) input into vertical so that the crystals rotate the polarisation by 90 degrees into horizontal which is just right for the output polariser set at 90 degrees to the input. If the light entering the LCD (your laser's polarisation) is at, say 20 degrees from the vertical, the crystals will rotate this by 90 degrees creating polarised light at 20 degrees from the horizontal. This will firstly decrease the intensity of the light coming out of the polariser by (cos^2)(20) and secondly may cause moire fringes. Perhaps these are the "bizarre diffraction pattern" you observed.

One way to determine this is to look unpolarised light coming through the polariser you removed with another polariser to determine its pass axis. The look at your laser with the same polariser to ensure it also is polarised the same as as the unpolarised, input light going into the LCD.

Remember also that all birefringence effects are wavelength dependant. In the case of incoherent light, this wavelength dependance may not matter too much, since it's meant for viewing and the eye is a log detector, but for coherent light from which you intend to make holograms, the response of the film is linear (until you bleach) and so lambda-dependance may show. The twist on the polarisation by the crystals may not completely align with the output polariser. It's probably a very small effect, but if you get funny results, this may have a bearing.

[quote:6d8065a13f]The "bizarre diffraction pattern" is not due only diffraction over small LCD pixels?[/quote:6d8065a13f]

Without seeing pictures, it'd be difficult to tell. To diffract 633nm through an angle of 5 degrees, the diffracting structure would be of the order of 7 microns. This would mean that the gap between the crystals would have to be about 7 microns, which would mean that the crystal size would be much larger than this so that the gap between crystals is not larger than the crystals themselves. I don't know how large these crystals are, but I would think they're not much more than about 100 Angstroms, or 10nm. This would make the difrractive structure too small to show diffraction (lambda/d <1 so that d > lambda, for HeNe, d > 633nm)

[quote:6d8065a13f]The twist polarization can be made at time hologram recording? It will not cause a small phase shift?[/quote:6d8065a13f]

The optic, e-axis of the crystal is along the long axis of the crystal. So long as the crystal orientation is in the direction of the beam, all the light is polarised perpendicular to the e-axis and there is no phase shift. There is no seperation of ordinary and extraordinary rays. In a twisted nematic cell, the crystals start off in one direction, perhaps horizontal and end up at right angles to vertical, but always with the e-axis perpendicular to the beam direction. Which actual direction the crystal "started" with depends on which polariser Michael removed. In any case, with no applied voltage, the polarisation may be parallel or perpendicular to the e-axis (again, depending on which polariser he took off). If parallel, then there will be a initial phase shift which would gradually vanish through the cell because as the crystal orientation revolves through 90 degrees, the polarisation would gradually become more and more perpendicular to the crystals and the e- and o-waves would merge. This would cuse the twist in the polarisation of the light. If perpendicular, then initially there would be no phase shift, but a phase shift would gradually appear as the crystal twisted and the polarisation became more and more parallal, thus causing a phase seperation between the e- and o-waves. So in one case, the e- and o-waves start off at the same velocity and gradually seperate create a "delayed" phase shift and in the other case, there would be an initial seperation of the e- and o-waves causing an initial phase shift that would gradually disappear. However, if the initial polarisation of the beam were polarised at an angle to the crystal direction of the first face, then there would be an initial seperation of e- and o-waves causing a phase shift which would maintain as the crystal orientation changed because the polarisation would always show an angle to the e-axis. So you effectively have a wave plate with both polarisation states transmitting simultaneously which, on exiting the crystal, would interfere. The interference pattern would be very directionally sensitive, since the phase lag would be very d-sensitive, and so the interference fringes will look a little like a moire fringe set. If a voltage were applied to the crystals so as to orientate them perpendicular to the faces, then all light would be perpendicular to the e-axis and so there would be no seperation of e- and o-waves. If, during recording, he were to apply a small voltage, the crystal orientation would be small and so he could calulate the exact change in e- and o-wave velocities to control phase shift.

I did not know that the frequency doubling in a DPSS was a KDP crystal. I would think the high voltages neceassary would create thermal effects. Colin gave a talk on DPSS lasers at the PCG meeting where he said that the major problem was temperature stability. Maybe this is the reason. Can you use the KDP as a Pockels cell? It would depend on the application and the phase shift required. The phase shift of a Pockels cell, delta(phi), is given by,

delta phi = k(n^3)r_6_3V

where k is the wave vector, n is the ordinary index, V is the applied voltage and r_6_3 is the 63 component of the electro-optic tensor. For the DPSS, the voltages and r_6_3 componenets must be carefully chosen to give the right retarndance to alter the IR to visible. For another application, I'd think you'd need to find a new "operating point" and/or a new crystal if the r_6_3 component needed a voltage such that it caused side effects such as temperature.
