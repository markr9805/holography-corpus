Joe Farina Mon Dec 22, 2008 5:43 pm #3
Using a tile cutter in a frame (i.e., from Harbor Freight or a home center, price ~$15 to $20), I blast right through the emulsion into the glass. These things are pretty much foolproof, when the lever goes down, the carbide wheel presses down through the gelatin into the glass, and locks into position. The amount of pressure is locked at this point, and you simply pull the handle towards you to make the scribe. The only thing which contacts the emulsion/plate is the tip of the carbide wheel, and the cut is perfectly straight, without any need for a ruler. These things might have a slight tendency to "crunch" the edges of the glass, but that doesn't bother me. But I do need to blow away the tiny fragments of glass which land on top of the emulsion when using this process. I just use a blow-bulb.

Joe Farina Mon Dec 22, 2008 7:19 pm #5
Hey Tony. Just wanted to mention a few things if you're going to buy a tile-cutter. I'm not sure if they have my "beloved" Harbor Freight in your neighborhood, but if they do, I would go there first. Home centers also have tile-cutters, but they tend to be more expensive, maybe running as high as $50 or so. Tile-cutters also have another feature which I typically use: a little lever which presses down (after the scribe is made) to break the glass. The only problem is that it will be pressing down on the emulsion side (which will be facing up) and will mar the surface in that area. I modified the lever on my tile cutter by grinding it down so that it's quite narrow, so that the damaged areas are minimized. Of course, you don't have to use the lever to snap the glass, you can take it out of the cutter, and use a dowell or whatever means you use.

Although I know I'm in a minority, I use the Mayer Bar method on large sheets of glass, then cut these large sheets into many smaller pieces using the tile-cutter. I take a large sheet of glass, say 14 inches square or thereabouts, place it in the laminar flow area, pour a line of gelatin near the top edge and draw the Mayer Bar across. I take care than gelatin does not spill off any of the edges. In other words, there are substantial areas of the glass (along the top, sides, and bottom) which either have no gelatin on them, or have very irregular puddles. But the middle area of the plate is fine.

After the plates dry for a while under the laminar flow, I take out the tile cutter and rapidly trim off the four edges of the plate. If the tile cutter hits a large thickness of gelatin, it makes no difference. As I mentioned above, it blasts right though. Using this method, I can produce large numbers of plates in a short period of time.

Joe Farina Mon Dec 22, 2008 8:29 pm #7
> Dutchelm05: What keeps the gelatin from pooling up?

Ooops, Tony, I made an important omission and you caught it right away. I use a water-cooled/heated aluminum plate underneath the glass when coating. I turn on the hot water, the glass gets warm, I make the coating (and let the Mayer Bar just lay there at the bottom edge, which keeps the excess gelatin from back-flowing towards the center), then I quickly flush out the hot water by turning on the cold-water tap, and the gelatin quickly gels before the left, right, and bottom pools get the chance to migrate back towards the center. That was a crucial omission, my apolgies.

By the way, my cooling plate consists of a copper coil sandwiched between two 1/4 aluminum plates (tightly held together by threaded fasteners).
