Well, I just didn't know that a real laser beam requires shaping...

I'll confess that until recently, I was used to working with generic green beams - basically collimated and unlikely to radically change size over distance.

Seems these rules don't apply to a real laser beam... The beam from my JDS apparently focuses to a tiny waist 100mm from the laser head then slowly slowly slowly diverges from there.

I'm currently up to my eyeballs in new camera geometry, and will for the first time be isolating the laser on the table with the rest of the camera - a cool thing for obvious reasons, but also a new twist in that the beam no longer travels halfway across the room before reaching my optical elements... Resultantly, object beam lenses and a spatial filter objective are left trying to deal with a frikkin tiny beam that is very reluctant to expand to use-able sizes.

Swell.

One option is to use a fistful of mirrors and a couple decrepit corner cubes to run the beam down the length of my table and back again over and over until it has diverged into a use-able size... (While tending polarization) Riiiiiiight... Let's introduce multiple unecessary optical surfaces plusloads of opportunity for flexure, torsion and vibration. Perfect!

Nope.

So that's dumb.

Hey! Beam expanders seem to be made for this very need... Convert a wee tiny beam into a larger collimated beam. Outstanding! Will do. Oh, wait... The lowest price I've found so far is $595. $600 for a tiny telescope!?! Makes me want to punch someone in the face.

Right in the face. The pricing of optics absolutely reeksof elitism.

Well, power to the people! A good while back I picked up a couple of laser line levels - look kinda like a hefty tape measure (comprised of mostly empty space plus a red laser diode beaming through a refractive grating)... Said diodes eventually died, of course, and were torn open to see what's inside. The little focusing lens (acrylic I assume) seems to have some interesting properties... Time and again those lenses in conjunction with a second lens would create collimated beams of various sizes.

And yes - that little acrylic laser diode lens 4.5cm upstream of an adorable little mounted singlet from Edmund Scientific grab bag creates a 5mm collimated laser beam!

Beam expander on a budget!

$600? Right in the face.

JohnFP Wed Dec 12, 2007 11:38 am #5
> Am I to understand that simply reflecting this kooky beam from one of those would result in a larger collimated beam?

NO

If you place that focal point of your beam (100mm) at the focal point of this mirror then you will get a collimated beam but it will only be the size of the beam when it hits the mirror. I assume this to be still quite small and that is a good thing. The maximum size of the beam will only be as large as the collimator. Think about it in reverse. If the light is collimated, that is running parallel, then it can only be as large as the collimator. You cant get a large final beam to come off of an optic that is smaller while still maintaining parallelism. Most, and I assume Dave does this too, it to use this mirror as a collimator with the very small beam diameter such that the beam diameter does not get too large.

Justin, I am not sure what you are trying to accomplish here but tightening up the path lengths allows your, converging to the 100mm point and then diverging from that point on, beam to be a smaller diameter when it hits the objective. This should be better then having it run a few meters and then be quite large and try to get it into an objective or the likes. Are you saying the beam diameter is too small for the objective? I have never heard of that.
