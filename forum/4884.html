Here's the project of the week here. I built a translational stage for my variable beam splitter. Thanks to a tip on this forum, I bought this:

[http://www.surplusshed.com/pages/item/m1527d.html](http://www.surplusshed.com/pages/item/m1527d.html)

When I got it I was a bit surprised at how large it was - but that turned out not to be a problem. I removed the round clamps that are designed to hold it to the microscope stage. I also removed the spring clip. This opened up the area for the beam splitter, which is held in place with hot glue. The entire assembly is mounted to a piece of 1" angle which has two holes drilled in it.

It works like a dream - literally dial your desired ratio while watching the numbers on a light meter!

Dinesh Mon Jun 06, 2005 12:38 pm #4
Ideally, this would be correct. In other words, if the beamsplitter surfaces were perfectly flat, perfectly clean and the material were perfectly homogenous. In reality, there's loss due to scattering at the surface, which creates a small amount of glare, and internal losses due to inhomogenities in the material. It depends on what you mean by "a lot", but a reasonably clean, well manufactured splitter set at 50:50 with a 100mW will give about 45% or so both ways and you lose about 10% worst case and about 5% on average. With a cube, the entry angle is head-on, ie zero, which minimises glare, and the reflection is caused by optical non-linear methods relying on polarisation, so internal reflections, also polarised, may create a cone on the output rather than a clean beam. But this cone will probably have a narrow angle. The crystals are usually internally "smoother" than glass. Having used both, I can't say I've noticed that much difference between a circular Newport-type variable beamsplitter and a polarising cube. However, I have seen other reflective ndf's have a lot of glare. For me, the disadvantage of the cube is that you lose flexibility. The beams have to come out perpendicularly and you need additional mirrors to steer.

I don't know about other holographers, but I rarely clean the beamsplitter, besides hitting it with an air gun every now and again.

I have one of the cheap Edmund variable beamsplitters glued to a thicker piece of glass (using optical adhesive), to make it easier to separate unwanted reflections. Light loss is really bad. Out of curiosity, I just took some measurements, and I was putting 15.6mW in, and getting 4.2mW and 4.9mW out. So the total amount of power went down from 15.6mW to 9.1mW. I guess this loss is due to the metal coating on the beamsplitter.

Quoting from the second edition of Saxby, page 150:

"A metallized variable beamsplitter may absorb as much as 40% of the incident light energy at the high-reflection end, and some is also lost by reflection ('Fresnel reflection') at the second surface."

and also:

"Inconel is a hard nickel alloy, and is used in general-purpose beamsplitters because its reflectance varies very little with wavelength; but it absorbs more light energy than aluminum (about 40%). Silver has a higher reflectance than any other metal, but it is prone to oxidation."

I am also considering changing over to a cube beamsplitter. I'll need an extra waveplate (I have two now, one for each beam after the beamsplitter) to go in front of the cube, and I'm sure there will be some losses due to it (and the cube), but I'm guessing it has to be better than what I'm using now.

Dinesh Mon Jun 06, 2005 5:08 pm #9
Just out of curiosity I tried some measurements while waiting for the laser to warm up. I must say the results are surprising and odd. (Perhaps some theoretician can try and explain them )

I use a metallised circular variable beamsplitter from Newport. I started with an arbitrary "zero" position of the variable setting (V) where I ju-u-ust got maximum reflection. I then twisted the splitter through three angles of reflection(R): 20 degrees, 40 degrees and 60 degrees. I then varied the beamsplitter by rotating it through 90 degrees and twisted the splitter again through the three angles. I repeated the procedure for rotations through 180 and 270 degrees. In each case, I read the direct beam(i), the transmitted beam (t) and the reflected beam (r). The loss(L) is the sum of i and r subtracted from the direct beam as a ratio of the direct beam:

L = (i-(t+r))/i

All readings of power levels are in mW/sq cm. Anyway, here they are:

V...............R...................i...............r...............t...............L

0...............20...............0.300.......0.243.........0.006........17%

0...............40...............0.311.......0.247.........0.006........18.6%

0...............60...............0.308.......0.255.........0.006........15.3%

90.............20...............0.319.......0.029.........0.283.........2.2%

90.............40...............0.298.......0.030.........0.263.........1.7%

90.............60...............0.308.......0.023.........0.275.........3.2%

180...........20...............0.317.......0.135.........0.081..........31%

180...........40...............0.320.......0.134.........0.078..........33.7%

180...........60...............0.318.......0.149.........0.073..........30.1%

270..........20.................0.319......0.231.........0.019...........21.6%

270..........40................0.319.......0.231.........0.019...........21.6%

270..........60................0.319.......0.231.........0.019...........21.6%

Firstly, the readings were crude, the error wasn't measured, but I suspect around 10%. However, it's interesting that the loss are pretty variable and seem to depend on the setting of the beamsplitter, being lowest at 90 degrees and highest at 180 degrees.. I did think I could have been on a "dodgy" part of the splitter, but the errors were consistent when I rotated and twisted it for a given setting. Any inhomogenities inside the splitter at any point on the surface or inside should have disappeared when I rotated it.

All these measurements posted here are totally consistent with what I found, for a variety of circular variable metallized beanm splitters (loss sometimes in the order of 50%), and uncoated surplus cubes (sometimes, surprisingly, also with losses in that order). On the other hand, with a coated polarized cube I was lucky to get off ebay, plus coated waveplates, the loss is just a few percent. And it's very simple to adjust in comparison to a circular splitter (which always tens to tilt and which also tends to introduce interference bands). So IMHO that's by far the best way to go.
