Ed Wesly Wed Aug 31, 2005 3:32 pm #3
I have one, and it is pretty good! You look down at this circle of film on the table, lit directly from above, turntable optional, and you see a woman opening up her blouse, but by the time she gets to the naughty bits her back is to you. Unfortunately I don't have a jpg of it.

The first time I saw one of these was at the Lake Forest Symposium of 1988. I asked Craig about what was his inspiration for this configuration, and he said it was his retort to the Princess Leia concept. Also it was a good use for some really big mirrors he junked out of some Applied Holographics Holocopiers.

A master for on of these things would be characterized as a stereogram with radially arranged slits. During recording, the film of the master looked through a slit at the groundglass where the film was projected. After the exposure, the film holder was rotated to the next frame.

If anyone wants to make something like this again, they would have to start building a machine from scratch. Whatever is left of the pieces of Craig's machine are languishing in a CFC storage space, and that probably is not everything.

Holodisc Wed Aug 31, 2005 9:14 pm #5
You can call them HoloDisks... I call them Holodiscs.

I'd been making various Holodiscs (and other multiplexes) since early 1976, until about 1999 when my subjectry went from POS, tradeshows, and product promos... to scientific uses exclusively. Craig's work was damn cute. The woman partially disrobing is a great example of a multiplexed reflection based on sequential frames of a woman moving about on a rotating turntable.... and yes, when she turns away, you want to reach out and turn her around.... lol

Its a fairly easy device to make, but stepper or servo controlled camera, real object turntable, table film transport and table rotator are 'pretty much' essential, although... through tedious efforts.... somewhat manually shot multiplexes such as Holodiscs can be shot. My first one was shot almost entirely manually. The 36 exposure rolls from the camera were spliced together, the turntable my model was sitting on was advanced 5 degrees or so!.... by hand between shots, she had to HOLD that pose for long seconds!, the film was cranked through a manual film plane with skids, the rotator was merely a film holder on an axle with a bearing that had a friction press as it tended to want to turn on its own... etc etc etc....

lol But, a few tries, slow devotion, and long wait times sitting on the cold concrete floor... and VIOLA!!

I haven't been able to locate actual photos of the holographic reconstructions themselves... ??? But I did find two shots... one of them the cigarette display unit with the rotating ash top holder for an 8" monochromized full aperture transmission model made for Phillip Morris, and a shot of one of the 'models' (I made 6 protos for them) in this case, a super high contrast pack of cigarettes on a prep turntable (turntable used during object preparation to get a 'feel' for the scene). Most of the other units in this particular contract were video transfers from footage they gave us (race car racing, horse running around in corral, guy smoking, girls running through garden, etc.)... this pack of cigarettes had the pack floating 1/2 down below the plane and 1/2 poking through above... as it turned a cigarette came out ready to pluck. When and if I find those pictures of the finished holograms, I'll post... in the meanwhile:

Holodisc Thu Sep 01, 2005 9:58 am #8
> Do you mean that the top of the display shown is an 8" diameter image-planed transmission hologram illuminated with monochromatic light at normal incidence? Was the image viewable at 45 degrees? That would've been an excellent image!

No, it was not an image planed transmission illuminated with monochromatic light at normal incidence.... that particular series was based on a focused full aperture white light hologram with built in monaxial phase plating to give it a monochromatic look. The tungsten filament standard Edison light bulb was located at the bottom, center of that display shown, and yes, the light was normal incidence to the hologram as the inside of the tube and the top turntable segment were lined with black velvet. The image was designed to be visible at 45 degrees, 360 degrees around the unit. The advantage of this setup was the direct film to hologram process, not necessitating an H2. We started offering this type of white light transmission in 1979. Over the 15 years it was in production, I estimate we sold about 450 of them, at an average price of $2,000. ea.

> What happened to the deal?

You mean this particular deal, as there were many?... ok, Phillip Morris paid more for their set, $36,000. for 6 sample discs and a FREE display! lol , and as a result we were going to produce one, and a backup for 1/6 that price to place in every 7/11 convenience store, this obviously would have netted quite a bundle... but the final deal fell through. My father had died from heart disease, and chain smoking and ... well, I felt guilty! So although I made the prototypes just fine, I struggled with it too much. Since then, I wish I hadn't done that and instead taken their money and did something like support Cancer R&D... but, that moment came and I did what I did. If you look at the display, you'll see what I did, as the red lips smoking the cigarette are from 6 foot under! lol Pretty sarcastic huh?... yup, and they thought so too!

Note: A percentage of the Holodiscs were 'color' or pseudocolor more accurately with overlapping spectral orders Those were more pricey (and the result was... just ok, with an added limitation of having a much tighter reconstruction cone, but the monochrome ones, being wide angle and less expensive, typically won the sale. Multiplexes of discs, planes and drums only made up a small part of our product line, I always thought of them as gimicky. (By the way, the largest Holodiscs we made were 18" dia., not in color though). Now 'entertainment gratings' and projected image scanner discs, that was different, I loved those!

> I also think, however, that credit for inventing the "Holodisk" (the thing people actually encountered and are referring to) belongs to Craig. He's the one who "whipped it out"!

Well, Holography is an interesting field all right. I think that many of the devices seen, especially in the 70's and 80's came from a number of different sources, but few withstood the onslaught of paranoia whether the Basic Patents were going to reach out and crush them or not. And investment money was tight for that reason. The way that Craig went, trying to patent the Holodisk, was not the way most Holographers were going. Most Holographers were trying to fly under the radar and not get clipped. That pretty well explains my position during the 70's and 80's... so whatever.

Holodisc Thu Sep 01, 2005 5:41 pm #10
> This technique generally uses a lens (instead of a master hologram) to focus an image into the recording plane of the final hologram, usually with full parallax. What do you mean by "full aperture" here?

(That is if you mean 'full parallax' of a flat 2D image, as it doesn't matter a whole bunch... focused on the Holoplane gives a brighter image, the stereogram encodes its own 'depth'.)

Exactly that... not verticle aperture limited as in a Bentongram or even horiz. aperture limited as in a Crossgram.

> If the hologram needed a "phase plate" (by this do you mean a grating?) to make it monochromatic, it must have been a transmission hologram.

Well yes, that particular model, as described, was a 'transmission' mode holographic encode, but certainly not a 'transmission' hologram which necessitates strictly monochromatic light per se.

> If the hologram was a stereogram recorded in a one-step system, with reference and object beams as you describe, the recording material must have been fully exposed for each perspective view. How many exposures were done to make it?

If I remember correctly, that series used 60 exposures. Photosensitive material was 8E75 (HD I believe) Processing was proprietary. Final copies were contact printed. Brightness and contrast were 'acceptable' with a 100 watt bulb, for non-direct ambient lighting viewing.

There were lots of approaches to this issue. Some units had many less frames, some units had as many as 160 frames, some units had mini-exposures, some units used diffused light bulbs, some units used Quartz Halogen slide projector bulbs, I think a few of the units had a slide projector mounted for illumination which also sequenced through standard pictures which placed a vignette image onto a diffusion sandblasted edge concentric strip of the disc so that there was a hologram floating inside a 'frame'. A couple of units, in I think '83, used 16mm closed loop projectors to make a 'dynamic frame' projected onto the edging, in fact one unit... a kind of montage idea developed by a customer that liked the dynamic frame concept, ...had small patches of diffused glass created by masked sandblasting, where a projected standard image occured while the area around those, which were empty of that image, were where the Hologram dwelt. In one example, our first demo for them, it looked like solid projected rocks, in a stream of flowing Holographic splashing water (tradeshow, and product introduction displays netted quite a bit more revenue because of all the 'extras') ..., all kinds of ideas just kept kept popping up. Word of mouth creates inertia. We did a number of waterjet cut Holograms, as an example, a Holodisc was cut into the logo of a educational supply company with fine ears (like a giant intraocular lens), spidered out to a slim disc edging. That edging rolled around in a frame with bearings, with a motorized friction wheel. The image in the logo would shift and change with the areas missing the Hologram (cut out by the waterjet) were strikenly 'empty!' of image... as often times, light hitting empty glass which isn't perfectly clean, is very distracting.

I used to keep an album of photos of these projects... but 1/2 doz facillity moves later, I have no idea where any of those are... all I can do is keep an eye out, but I always keep 6-7 self storage units full of boxes (right now its 9 units, 2 for building materials as I continue here at the second facility) so....sorry.
