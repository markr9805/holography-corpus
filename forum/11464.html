Din Sun Dec 07, 2025 1:43 pm #5
Hi Jody

Actually, we probably had the JOSA paper by Benton. Don Broadbent had a stack of JOSA papers from 1970 to 1996 (I think, I never catalogued them) which he left behind when he left the San Marcos lab. When we took over the San Marcos lab, we inherited these papers. Unfortunately, a lot of the history had to be left behind when we were forced to abandon Don's old lab, including the stack of JOSA papers.

A couple of further points:

Some of Benton's students (at least one I'm aware of) have gone into technical holography, ie out of the display milieu. In particular, Joel Kollin was a student of both Benton and Leith, and, as of a few years ago, he was in the Microsoft Research group. We (Triple Take) collaborated with the reseacrh group, and with Joel, over a number of projects in the last 10 years or so. Specifically, in the last 10 - 15 years, there has been a rising interest in AR glasses and diffractive waveguides, and Microsoft, with it's HoloLens project was getting into this field. Joel is now working for Swave, which has had a breakthrough with holographic AR glasses ( [https://www.insightmedia.info/has-swave ... akthrough/](https://www.insightmedia.info/has-swave-developed-a-holographic-display-breakthrough/) )

Which brings me to the reason I believe that there are no Benton archives. When Benton was alive, he helped many a display holographer; but, he also worked in areas of technical holography with his students and promoted technical holography. He invited several researchers from Asian countries to the SPIE conferences, believing in opening up Asian researchers to technical holography here and vice versa. Benton dabbled in display holography, but he was primarily an engineer, and interested in the engineering, physics and mathematics applications of holography. For me, it's unfortunate that today, display holographers have abandoned any technical approach to holography to the point of repeatedly stating that there should be no physics or mathematics in holography! Such a statement would have been anathema to Benton! There are conspiracy theories and belief systems with no references to actual published literature, and not even an attempt to justify these belief systems. In other words, if Benton or anyone else well versed in the technical side of holography were to arise today, she or he would be sidelined against the noise and belief systems of a social media entrenched view of display holography.

Today, Benton is passé. No holographer today, primarily in the display milieu, would be bothered to assemble a Benton archive, or read one should it exist.

Din Mon Dec 08, 2025 1:36 pm #7
Actually, I was never a display holographer as such. When I was working as an engineer programming and designing digital circuitry in 1980 I saw the front cover of a trade journal - Wireless World - showing the achromat Head of Aphrodite from MIT (although I didn't know what it was at the time). This kind of intrigued me. Then I saw Edwina Orr's advert for a course in holography and, intigued, I called her. First, there was a one-day talk by Andy Pepper on possible applications of holography. Then there was a week-long course actually making holograms. There were 4 of us in that class broken up to two doubles; I was partnered with Paul Newman, also new to holography in 1981. Also in that group was the creator of Laza Holographics; considering where Paul, myself and Laza ended up, Edwina always called us the most influential class she'd ever taught. But, I digress. At one point during the week, a laser broke down, and, with my knowledge of physics and electronics, I recognised that a capacitor had burnt out. I managed to fix it, and Edwina asked me to stay on at Richmond. Then, by an amazing co-incidence involving meeting the Guiness rep at a pub, I ended up in Applied Holographics. A little later I met Kaveh and ended up at Icon Holographics. But, I was still a physicist at heart, so I asked Kaveh, "Is this all there is to holography? Just pretty pictures?" Kaveh advised me that if I wanted technical and scientific applications I must go to America. So, I did; I called a travel agent, and booked a ticket to America. "Where in America", he asked. "Anywhere, just America". Taking the next flight to America, I ended up in Sacramento, and, a few weeks later, I ended up at NTS designing HUDs, Laser Protection Goggles and wondering whether you could record holograms on this new material in 1986 - liquid crystals - there was a need for protection against EMP's, bursts of radiation from a tactical nuclear weapon that could blind a soldier. NTS was asked whether a holographic solution existed, and, being in the research group, the problem was shunted to me. So, basically, I was always a technical holographer that got dragged into holography via display.

The point I'm trying to make is that today there is a lot of demogogic dogma concerning the mechanism and science of holography. This seems to have everyone either 'believing' in nonsense, or, for those who know better, cowed by it. This, in itself, may well be predictable in our social media environment. However, I believe that the Benton I knew would both laugh and be shocked by this dogma, especially the nasty tone by which it promulgates. Benton was an educator, and, if you truly want to study what Benton did and what he taught, you need to accept the knowledge he passed on. I have Benton's notes for his MIT course; there's a whole chapter on diffraction efficiency, and not a single statement/equation relating to any dependance on polarisation. To state that "holography has nothing to do with wavefront reconstruction" is abject nonsense! The core idea Gabor had was that you could record both phase and intensity of a wavefront ("The whole writing'), and then reconstruct the wavefront with a longer wavelength. There is little point, I think, in having a Benton archive if this dogmtic nonsense is accepted more than the teachings in the papers. It's disingenious to want a Benton archive, but accept what Benton would have thought of as rubbish.

In fact, Benton and I did discuss the physics of holography. In 1987, Benton asked me to join his group. At that time, I'd just gone through 10 years of academia, albeit in theoretical physics, not optics, so I turned hom down by saying I couldn't afford it. Benton's reply? "For someone like you, I'll find the money". In the end, i told him the real reason for turning him down. However, Mitch Henrion - who I'd just met - was studying art at Brown. She asked me what courses she needed to really understand holography. I suggested at least physics. To my surprise, she registered at the physics course at Brown - not an easy course! Impressed as I was, I pretty much dragged her to Cambridge MA, showed her to Benton, and told Benton, "That place in your group you offered me? Give it to Mitch" So he did, and Mitch ended up in Benton's group at MIT.
