If that is the question, then there are many cues to 3D vision but three main ones: accomodation,vergence, disparity.

Accomodation is the contraction of the lens in your eye. There are muscles, known as the ciliary muscles, that contract this lens and so deform it. The normally relaxed eye lens is round and focuses at infinity (which, as far as your eye is concened, is about 25 feet). the nearer the object is, the more the lens is deformed and, by a feedback mechanism, the brain reads this deformation and "backtracks" the object's position.

Vergence is the ability of the eyeball to rotate. When you see an object at, let's say 6 inches away, both left and right eyeball rotate towrds it. Once more, there's a feedback mechanism that tells your brain the amount and direction of rotation of each eyeball and your brain calculates the distance of the object from this data.

In the back of the eye, at the retina, there are a whole bunch of nerve endings that feed into the brain via a pipe. The point where all the nerves feed into the pipe is known as the fovea. When an object at 6 inches is seen by both eyes, each eye focuses an image of that object on different sides of the fovea. The exact distance of the image from the fovea depends on the exact position of the object. If the object were dead center between your eyes, the focused image on the retina will be equally on either side of the fovea. The brain knows how far away from and in what direction the image falls relative to the fovea and so can position the point in space.

Howeve actually, I think that Julien's question is the relation between the ray model of light propagation and the wave model, independant of any focusing action of lenses etc. If this is true, then rays are simply wave normals (except in a crystal). It's been known since forever that light goes in straight lines -rays - and so this model was pretty much the "standard model" for over 5000 years. It's because of this that Newton reasoned that light "must" be particles, since waves do not go in straight lines. When Young discovered interference in 1801 and Fresnel discovered diffraction in 1815, the wave model became more and more accepted. (It was always accepted in France because Descartes fervently believed in a wave theory, but nobody else dared to dispute Newton.). The situation was "resolved" by using wave theory for phenomena that were clearly wave-dependant, such as diffraction and interference - a branch of Optics known as Physical Optics - and using ray theory where it made the most sense, such as in lens and other optical instrument theories - a branch of Optics known as Geometrical Optics. In both cases, the light originated at some point and either spread out as rippes from this point - waves - or spread out as straight lines from this point - rays. The connection is that the rays are lines drawn perpendicular to a wavefront and so give you the direction of travel of the wave, since waves propogate in a direction at right angles to their front surface. They don't in a crystal because of birefringence. In this case, you have two waves tugging in different directions. The stronger "tug" deforms the round wavefront into an ellipse.

I think I know what you're driving at. Let me first start with a few generalities and then try to answer your questions specifically.

The first point to note is that any accelerating charged particle in an electric field from nearby charged particles will give out em radiation. Thus an electron-nucleus system in which the electron and nucleus oscillate with respect to each other will generate em radiation due to the acceleration of each with respect to the other. Also a free electron oscillating in the presence of other free charges and a complex electron system in a large molecule oscilating in a complex manner relative to all the other electrons in the molecule will all give outt radiation.

The other point to note is that light is generated by an atomic system going to an energetic state and then releasing it's energy via radiation. The popular books say that an electron hhas gone to a higher state, then falls back to it's ground state and releases a photon. This usually causes a lot of confusion and people frequently mix the photon picture with the wave picture. Let's just say that the atom itself "spurts out" a wave in a given direction. This wave lasts as long as the atom itself is releasing the energy, then stops. This is usualy about (10^(-8 )) secs. During this period, the em radiation is given out as a series of waves that "oscle" with a characteristic frequency and then abruptly stops. So over this period of 10^(-8 ) seconds, if the characteristic frequency is, say, 1 THz, then you get 10^4, 10,000, waves. Now this process is constantly happening to this and other atoms all around it. The average atomic diameter is about 0.1nm, 5000 times smaller than the wavelength of green light, so in a vloume of side lambda_green (500nmx500nmx500nm), you have 125,000 atoms all jiggling like mad and throwing off these oscillating bundles in all different directions over a period of 1/100,000,000 of a second each. The overall effect is of an atomic system spurting out a bunch of waves over a period of 1/100,000,000 second, then, at a later random time, another set of waves is radiated, again over the same period. This behaviour is repeated for all the atomic system nearby so that the net effect is like throwing a handful of pebbles in a pond. This "spurt" of waves is known as a wavetrain, because, I suppose, it's a train of waves . In the example of the 1THz frequenvcy, this wavetrain will consist of 10,000 waves and will be (almost) completely independant of the next or any other wavetrain. The overall length of this bundle of waves, the wavetrain, is known as the coherence length. It's predictability lasts only as long as the wavetrain itself as a maximum. That is, if you happen to catch the wavetrain at it's beginning, you can predict everything about it to the limit of the length of the wavetrain, 10,000 more waves in thre example. Of course, if you catcjh it at a later time, say 2000 waves in, then you only have predictability to 8000 waves.

So, the overall picture is a wave coming in from somewhere and striking an atomic system. The system then starts to oscillate under the influence of the electric field of the incoming wave. , which remeber, only lasts for (1/10^8 ) seconds. The atomic system then gives off a new burst of radiation, also only lasting (1/10^8 ) seconds, which moves on to influence other atomic systems downstream which in turn excite more atomic systems further downstream and so on.

In terms of observation at a given time in a given place, at P, say, the waves all arrive at P in wavetrains lasting 1/10^ 8 secs. This disturbs P for that period of time. Then the next wavetrain arrives and disturbs P again. The two disturbances are seperated by a random time and so each set of disturbances can only be predicted over the coherence time of (1/10^ 8 ) seconds. After that all information from this particular wavetrain is lost. However, considering the extremely short times involved, the observation system at P is constantly being bombarded by energy at a pretty uniform rate (in macro, non-quantum terms anyway). If you have two sets of atomic systems seperated by a large distance, then each set of systems radiate outgoing spherical wavetrains which arrive at P at different times. Thus P feels the influence of each set of wavetrains from each source seperately for the coherence time. Again,each set of waves, from whatever direction, only lasts for the coherence time. Predictability from each set is restricted to the period of the coherence time.

What if two influences arrive at either P or the atomic system downstream at the same time from two different sources? In this case, we assume that the Principle of Superposition applies. The influence of the two seperate sources is simply added. The net influence on either P or the atomic system is the sum of each individual influence. In this case, the contribution if either source is lost - only the sum maintains. Thus in an interference pattern, the exact shade of dark or light has no information on the strength of the sources that produced it. You can work out the shade of light or dark if you know the source strengths of each wave, but not the reverse.

Optical phenomena are basically staistical over the periods and distances of everday phenomena. The average value of a sinusoidal disturbance with a given amplitude oscillates for about a few hundred periods of the sinusoid, then "settles down" to a value of 1/2 the square of the amplitude. Since several hundred osccles of a light wave only last about 1/10^12 seconds, the average value of the light very quickly settles into a value of {amplitude)^2 and this is the intensity or brightness of light. Predictability of the wavetrain is determined by the length of the wavetrain, or the number of waves generated during the coherence time. For everday light, this is about 1/10^8 seconds. Lasrs have coherence lengths of several meters and so are predictable over a much longer time and space.

It's not a bad series of slides. It pretty much covers holography in a pretty nice intuitive way.

Anyway, you're quite right, there's no way to reconstruct the original waves from purely the amplitude and phase of the original waves. I think that was the one slide that was a little confusing. Partly they confused "plane" for "surface" and partly they confused the propagation of the sum from the propagation of the components.

When a whole bunch of wavetrains are all propagating, then within the coherence length, all the wavetrains in 3D space with the same phase, ie the same point on their particular sinusoid, can be connected together like a sort of iso-phase surface. This surface is known as the wavefront.

When a wavefront propagates, unless the wavefront were going through a dispersive medium, the wavefront itself is unchanged, if it's a plane wave. If it's a plane wave

psi = Aexpi(k.x-wt)

then, so long as w does not change (ie no dispersion), then it'll be Aexpi(kx-wt) forever in the same direction. Bear in mind, we're talking one wavefront which is composed of a gazillion wavetrains all along it's surface and all with the same phase. If the wave were diverging:

psi = (A/r)expi(k.r-wt)

then the inverse square law applies and the surface remains unaltered, but the wavefront along any direction is no longer the same.

The addition part simply illustrates that this may be one way of producing a new wave. Once produced, from the addition of two other waves, this new wavefront will propagate forever in the same direction if it's plane. If not, the surface maintains. In other words, the production of a new wavefront is caused (one way, anyhow) by the addition of other waves and thewn continues to propagate independantly from "it's parents". This is a seperate phenomenon from the propagation of a wavefront.

Dinesh Thu Aug 26, 2004 1:33 pm #12
We're getting into Coherence Theory here. It's a little difficult to explain without diagrams so this may sound a little convoluted.

There are basically two types of coherence, temporal and spatial. If the source gives out light all of the same color, you have perfect temporal coherence, if not any spatial coherence. You might be able to achieve this by putting a color filter in front of an ordinary light bulb. The better the filter, the smaller the number of frequencies that get through and the greater the temporal coherence, even though the spatial coherence is still almost non-existent. If the source size is small, such as a pinhole, then you have high spatial coherence, if not any temporal coherence. You can achieve this by letting sunlight through a very tiny hole. The sun itself is not very spatially coherent. The angle the sun subtends at your eye is about half a degree, for high spatial coherence, you need to subtend an angle of about 10 minutes of arc (about 1/6 of a degree). If you made a tiny hole through which you allowed sunlight and then put your color filter over this hole, you'd have both spatial and temporal coherence. The degree of spatial and temporal coherence depends on how small the hole is and how few frequencies the filter allowed through. The number of frequencies allowed through by the filter is known as the bandwidth. The laser is a source with an extremely tiny source size and a very small bandwidth and so has a high degree of both temporal and spatial coherence. The coherence length of a source depends on the temporal coherence - the narrower the bandwidth the longer the coherence length.

It's the degree of spatial coherence that determines the wavefront and it's properties. When the source radiates, a source with low spatial coherence will radiate wavetrains from all over it's area at different times. So, if the source is let's say an inch in radius and the source of each wavetrain is 1/1000th of an inch, then the area of the source is 3.141 sq inches and there are 3,141 sources of wavetrains all spurting out wavetrains at different times. It's not really possible to connect the isophase points of wavetrains given out at any time, since a lot of them are given out and emerge at random times. However, if you could narrow down the source size, then only a few wavetrains are given out at any time and now it's possible to select the wavetrains given out in a "spurt" period", follow them and connect points of isophase. A useful analogy may be runners coming out of a room. If the room had large doors so that it could fit many people across it's breadth, then many runners could run out at different intervals and it's difficult to follow a group that came out simultaneously. However, if the door were made smaller and smaller so that fewer people could emerge at any given time, it's possible to track the few that emerged at any time and follow them. If you connected the runners that came out at any given time, the line they made would be the wavefront. If you did the same with birds flying out of a hall, then connecting the birds which the birds flew out of the hall at any time over the area could be connected to give you a 3D "wavefront". You can see that in the case of wide doors (low spatial frequency), the wavefront would be quite complex and constantly changing, while with narrow doors (high spatial frequency), the wavefront is fairly well behaved

Of course, if some people, or birds, ran or flew faster than others, the "wavefront" would quickly distort . That is the line, or area connecting any group of simultaneous "exiters" would distort over time (If some ran faster than others due to different conditions of the medium - say the air was warmer higher up so birds flying higher up go faster - the medium would then be known as "dispersive"). However, if all the runners or birds had the same speed (high temporal frequency) , then the connecting line or area would not distort, but maintain it's shape. If the birds or runners flew or ran - let's stick with the 2D picture so as not to keep repeating "birds or people ran or flew" - if the people who exited simultaneously ran radially outward so they formed an arc of a circle, then this arc would maintain it's shape but expand out ward. In other words, a time lapse photograph would show succesive, expanding arcs. This is a circular - or spherical in 3D - wavefront. In a laser, with it's high spatial and temporal frequency, a wavefront pretty much maintains over very large distances, but not forever to infinity. This is why the slide says "almost". The reason it's not forever to infinity is that even a laser has a bandwidth and a finite source size - it's impossible to have a bandwidth and source size of zero - so you can't exactly track people who came out simultaneously and some people do run faster than others, very, very slightly faster, and so the wavefront will eventually distort. The better the coherence, the longer will the wavefront maintain.

The shape of the wavefront as it's generated depends on the shape of the source that generates it. A very small hole will generate semi-spherical wavefronts. If you block off the outer parts of the semi-spherical wavefront, you have an expanding arc. However, lenses and mirrors function by altering wavefronts. A lens or mirror will take an outward, expanding wavefront and cause it to collapse inward to a point ("focus"). It does this by slowing down different parts of the wavefront by different amounts. if you can precisely slow the more outward ends of a wavefront in exactly a manner that the rate of slow-down depends exactly on how far away from center the wavefront is, you can shape the wavefront pretty much any way you want. The better the lens or mirror, the better this "graduated slow down" of different parts of a wavefront. If the wavefront is not exactly slowed down right, so that a circular wavefront is made into a funny squigly wavefront, you have distortions called "aberrations" by optical types. Hpwever, if you had a perfect mirror or lens, it's also possible to slow down the ends of a wavefront so that the circular wavefront is now flat. This is known as a "plane" wave. "Plane" , because geometrically in 3D space, the semi-sphere has been converted into a flat plane. The advantages of a plane wave is that it not only goes on "forever" (depending of course on spatial and temporal coherence) but it does not fall off as an inverse square of it's distance. Also, when reflected off mirrors, a plane wavefront remains plane . In holography there's another advantage: it's its own conjugate.

When you see an object, what you register is the sum of a large number of wavefronts emanated by the objectt whose shape depends to a high degree on the light illuminating the object. In a photograph, all you record is the maximum height of the waves on the wavefront - the intensity, not their shape. In order to get a 3D view of the object, you need to record the exact wavefronts given off by the object. However, the wavefront is a connecting of points with the same phase, so you need to somehow capture the relative heights of all the wavefronts coming off different parts of the object. The object is a large source of a gazillion wavefronts and these multitude of wavefronts with their relative varying heights is what the brain percieves as 3D. One way to do this is by having a standard against which to compare all the varying wavefronts and their respectiove heights from any point to all the points at the plate. So you have a "standard" of let's say a number. Let's say 10. Now divide your object into regions, let's say 5 regions. Then the wavefront from region 1, at the plate, has a height ("phase") of 3. You subtract the standard, 10, from the wavefront height of region 1 at the plate and record 7. Region 2 has a height of 8 so you record 10-8 or 2 at the plate etc, etc for all the regions. Now you have a plate with a bunch of numbers. How do you get the respective heights from the original object back and recover the shape of the object? Simple! You know that you subtracted each height of the respective wavefront from 10, so you now simply subtract from 10 again. 7 is recorded as having come from region 1, so the original object's wavefront from a particular perspective was 10-7 or 3. You now project a wavefront which will give a wavefront of 3 to the viewer. This can be done automatically by reversing the process of adding the constant amount and this is the function of the reference beam and the recording beam. Bear in mind, however, that if you don't exactly use the same number to subtract a second time to recover the origianal numbers, then the wavefront that comes back at you is not the same as the wavefront that was recorded. This distorts, or "aberrates" as optical types call it, the object's wavefront.

I think Julien's question was - "how is the wavefront model equivalent to the rays of light model and how does the eye see in that model? "

Here's my two-bits -

They are both only models, and not equivalent, we just use one or the other to explain different things.

-and "using that (wave) model, how does the eye see?"

I don't think that's the right model, because imaging in a lens system with incoherent light is not a wave process, it's simple raytracing thru a pinhole. The waves are certainly going along for the ride in each ray, and could be computed or recorded, but they are not creating the image by coherence or diffraction alone so usually are not of interest.

- "When you record the hologram you only store information about the sum, and you lose information about the individual spherical waves."

Not so at all. Think a little bigger than a point. Each point may be a sum but that point doesn't make a hologram, you need a fair sampling of points - the more the better. The minimum set of points (film grains) would define one speckle (or fringe), and the "popcorn" shaped outline of each speckle IS a sum of the spherical wave patterns meeting at that spot. It's a SET of points that begins to record directional information. [quote:3685ab23a7]I don't think that's the right model, because imaging in a lens system with incoherent light is not a wave process, it's simple raytracing thru a pinhole. [/quote:3685ab23a7]

[quote:3685ab23a7]Now that's a good one. Is it too sloppy to say that a wavefront passing thru a lens is fourier transformed? [/quote:3685ab23a7]

[quote:3685ab23a7]Nils Abramson's "light in flight" created visuals of actual wavefronts going thru lenses[/quote:3685ab23a7]

Aren't these contradictory? In fact, light going through a lens is both a ray process and a wave process. The two are not contradictory but complementary. A wave going through a lens suffers a phase lag as it goes through the lens, but the phase lag is varied across the wavefront, due to the differing thickness of glass along the axis of the lens. Because the phase lag is directly dependant on the height above the lens axis, a spherical, diverging wavefront is transformed into a spherical convergent wavefront. Where this convergent wavefront converges to a point is the focal point. However, this is a very wavelength-dependant process. The phase of the complex wavefront, ie the height of a bunch of waves relative to all the other waves in the wavefront at any time, is an addition of all the heights of all the waves at any given point. This clearly depends on the frequency of all the waves in the complex wavefront. When this wavefront passes through a dense medium like glass, the various components of the wavefront begin to seperate out because the velocity of light through a medium depends on the "color" of the light. This causes aberrations like chromatic aberration. In order to simplify the analysis, you use a "ray model" in which you asume a perfect spherical wave with only one component. This "perfect" wavfront also has a wavelength of zero - hence no diffraction effects. Then you draw a vector perpendicular to this perfect spherical wavefront and call that a ray. You've now transformed a complex problem of differential equations into a simple vector problem. Wave analysis is used for lenses and other optical components in cases where wavelength and diffraction effects need to be considered. This is then known as Physical Optics, as opposed to Geometrical Optics. Study of lens aberrations, for instance, require diffraction effects to be taken into account and so you can't use a ray model. In fact, Fourier Transforms are exactly used by lens designers these days to calculate the spatial frequencies going through a lens and how aberrations are dependant on the spatial frequencies. There's a very good description of using Fourier Transforms to analyse incoherent light going through optical systems in Goodman"s "Introduction to Fourier Optics"

Speckle is caused by tiny irregularities on any surface. Normally, this is never noticed. However, when you shine light of exceptional coherence, such as a laser, onto a surface, this light can tell surface variations down to half a micron. The eye has a certain resolution which means that it can only see any surface within a tolerance of about a few millimeters. Thus a laser beam on a surface will interfere on the "craggy" surface seen by the laser, however, these interference lines are too small to be resolved by the eye. The eye thus sees an overall intensity caused by the actual interference lines - a lot of interefrence lines within the eye aperture (caused by rapid variations in surface profile from a particular part of the surface) will be seen as a dark "patch", while a less "craggy" surface part will not have as many interference lines on it and so the eye sees it as less dense. By the way, Martin, this theory can explain why you speckle does not change when you remove your glasses - eye resolution is a function of diffraction at iris aperture.
