From the glossary:

Reciprocity failure - in photographic emulsions occurs when exposure times fall outside a films normal range. At these times an increase in exposure is required in addition to the assessed amount. This can be achieved either by increasing intensity or time.

Reciprocity law - states that exposure = intensity x time, where intensity is equal to the amount of light and time is equal to the time that amount of light is allowed to act upon the photographic emulsion

----------------------

So the reciprocity law is linear within certain exposure times and energies. Outside the ranges there is "Reciprocity Failure". You start to worry about it in holography when you get to very short or very long exposures.

Ed Wesly Wed Mar 30, 2005 8:16 pm #10
Case study of holographic reciprocity failure:

While working at Northwestern University with Hans Bjelkhagen in 1986 we had to come up with a processing scheme to make Single Beam Reflection holograms (aka Denisyuks) using a pulsed ruby laser. We were not getting too much density during development of test exposures on a standardized object. We were keeping the oscillator voltage constant, comfortably over threshold without over-driving it, and running the amplifier voltage up from its threshold to its maximum, producing at least about a Joule at the max.

Certainly that much energy in a single pulse with a nice even 8" (20 cm) diameter spread on a 4" by 5" plate should have come up with an insanely overexposed plate. One Joule in the typical 20 nanosecond pulse width that means we were producing around 50 MegaWatts! Something was amiss! Of course, the problem was reciprocity law failure, where the basic E = I x t wasn't working.

A 200 mJ/cm^2 typical CW exposure translates to a required 1 kilowatt per cm^2 flux at 20 nanosecond exposure time. The spread beam had about 300 - 400 cm^2 area, so our radiant flux during the biggest Q-Switched pulse could be as much as 125 kW/cm^2!

Certainly there was enough light to play with! (And certainly feel free to check the math yourself! These numbers are pretty outrageous!)

When we worked at Fermilab making pulsed ruby exposures of bubble chamber particle tracks, we used Kodak D-19 doped up with 1.5 grams of Phenidone. This made acceptable unbleached holograms on Agfa 10E75 film in a Gabor in-line scheme, but that developer wasn't cutting it on SBR's.

So while Hans was out of the country on summer holiday, I was working with a student on running the exposure tests with a ton of different developers. Hans' idea was to add 1.5 grams of phenidone to any and every recipe we could find.

We were trying Benton's PAAP formula, and I told the student to try using 2.5 instead of 1.5 grams of phenidone in the developer along with the ascorbic acid and sodium hydroxide, etc., as he was doing the mixing. He replied that he already had put in the phenidone, but he didn't remember anything about a .5 being involved. He was a little out of it from the night before. So I said just add another gram just for the heck of it.

We ran the test series, and the plate turned instantly dark! After bleaching and drying, (we were using 'Chrome or reversal bleach to give an instant shrink form the long ruby 694 nm red to something easier to perceive), the plates were blank, like typical of severely overexposed plates in the develop - reversal bleach scheme!

Except for a little glint of something on the minimal expo! We were putting 4 exposures on one plate, and in one of the diffracted shadow lines from the piece of cardboard resting on top of the plate there was a nicely exposed thread.

We figured out that there must have been at least 5 or 6 grams of phenidone in the developer so we mixed up the PAAP with 6 g of the stuff. We backed up the object to lower the radiant flux, and ran the tests again, and dadgummit, this brew worked fine! Our minimal pulse energy gave results comparable to CW work!

Thus was the birth of the developer now known as SM-6, for Salim's Mistake with 6 grams. (Salim Idriss was the lad's name, are you out there?) The amazing thing is that no one, not even Hans or Nick Phillips would ever dream of adding such a radical amount of the super-additive developing agent to any existing recipe. But luckily for the world of pulsed holographers a hung over researcher stumbled into the right amount and my eagle eye spotted it. And of course never getting any credit or a dime for it.

For a more detailed write up of the developer, I will gladly email a copy of it to anyone who sends me an address to reply to.
