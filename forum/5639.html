I have just finished preliminary tests. I have been successful controlling the temperature of a HG6512MG 50mW laser diode (650nm), housed in a aluminium block, with a HTC3000 TEC controller+evaluation board. I have thus far been able to adjust the range of power between non-lasing and full power without any apparent mode hops in the reflection of a dvd. I will try to mount all the electronics in a convenient housing tonight, and tomorrow the first tests with making a hologram.

Here are the links to the parts I used:

Laser Diode:

[http://thorlabs.com/NewGroupPage11.cfm? ... al_ID=1493](http://thorlabs.com/NewGroupPage11.cfm?ObjectGroup_ID=7&Visual_ID=1493)

[http://www.mi-lasers.com/cgi-bin/shoppe ... ode_optics](http://www.mi-lasers.com/cgi-bin/shopper.cgi?search=action&keywords=diode_optics)

TEC Controller + Evaluation Board:

[http://www.teamwavelength.com/products/ ... asp?part=6](http://www.teamwavelength.com/products/product.asp?part=6)

For powering the diode I used a simple circuit with a LM317 as described in Sam's Laser FAQ.

As a temperature sensor I used a 10k thermistor. I glued (Zalman Heat Conducting Glue) the heat sink, peltier element, and diode housing to an aluminum plate, with room to spare for a ECDL setup in case that is still needed.

Total cost so far: 300USD.

A tip from Colin was very useful, to use thermally conducting epoxy for the NTC sensor. He also suggested using shielded cables for powering the diode and feedback from the NTC. Thank you Colin.

The way I did it was to find two temperature regions where the laser jumps between modes and then select the operating temperature at one in between those two temperatures. The regions where the laser hops are very easily spotted by looking at the second order reflection off a dvd. Two distinct reflection lines will be seen instead of one. I confirmed single modeness by making a 15 minute exposure on DCG. No mode hop lines were visible. Then I again made the setup with a dvd, taped a white sheet of paper on the wall and made a pen drawing over the second order reflection line. I let it run for three ours and made regular checks. The line did not move even 0.25mm. (With a mode hop, the line will move about 8mm at the distance of my dvd to the wall).

Tom B. Fri Jun 23, 2006 3:23 am #5
Hey Hans - your diode tests sound promising. I wrote the following before your last post, but some of it might still be of interest.

I think there are two separate issues here:

1) Mode hopping is the easiest to deal with - as the diode operating current or temperature varies, the diode center wavelength changes in a stepwise fashion, changing slowly between modes on the flat part of the step, then jumping to the next wavelength. When it is near one of those steps the wavelength flips chaotically back and forth between modes. There is also an amplitude change associated with each flip which can be detected with a photodiode. I have been able to see mode hops by warming and cooling a common laser pointer and bouncing the collimated beam off a reflective grating. I think a CD would work well enough, but a long throw is needed to easily resolve the hops. A DVD would produce more dispersion. For a distance of about 5 meters, I think the displacement of the diffracted dot that I saw for each mode change was about 5 mm, which I think worked out to about 0.1 nm.

The cure for mode hops is to keep the diode operating current and temperature in a stable area. What I do is stabilize the diode temperature with a passive heat sink (lump of metal) which is insulated from air currents. Then I vary the diode current near the desired operating level to find an point far from the nearest mode-hop zone.

2) Multimode operation is more problematic. Some diodes (especially high power ones) emit multiple closely spaced wavelengths at the same time. The effect of this is to produce a periodic coherence function which is visible as contour lines (sliced bread) a few mm to 1 cm apart in the hologram. By the way, most non-etaloned HeNe lasers produce at least two lines about 1-200 MHz apart. This seen as a limit to the coherence length of something like 10 inches, but as has been observed, the coherence function repeats every 10 inches so really it is the same type of sliced bread phenomenon, just with much thicker slices. I guess, working backward, that this would make the diode laser mode spacing something on the order of a few GHz.

The only thing I can think of that would force single mode operation in a stubbornly multimode diode is some type of wavelength-dependant feedback - either an etalon or a grating as used in an Extended Cavity Diode Laser (ECDL). I don't think that this would totally eliminate the need for temperature stabilization to prevent mode hopping, but I think it would help.

A crude calculation about the accuracy of the dvd mode hop test:

A mode hop shows up as a jump of the second order reflection line of about 8mm in my dvd test setup. According to the datasheet of the HL6512MG, 17 mode hops fit in 5 nm. That comes down to 0.29nm per mode hop. So the resolution of my test should be about 0.037nm per mm of shift in the second order reflection. I can visually determine about 0,25 mm by drawing a pen stripe over the second order reflection line. I see no shift in this reflection line. So, the laser should be stable within 0.037*0.25=0.009nm. This is the best I can measure at the moment. Even if this is the best I could get, this would equate to a coherence length of at least 23cm. Not bat for such a small device at 50mW. Perhaps the coherence length is much more. But I will have to measure with a Michelson setup.

> I have been able to see mode hops by warming and cooling a common laser pointer and bouncing the collimated beam off a reflective grating.

Yes, this is what I have observed. When I start at a temperature that gives me one single second order reflection line, and then start warming the diode slightly with my TEC controller. First a weak second line starts to become visible (sometimes slowly flashing). As the temperature increases, the line becomes more constant and more bright, untill both lines are equally bright. After again some increase in temperature, the original line starts to weaken and blink on and off. Eventually, at again a a slightly higher temperature, the original line disappears, and the new line remains.

> The cure for mode hops is to keep the diode operating current and temperature in a stable area. What I do is stabilize the diode temperature with a passive heat sink (lump of metal) which is insulated from air currents. Then I vary the diode current near the desired operating level to find an point far from the nearest mode-hop zone.

This is how I used to do it with my D&S laser (no TEC). But I found that eventually changes in ambient temperature would shift the diode into a mode hopping region. Usually during a long DCG exposure or during settling time. I found that behaviour frustrating and unacceptable.

I am now able to control the diode temperature within 0.01C with a cheap TEC controller. I keep the current constant within 0.1mA with a simple LM317 in constant current configuration and a 10ohm multiturn trimpot in series with a 10ohm resistor in the feedback loop of the 1.25V reference.

> The only thing I can think of that would force single mode operation in a stubbornly multimode diode is some type of wavelength-dependant feedback - either an etalon or a grating as used in an Extended Cavity Diode Laser (ECDL). I don't think that this would totally eliminate the need for temperature stabilization to prevent mode hopping, but I think it would help.

This is the reason I have ordered a 1800l/mm grating and a laser diode can opener together with my 6512 diodes, in case a TEC controller would not be sufficient.

With my D&S laser the mode hopping was such a frustrating and time consuming business that eventually I became quite skilled at recognizing mode hopping. With some practice it is even easy to spot mode hopping by looking at the speckle pattern of the laser as you adjust it's current. It can also be seen by looking at the lines caused by the diode facet/collimating mirror combination of the expanded beam. Finally, a shift in brightness can be spotted when mode hopping occurs. A michelson interferometer shows the mode hopping. But this is troublesome because the pattern can often shift due to other causes as well. But the dvd is by far the easiest and most accurate test I have tried so far.

Hans Fri Jun 23, 2006 4:30 am #8
I think that what is actually happening in these diodes is not really a multimode operation when mode hopping starts. It is more like a rapid hopping between two competing modes at certain temperatures (cavity lengths). This accounts for the blinking of the second second order reflection lines at temperatures when the mode hopping starts. I think when two reflection lines of the dvd are clearly visible that both lines are rapidly flashing on and off, too fast for the eye to see. I am now convinced that, at least with the HL6512MG, it is easy to keep the diode out of a mode hopping state when temperature and current are kept under strict control.

I don't know why some high power diodes are sold only as multimode diodes. Could that perhaps have to do with the flatness of the facets of the crystal? Or perhaps with homogenity of the dopings inside these bigger diode materials?

The big question here is: How wide is the bandwith of the laser when it does not mode hop? I guess this question can only be answered by a Michelson Interferometer test, or a SFPI (Scanning Fabry Perot Interferometer). Of course, we holographers have another instrument at our disposal: The transmission hologram. I have only done Denisyuks so far. But I propose a transmission setup with several little mirrors, positioned at varying distances from the plate. When re-playing, the coherence length could be determined by spotting the mirrors that disappear in the final hologram. If the mirrors reflect directly back at the plate, exposure times can be kept short to reduce chances of table movement due to temperature shifts in the table.

Another big question: Does the wavelength move slowly over time. My dvd tests show that this does not happen over a wavelength shift of more than 0.009nm (that is the resolution of my test). I guess in a super stable Michelson setup, this could be confirmed.

When I was experimenting with my etalon to find the adjustment that would yield the highest power output without losing the long coherence length, here is what I did and I found it to be superior in helping me get more intimate with my laser and etalon. It will be well worth the trouble.

I built a rigid interferometer. It was made on a solid aluminum plate. I attached some blocks and rods that I got from Lenox Laser [http://www.joeblox.com/pricing/eblox_Pricing.htm](http://www.joeblox.com/pricing/eblox_Pricing.htm). Those things are great as they can be reused, versatile and easy to configure different geometries. I set up a “+” configuration in which the expanding lens, beam splitter and first mirror were only about an inch apart from one another and were locked down. Then the second mirror was adjustable from one inch to 12 inches (22 inch differential) and could be locked down. Of course you could find other hardware to use but the idea is to have it locked down all on one small rigid base.

Originally I placed a piece of glass for a beam splitter just outside the laser but before the shutter. Glass only steals about 10% of the laser energy. The beam splitter directed the beam to the locked down interferometer. This allowed me to monitor my fringes while playing with the etalon. One of the lines, I cannot remember which one now, must have a sister line very close because I get the same flicker if the etalon and laser tuning is just right (or just not right). This drawing is how I had it configured in my old lab. [http://www.sketchys.com/HoloPage/HoloFi ... Table2.jpg](http://www.sketchys.com/HoloPage/HoloFiles/Illustrations/Table2.jpg) Again, I could monitor the stability of my laser, real time, during the actual exposure.

What is so beautiful about the locked down interferometer is I still have it today, 2 years later, and I can throw it on the table and direct a beam into it and it needs no settle time and is completely stable. I know for sure all movements if any are from the laser only. At least if you could monitor you fringes real time it would save you from having to process a plate in which you knew the laser hopped. Anyway, my hypothesis is based on encounters with a few diodes that produced sliced bread holograms to some degree no matter what the drive level was. There was no evidence of mode hopping (no AC noise), and they produced nice stable fringes but only at multiples of the coherence length of a few mm. Since the gain bandwidth of laser diodes is reputedly fairly broad, the simplest explanation to me was multiple longitudinal modes. Note also that laser diode manufacturers claiming SLM operation never give a mode purity spec. .

Some quick calculations as a sanity check:

Assume laser diode mode spacing to be 150 GHz and only 2 lines are present for a wavelength range (delta V) of 150 GHz. The coherence length is c/(2 * delta V) = 3 X 108 / 1509 = 0.002 m. Or 2 mm, in the same ballpark as the peak to valley spacing of the sliced bread fringes. Without going into details, the 150 GHz mode spacing would correspond to a wavelength range of about 0.1 nm, which is also of the same order of magnitude as observed from viewing mode hops.

Joe Farina Sat Jun 24, 2006 6:58 pm #20
The following paper may have some relevance to the discussion:

"Bistability and mode hopping in a semiconductor laser" by George R. Gray and Rajarshi Roy, in Journal of the Optical Society of America B, Volume 8, Number 3, March 1991, pages 632-8.

This is one heck of a paper. I could probably read it until the cows come home, and still not understand it.

Anyway, I will quote some things of interest:

"....it is found that there are two distinct regimes of multimode behavior in semiconductor lasers: one in which the modes are oscillating simultaneously and one in which they oscillate in a bistable manner. These regimes depend on the level of the injection current and on the temperature of the laser mount....The laser's bistable regime is marked by the presence of mode hopping between two longitudinal modes. Mode hopping is characterized by the random, nearly complete exchange of power between two longitudinal modes of the laser. In contrast with mode partition, which typically involves weak modes sharing power with a strong mode, mode hopping signifies a more violent competition between the modes. The two modes may have a nearly equal average power, but the nonlinear coupling allows only one of the modes to oscillate at any one time. Spontaneous emission noise then initiates the hopping between modes....we investigate the fact that both bistable and multimode (simultaneous operation of two or more modes) regimes are found experimentally for different operating points."

wler Fri Jul 07, 2006 4:06 pm #24
> Colin Kaminski: Nice work! What is the conversion between thermistor mv and temp?

In words: first compute what the thermistor resistance is in terms of the voltage - what goes in here is the 4096mV reference voltage across thermistor plus a 10K series resistor. Then use the thermistor resistance as an input to the "Steinhart-Hart Equation" to compute the temperature as 1/T = A + [B ln(R)] + [C ln(R)3] for certain numerical constants A,B,C.

All-in-all, here's the more or less self-explaining code I use in my Labview program to display the temp (this is for a standard NTC with 10K at 25 °C):

Vref=4096;

R=10000Vth/(Vref-Vth);

Temp=1/(0.001129148+ 0.000234125ln(R) + 8.76741E-8(ln(R))*3)- 273.15;

In this way, 2270mV corresponds to 20.1 °C and 2210mV to 21.4 °C.

Update: I now have complemented those noise scans over the LD current/temperature plane, with a more detailed spectrum analysis. The upshot is:

1) The noise in those diagrams is for real, and only few "black" regions remain without noise. The noise intensity varies very strongly, like 1000:1, and it is not yet clear yet whether a small noise is detrimental for holography or not.

2) Even the dark regions without any noise are not safe, as there can be multimode behavior without any noise. This happens especially at high power levels in the order of 100mW.

3) Safest appear the extended dark regions at reduced power without any noise, they are clean single mode. Power levels there are around 50mW.

I still need to check those regions with small noise with a scanning interferometer, so check the line width.

For explicit details for the Rohm diode, see [here](http://perso.orange.fr/redlum.xohp/laser/diodelasers.html#analysis).
