Hey Joe, I thought I would post here as I already have enough group logons and didn't want to sign up with google also.

When I was using a fringe locker I noticed a couple of things.

If your fringe movement (amplitude) in the short time is larger then the movement range of your piezo then you will not get a good lock. The electronics can minimize that with "jumps" to the correct same relative position but the speed at which the electronic can react will dictate fringe contrast.

If your fringe drift is larger then the movement range fo your piezo but is very slow, you will get a good lock that will jump once in a while. This isn't so bad and will give you a hologram but depending on how able the fringle locker is to bring the fringes back to the same spot after the jump will determine the contrast of your fringes. This too can be controlled with the elctronics.

Third and most important. If you have movement in your fringes without the fringe locker then the chances are that you will have the same movement after the copy hologram and totally within the fringe locker. This gives a false reading to the locker and may move the piezo to compensate for movement in the michaelson after the hologram and thus ruin the hologram on purpose. It is most important that the everthing be as stable as possible and for sure the fringe locker michaelson be the most stable items on the table.

It would not even be a bad idea to lock down the michaelson set up for the locker with heavy weights, glue and clamps (if you get my point).

Our exposures were 30 to 45 minutes. I would watch the finges without the fringe locker turned on for 30 to 45 minutes and make sure they did not move more then one fringe for that duration. If they did, it was back to securing and locking down optics even better. Once I was within the 1 fringe movement I would turn on the fringe locker. The fringes would stay completely locked for the duration of the exposure in resist.

> Joe: Also, the detectors appear to be located on the circuit board itself. So you put the whole circuit board into the fringes?

Actually yes. I take a white card and cover the entire board with exception of two holes cut out, one for each photo cell. This allows me to see pretty precisely where the fringes are in relation to the cells. The photo cell is smaller then its mount. So my holes are just big enough to open where the inner part (the actual cell) is.

> Joe: Judging by your drawing, the piezo looks like an ordinary piezo speaker.
Yes it is. It is one I took out of a smoke detector. It is important to have the mirror smaller then the white crystal part of the piezo. It is also important to remember how the piezo wants to move. It is a disk that will want to bend into a convex or concave bowl form. So with that it is also important to glue the mirror with just a single small drop of glue in the very center of the piezo white crystal part. Then to mount the crystal's back to a gimbal mount, I do the following. I take a flat metal washer in which the inner diameter is just slightly smaller then the outer most diameter of the whole disk. I put two very small drops of epoxy very close to one another on the inside edge of the washer. I then put two more drops very close together on the opposite side of the inner washer edge. This allows the disk to cup in or bow out in the center thus moving the mirror in and out. If you glue the entire back of the piezo to a flat base the piezo is restricted in it concaving and convexing movement. I hope I explained this clearly. If you look at a standard cone speaker you will notice the cone is supported by the outer ring only which allows the center part an in and out motion.

> Joe: How do you know when the piezo is centered correctly?

I turn the piezo on and move the set of photo cells until the fringes seem locked. If you then turn the pot very, very slowly one way you should see the fringes move in a direction. At some point the fringe either stops moving or the fringes jump to the next set of fringes. Either of these is the max in that direction. The same can be done the other way. Then adjust in the middle.

> Joe: Also, it's been said that horizontal fringes are better than vertical ones, since table movement is generally in the up-and-down direction rather than side-to-side.

I have never heard of that one. If your mirrors are perfectly perpendicular to the table and you get an up and down motion, there would be no change in path lengths of the mirrors. What we are trying to control here is very simply a change in the relative path lengths of the two beams. If you set up a Michelson and get a bulls eye pattern then introduce any type of motion the horizontal and vertical parts of the bulls eye fringes move exactly the same distance. So if you only look at (or set your photo cells at) the horizontal or verticals fringes you should see the same relative movement in my opinion.
