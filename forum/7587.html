sNow I have not tweaked it but here is a start. If you let me know which language you decide to use I may be able to help you with specifics. The code for the MODCOM should be correct, but please correct me if I misread anything.

So lets say you have your master module as the stepper motor and it’s DIP settings are 00000 (A) and the relay module is slave and has a dip setting of 00001 (B).

You have the film in place and the slit is below the film so that the film can be loaded and unloaded in this position. Unless you get a feedback positioner you will have to manually find this the first time (first plate). Not too hard really as I am sure you have a few percent of film length to play with. A dry run will prove most useful. In fact, you can write two small programs in which one moves the slit up a few space and one down a few spaces thus if you motor does not like to be manually moved, you can move it up or down a few time with those two min-programs until it is in the correct spot. You could then run this program with a settle time of 1 sec and and exposure of 1 second to see where it ends up. And knowing the number of slits you could move it back the same number of steps with yet another program and then tweak from there with the first two movement programs. Stepper motors I have used in the past didn't mind at all being moved manually as long as there was not power to then, so then again, it would be even easier.

Your code would be as follows (this is not basic yet, just logical) and words in [ ] are just comments. Assume the relay for your shutter is the first one (A) on the card and the documentation states the relay is normally open (no current to open shutter provided shutter is closed with no voltage to it)

You would also need to determine how far each step moves the slit.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

HouseKeeping

Send to Port x AP0M50 [Sets starting position to 0 logically, then moves the slit up 50 steps]

a=1 [ Set bmp variable number to 1]

e=0 [This is the exposure number variable]

f=25 [This is the number of exposures wanted]

Call bmp(a) [Calls the first .bmp file (bmp1)]

Write bmp(a) [Displays the first bmp1 file to the LCD]

Main

Send to port x BCA30000 [Closes Relay A (opens shutter) for 30000mS (30 seconds)]

Send to port x BOA [Opens Relay A (closes shutter)]

e=e+1 [Counts the number of exposures]

If e=f then go to END [If the number of exposure = to the preset f then exit program]

Send to port x AM10 [Moves slit up 10 steps]

a=a+1 [Sets BMP index up one]

Call bmp(a) [Calls subsequent BMP file]

Write bmp(a) [Displays subsequent BMP file]

Pause (180) [Pauses for 3 minutes]

GoTo Main (Starts back over at Main)

END

Few corrections needed. Move is reative not cumulative and we nee to home the slit afterwards. Since we cannot use a varable in the string we may have to step the motor up and then back....rats. Back to the drawing board....lol.

Hey Justin,

That little machine being eaten by the big one is not bad at all. I've owned one for a few years and did a lot of stuff on it. Its small but will do most everything a holographer needs with some creativity.

It will fit on a table in your apartment easily. The metal chips it makes would be a mess. But with a shopvac your good to go. Those come small too.

It weights about 100lbs and can be carried around without too much trouble. You could keep it on the closet floor and move it outside to work. This could not be done with my big one of course. Thats 1000 pounds.

I know exactly what your saying about things bolting together. When I first got the small mill I was amazed by the power of making 4 threaded holes on one piece and 4 countersunk holes on another and they would not only fit perfectly but you could even spin the part and use other holes and it still fits perfectly! Just like a "real" part. And thats the whole point. You can make real stuff. Just being able to line up holes and cut a slot alone makes the first machine would the money. And of course it can do a lot more.

Now, if you just need to drill perfectly you CAN do that on your dad's delta drill press. Really.

What you need to do is get a small x-y table for the press. Put your part in there and use the handwheels to move the part an exact amount. You will have to deal with backlash, but some careful planning will totally remove that problem. Next get a set of center drills. Those are little stubby drills just to get it started. Then switch to a normal bit and your good to go. You could buy all that stuff for less than 100 bucks. and it will fit on the drill press table permently, no new space needed.

[http://www.use-enco.com/CGI/INSRIT?PMAK ... TPG=INLMK3](http://www.use-enco.com/CGI/INSRIT?PMAKA=201-2826&PMPXNO=951820&PARTPG=INLMK3)

[http://littlemachineshop.com/products/p ... -390856429](http://littlemachineshop.com/products/product_view.php?ProductID=1230&category=-390856429)

This would do it for you. If you want to get even better you could put a digital readout on the table. Use a 20 dollar digital caliper and bolt it to the table.

[http://littlemachineshop.com/products/p ... &category=](http://littlemachineshop.com/products/product_view.php?ProductID=2983&category=)

That would go on the long axis of the table a smaller one would go on the y axis.

with that on the table you wouldn't have to worry about backlash either. The handwheels with still have it but the digital caliper will only move with actual table motion. For drilling that would be almost as accurate as a milling machine. Not totally but more than close enough for screw holes to line up right. Real parts!

BTW, no machine can drill bearing holes perfectly with a drill bit. A drill bit bounces around in the very hole its drilling. You need to drill close to the final size and use a boreing bar or a reamer to make it really round. You could not use a boreing bar on the drill press, its not ridged enough but a reamer would work just fine.

For bolt holes and matching threaded holes a drill bit is fine. For a good press fit with a bearing you would want to ream.
