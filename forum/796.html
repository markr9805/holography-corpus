Don Gillespie of El Don Engineering in Ann Arbor, Michigan, asked me to consider an idea of his, and wanted me to post it here on the forum also. If anyone wants to contact Don directly, I can provide his email address and/or phone number.

The way I understand it, the idea is basically to use the temperature-dependent nature of a nickel plating process to establish a nickel grating on a glass surface, using the heat generated by a holographic fringe pattern located on the glass surface, while it is immersed in a nickel electroless bath. The idea is to produce a nickel shim directly, without the need for a photoresist hologram.

This is what Don had to say:

"electroless nickel is the way to go in this application. It happens that electroless nickel will plate out on any surface that has free electrons available. This is principally any metal as free electrons, (a fluid electron sea), are what define them. A glass or plastic substrate is an insulator and by definition does not have free electrons. The outer shell is bound. If one wishes to plate an insulator, say glass or plastic, then the surface of the substrate must be made to have a conductive or metal surface put upon it. In practice this is done by vacuum evaporation or by dipping the non-conductor into a solution of some metal compound, in this instance, and generally, one having palladium in it. Thus, if the object to be plated is coated with an adhering and conformal, molecule(s) thick, layer of the metal compound, it will permit nickel to plate out onto this surface.

The important think I bring to this discussion is the recognition that the rate of nickel deposition from the electroless nickel electrolyte is a function of the temperature of the local plating environment. Therefore, if the surface of the prepared substrate is heated by the absorption of photons from a light source, namely a laser interference pattern constituting a hologram, the grating will be reproduced as a result of selective plating thickness build up.

It is a bit more involved than this, but this is the basics of it. Anyone skilled will unravel the rest.

Note; in this method the holograting is produced directly out of the plating solution as a result of minuscule

local heating and thus plating rates. Also, note; the wavelength of the light producing the holograting is of no consequence, as the grating is formed by simple heating of the local environment. In essence, this is a black body absorption thing. A higher power pulsed laser, rather then a lower power cw laser may be an advantage as it will produce a higher T-delta for a very short time interval, and this may make things more manageable. The requirement of coherence is still required as this is fundamental to the generation of the holograting.

_____________

Joe, as I mentioned in my last em, you may put this before the holo forum if you will. It is my intent to put this into the public domain. It will be interesting to see how a discussion may develop.

Cheers, Don"
Jeffrey Weil Sun Jan 05, 2014 7:35 pm #2
I see a few problems with this approach.

1st is that the electroless nickle is deposited on the master well before it goes into the tank. There's no way to even try this method. Applying the nickle is a totally separate step from plating. It's done in a fume hood, not the tank itself. You can't do it under water. But let's forget that for a minute. There's other issues even if you could get around that.

No material is so non heat conducting that you're going to get a large temp differential over such small areas. We're talking a few hundred nanometers. The heat will very quickly flow across that distance. Also, the difference in reduction of electroless nickle due to temp is small. The bright nickle forms within a few seconds after the two solutions meet on the plate. Local heat will only change this by fractions of a second. The timing needed is impractical and probably impossible.

Next, Don's talking about having lasers interfere inside heated plating tanks with the solution moving due to the pumps needed and the heat forming convection cells (also disturbed by the pump). That kind of motion will kill any interference that might of happened otherwise. The density variations within that solution are large when you're talking about things on the scale of holographic interference. Even if you could keep things still over the course of hours, yes..hours, growing nickle is slow, as the nickle grew it would block the beams from hitting the surface you're trying to locally heat. This process is self terminating well before it would be finished.

Another issue is frequency. Don has a point but he's forgetting about the color of nickel sulfamate, the main ingredient of the plating solution. It's a very deep green and you'll have to go through a few feet of it. Nothing longer then 500nm or so is going to pass at all. So you're stuck with the same laser problem as standard resist holography but worse. No matter how close you match the frequency of the laser to the solution you're going to have a lot of absorption. You're going to need a bigger laser then you would with resist, much bigger since you're trying to induce thermal effects. Now let's look at the size of a modern shim for a wide web machine. You have to cover all of that area with the absorption of the sulfamate trying to stop you and with enough power to heat metal. You'll need 100 watts or something crazy like that. There's also dissolved solids in the solution, boric acid for example, that might be too scattering for good holography.

Only 3 more to go

Nickel sulfamate is pretty expensive stuff. To do this you'll need a tank large enough to get the beams where they need to be and at the proper angles and size of coverage. That tank is going to be huge. Possibly with optical glass incorporated into the design. The jigs used to mount the shim will have to be holographically stable. This could easily cost tens of thousands of dollars per tank.

No custom imagery! Don's plan will only make simple diffraction gratings. No 3d models, 2d-3d's or stereograms. No multi beam gratings, no shaped gratings. This is very limiting. Once you have a pattern in metal you can use it for decades. The industry rarely needs new simple gratings.

Here's the last problem I see and it's a killer on it's own. There's no advantage in doing it this way other then saving the cost of the resist plate. Even if his plan could work it's still laser based interference holography. Only now you're doing it in a very hot, very humid room with fumes in the air that eat up any metal other then stainless or titanium.

Don, please don't be mad at me for writing this about your idea You were very helpful years ago when I got my SP 907.

> Joe Farina: The important think I bring to this discussion is the recognition that the rate of nickel deposition from the electroless nickel electrolyte is a function of the temperature of the local plating environment. Therefore, if the surface of the prepared substrate is heated by the absorption of photons from a light source, namely a laser interference pattern constituting a hologram, the grating will be reproduced as a result of selective plating thickness build up.

I'm not sure if the temperature difference is enough for this.

The rate constant as a function of temperature is given by Arrhenius equation:

d/(dT)(ln(K)) = E/(RT^2)

where K is the rate of deposition, E is the activation energy of the reaction, R is the gas constant and T is the temperature (of the bath, by the way, not the metal - more to say on that later). Now, integrating this gives the rate as:

K = K' exp(-E/(RT))

where K' is a constant depending on the initial concentrations of the various entities to ensure the proper redox potentials. We can now determine the variation of a small change in the rate (delta(K)) for a small variation of temperature (delta(T)) by differentiating:

delta(K) = {K'{E/(RT^2)}exp(-E/(RT))}delta(T)

Thus, the change in rate is inversely proportional to the square of the bath temperature, but the rate itself is proportional to the inverse exponential of the temp squared. The temperature variation would thus have to be fairly high to be able to substantially different for the interference areas. The "heat" caused by a laser beam is due to the very high coherence of the E field, and not just the photon bombardment. In other words, it's a field effect, not a particulate effect. In terms of heating caused by photon bombardment , the photons would have to increase the mean square kinetic energy of the electrons in the metal, but the photon is a neutral particle. Thus, I'm not sure the Boltzmann relationship

m<c>^2/2 = (2/3)kT

is valid. For the effect to be realisable, you'd need to hit only the micron or so of one fringe with a high powered laser, while ensuring that the other fringe is kept cold (due to the inverse T^2 effect). Also, the temperature of the bath needs to be in excess of about 80C in order to get a decent deposit rate, which means you cannot have variations of temperature on a plate while keeping it dunked in a 80 odd C bath. By the way, the method itself sounds quite dangerous. In this type of electroless Nickel plating, free hydrogen comes out and one of the reactants is hydrazine, which is explosive!

About the growth rate of the nickle. Forget those charts for the first hour or two of the growth. Too fast and it'll split, too slow and production is held up. When you make the final shim the growth rate is now tied to the hardness of the grown nickle. In that tank you sometimes grow slower then you otherwise could to obtain the properties you're looking for in the metal.

Thank you Dinesh for the analysis, I'm sure Don appreciates it. Also, thanks Jeffrey for the additional comments.

I really doubt that the company is using SHSG. Having done DCG (regular and dye-sensitized) and SHSG (on Russian material), I can say that regular DCG, and even dye-sensitized DCG, is much, much simpler than SHSG. The problem with SHSG is that you are relying on a Ag/gelatin layer which has been made by someone else, and its qualities can be hard to predict. Particularly, the hardness/age/swelling behavior of the gelatin layer. It is quite an unpredictable process. With DCG, the layer does need to be coated, but this really doesn't involve that much labor, one simply needs a clean hood/laminar flow area and some Mayer bars. The important thing is that you know the exact composition of the layer -- the type, age, and storage history of the gelatin.
