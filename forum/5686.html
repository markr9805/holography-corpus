There are a lot of issues with amplification.

Here is a few things that come to my mind.

guess this could be a holowiki entry:

1. The gain medium's emission cross section [http://www.rp-photonics.com/cross_sections.html](http://www.rp-photonics.com/cross_sections.html) relates to the pumping and energy storage leveling off (population inversion level) achieveable. Once gain reaches the physical conditions ripe for ASE, the depopulation increases and the gain levels off. The larger the emission cross section the lower the useful storage to be extracted. This can be modulated by temperature for example, cooling ruby and heating nd:yag will allow more extraction.

2. input beam energy density and power level also play a role in extraction efficiency and output level. optimum j/cm2 increases extraction which gives higher output.

3. signal gain is also related to rod length. The more amplifer length the higher the output. Additionally higher gain can be had from many short rods well spaced apart than from one long rod. (ASE related)

4. small signal gain is exponential compared to larger signals regarding amplification.

5. amplication of q switch pulses is different. Generally much less output due to optical pump time is much longer than signal time. after the signal all pump energy is wasted.

Let me demonstrate point 4 and 5:

6.5mj osc, 244mj amp. 32x

So for a single pulse only 3mj extracted from the oscillator, most of the energy was wasted.

in the second case notice "small signal"gain is much higher compared to the third case 61x compared to 32x.

ron olson Thu Jul 13, 2006 7:09 pm #8
To whom amplification may concern:

The issue with Nd:Glass is not so much gain related as it is medium related. Nd:YAG is a difficult crystal to grow in large boules - the Neodymium atom is replacing a smaller aluminum atom and this doping distorts the crystal making large boules very expensive and of dubious optical qulaity. Nd:Silicate glass (as used for Nd:YAG amplification) is cheap to pour and doping levels of 2-3% result in amplifiers that introduce very few detrimental effects in the beam and fewer losses in the pocketbook. Because the glass has less gain than YAG, the chances for spontaneous emission (and thus gain depletion) are reduced along with the need for anti-reflection rod coatings. Nd:YAG's chief attributes are narrow bandwidth (good for SLM oscillators); high gain and good thermal conductivity (good for high average power amplifiers). Keep in mind that what you gain financially in a cheaper glass rod, you pay back in-part with increased demands on the power supply (capacitors are expensive in their own right) but if your goal is to produce a single-shot, high energy beam with the best possible beam quality - I believe Nd:Glass is your best option.

You make some good points about Yag and Glass but obtaining higher output is also related to some of my earlier points on laser medium's inherent characteristics. Extractable energy is basically Est*area where Est energy storage density in j/cm2. small signal gain is exp(Est/Esat) where Esat is photon energy/emission cross section. So Esat for Yag is 1.82E-19/6.5E-19 or about 0.28 j/cm2. For Q-246 nd:silicate it would be 1.82E-19/2.9E-20 or about 6.28j/cm2 Since gain is related to Est/Esat and if Est is much greater than Esat the high gain causes ASE to become a problem, If Est is much lower than Esat then system losses are a problem. So for efficiency Est closer to Esat is preferred. This means that for a given gain of say 2.718 = exp(est/esat) where est and esat are the same, (I'm normalizing this) then for nd:yag Est is .28j/cm2 and nd:glass is 6.28j/cm2. Going back to extractable energy given a fixed area of rod size then nd:glass is storing more energy that can be extracted as useful energy. I hope that helps

Hans,

A good rule of thumb is not to exceed 10j/cm2 of energy density and best to keep it closer to 5j/cm2 for coating issues. This is based on typical q switch lasers of the 5 to 20ns levels. The shorter the pulse the higher the peak power and the damage threshold is reduced. Also when ordering optics make sure they also can handle this as well. So 4 joules averaged in 1 cm2 would be a good target. The Peak gaussian ouput is 2x this or 8j/cm2. So watch out for peak energy as well. This density indicates a minimum of 1 cm2 rod area. But don't forget diffraction effects and the rule of thumb there is 2x the required diameter for typical gaussian beam profiles. This would indicate about a 1/2 inch rod diameter which if nd glass and would require typical 5000 joules for a 6 inch long rod for example. Also this gives you some room incase you encounter more negative thermal lensing than you expected on the final amplifier. The use of a SBS conjugate mirror of course helps to correct some of these issues but also single pass systems also are holographic but thermal lensing muste be considered. I agree Koechner just compiles the references into his book and you do have to work through these formulas and other sited references. This response is primarily for Hans (assuming its not too late - he might have finished his project already

I wanted to mention that there are lots of areas worth consideration if you are worrying about expense in a laser project.

Optics can be _incredibly_ expensive and really awkward to source, especially when you start looking at mirrors and etalons. For a first pulsed laser project, it is definitely worth considering a route which will not lead you to an expensive dead-end when you need that all-important component later on.

I have found sourcing YAG optics is relatively cheap and easy (particularly HR mirrors, windows, prisms and q-switches) since they are abundant in industry and usable ones are retired from service regularly.

KTP crystals are also relatively common although they always sell quickly and can sometimes be pricey. YAG rods are common but tend to be expensive - often exponentially with respect to rod size!

Surplus/ebay rods are often damaged and useless even when the seller states otherwise (you can't repair them, in case you are wondering - its _way_ too expensive - I have had quotes before!) so unless you are sure of what you are getting it is perhaps wise to avoid non-trusted sources for rods.

As an example: I have a wonderful 10mm x 150mm yag rod which I got from a trashed laser, but it has a tiny pit on one end which makes it practically useless for anything other than blowing holes in stuff (and it probably wouldn't last long doing that). I inquired about repair costs for this (and other salvaged, bargain & freebie rods) which resulted in alarming responses. A minimum of $400 to refurbish a 50mm yag rod! I won't even mention the quote I got on the 10mm x 150mm rod (I hope this deters people trying to pick up 'repolishable' crystals on ebay).

Ruby rods and optics are comparable with YAG cost-wise, potentially cheaper but usually harder to source due to limited applications. Ruby is now nearly redundant in industry. Most of the optics I have found were at a good price, but ireally nteresting parts (like partial mirrors with known R%) appear so rarely. The only reliable source seems to be those military tank laser heads which are still around (but even they are getting more scarce) and retired medical lasers - although some have rods with integral mirrors which limits their usefulness.

You can also expect a difference in cost for capacitors intended for a ruby or yag laser. Ruby lasers tend to need a big bank of capacitors, but the capacitor profile is not as demanding as YAG - the pulse forming time is longer. The electrolytics I used in my first ruby laser were common photoflash types and worked ok. The maxwell capacitors I used for YAG were difficult to obtain and expensive, and if I had tried to match the electrical storage of the ruby laser it would have been _prohibitive_.

If you pick an exotic gain medium (however cheaply you might source it) with an unusual emission line for your oscillator, you could have problems getting the necessary optics. Some mirrors have very narrow wavelength response - for instance yag mirrors coated for 1320nm will allow most of the 1064 to pass in order to prevent 1064 becoming dominant. If you are forced to pick'n'mix optics you have a better chance of success with common, matched components than exotic or poorly documented ones.

My point really is, depending on what you decide upon early in your design, you could end up with disproportionate expense later on when you need to add something specific. By sticking to common materials you'll have a better chance of initial success, then you can start incorporating more unusual designs incrementally with a better chance of understanding the implications of each change, and hopefully with less expense.
