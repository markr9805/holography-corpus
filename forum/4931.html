Re the original suggestion, I'd have to say that I don't think a purely conceptual contest would really work - content is indeed sometimes important, but in art it is how well it is executed that matters to the viewer. Most times, it's the availability of a new model or technique that inspires my holograms - the conceptual content arrives (if it ever does) only after much arduous wrangling with the setup, lighting, and characters.

The nice thing about the cat contest is that it is very flexible as to content (so long as a cat is involved somehow) and cat models are fairly easy for everyone to obtain. I do plan to enter, by the way. No ideas at present, but I'm sure something will turn up.

An interesting variation on the idea would be to promote the idea of holographic sketches - drawings, paintings, or photographs (preferably stereo pairs) of an imagined holographic scene. The idea being to do everything short of actually making the hologram, and feasibility would be secondary. For example, full color could be permitted and living objects and very distant objects could also be included. Imagine that we all had access to full-color powerful pulsed laser sources - what kind of holograms would we make?

I think that it's necessary to break down the creative act into the need (or intent) of the creative act and the subject. Creativity for an architect, for example, is completely different to creativity for an artist which would be very different for creativity for a physicist. In the architect's case, I believe that the essence of the artn is not just dreaming up building designs but also fitting in the building designs environmentally and taking into account factors such as space, view, perhaps how the view will be affected by future events like growing trees, future high-rise buildings etc. In this sense, the architect's creativity comes out in the actual plans - whether or not the plans become a real building. The creativity of the architect, therefore, rests on the finished work. I must say, I've never studied architecture, but this is how I believe architecture works. On the other hand, the artist has both practical components and abstract ideas. The abstract idea of the painting of your sister's son is, as you say, meaningless. It needs to be painted or photographed in order to be realized. However, a better artist (or a more creative one, perhaps) will look at this picture or painting and maybe say, "If this picture were made with the lighting like this instead of like that, it'd enhance the sunlight and give the boy a more mischevious look." The more creative artist need not actually re-take the picture with the new lighting, he can simply state it in a magazine or to friends in a pub. However, the new artist is being creative without actually re-painting the picture. In my field of physics, creativity is generally expressed by explaining some observed phenomena. This "explanation" may be a clever manipulation of mathematics to expose a deep underlying fact about nature or simply to explain that the mysterious light in the sky is not martians, but reflections from the over side of the horizon by clouds acting as a lens. Maybe no -one can explain why the mysterious light in the dusk sky just sits there for a long time, then suddenly moves very, very fast. Perhaps a lot of people say,"UFO's!" Perhaps a creative physicist looks for other, nearby phenomena, noticed a concave cloud and says, "Aha, the cloud is a focusing mirror focusing the sun's rays from beyond the horizon. The spot of light is the image of the sun" I'd say this is just as creative, whether or not the physicist tells anyone about the explanation.

I think there's another aspect here. Perhaps this is very pertinent to the modern world. "Art" is tens of millenia old. The representation of reality, strict ot otherwise, probably came before language. However technology is probably only a thousand years old and technology as applied to art - the representation of some form of reality to evoke some form of emotional response - is only 500 to 600 years old. There is the other factor that things changed very, very slowly until the dawn of the twentieth century. Any down-to-earth person from 1677 could quite easily be comfortable in 1877. The technological revolution had changed the big cities, but in the countryside they still lived in 1877 as they did in 1677. In particular, the concept of the representation of a piece of art was exactly the same - a painting, a piece of music, a dance form etc. However, by 1900, there was a dramatic change in the technology. Now the immediate visceral reaction to the new technological representation - photographs, movies - was not, "This is a painting", but, "This is not a painting! What is that?" The "How's it done?" reaction may have occured to some, but to most, it would have evoked wonder, fear or just "Brain hurts! Take it away!" I understand that Melliere's movie of a train entering a station in 1899 caused people to rush out of the theatre in a panic. "New" was not something they experienced very often, and while "new" in terms of communication was truly "new", representations of art was intended to be interpreted as art, not as technology. People thought it was just as unholy to fly as it was to capture someone's image photographically, somehow as a ghost.

In the intervening years, as the pace of technology increased, "new" became expected. If you created a "new" camera, such a stereo camera, or a "new" movie form - panasonic, stereophonic, vistavision and all those others, it was accepted and appreciated because change itself was accepted and appreciated. Change was all around you and your dad or granddad was "old" if he couldn't operate a tape recorder or a super-8 movie camera. The more technologically innovative, the more "new", the cooler it was. To advertisers and marketeers even soap was cool because it was "new"! Soon everyone became an artist! I remember at the age of 4 being fascinated with an umbrella stuck in the ground with a bowler hat placed on the handle.

However, today, change has become too rapid for some. Today we're so overwhelmed by change that I think a lot of people are basically shunning it. They've given up trying to keep pace with "new" but simply accept it, if it's useful, and reject it if it isn't. Curiosity is gone and replaced by "What can it do for me?" I don't think people ask of HIGS, "Wow, how do you that?" anymore than they ask such a question of their DVD's, their plasma TV screens or they gazillion gigabit cell phones. If it's technological and "new", what's it do? What do you do with it? I got this question a lot when I showed HIGs to friends. Especially since they usually simply see an image of a small dead animal. Just "3D" just isn't enough anymore. Not when I see lenticulars and "4D" being advertised as a movie medium. And certainly not when the public at large believe those crystals with dolphins etched in them are holograms. Recently I've even seen "5D"! To be accepted today, I think any medium should do something so bizarre, so outrageous that the immediate visceral reaction is shock. Movies simply won't make it today without super-ginormous visual explosions, incredibly fast (milliseconds?) scene changes andnoise (not intelligent noise, just noise) in the thousand of decibels! Music seems to have to be violent or evoke violence to the mass market. On the other end, people are apparently paying 5 dollars to breathe 5 minutes of oxygen in "oxygen bars"! When I point out that a walk in the park...? The respond that this oxygen doesn't have (or is it: "does have") "free radicals" - whatever they are. No one seems to know what a "free radical" is, besides an anti-social jail escapee. You see? Bizarre sells! I'll bet that if someone makes a HIG of a fist that comes out and whacks you while playing a Beatles tune when you look at it, it'll sell a million! Porno holography looked like it was going somewhere, but even that has been overtaken by internet porn. The shock value of HIGs is simply a banal yawn. Or a shake of the head and an offhand, "That's cool" usually followed by a (usually unspoken) "What's it for?"
