Kaveh Wed Jun 21, 2006 6:45 am #3
Sounds like you have a good source of equipment, so use it to get some great results!!

The biggest problem in wavelength choice is the improper choice of green, which gives you bad yellows. Your 543 is near the optimum of 540, so I would expect very good yellows.

OK, so you are making single beam Denisyuk holograms of a Tweetie in a red wire. (I had to look up Tweetie in Wikipedia, so now I know it is yellow!)

First of all, let's make sure that your choice of wavelengths is not distorting colors. What do you see when you forget holograms, and just illuminate the object with the composite yellow beam? Do you see the correct colors? Any angle dependence?

If the objects look good in that light, then your wavelengths are good from a color rendering viewpoint, and the problem is the holographic recording.

I have a feeling that you are only recording a hologram with one of the wavelengths, and the different colors you see are while you tilt the plate in white light.

Is your recording material suitable for both wavelengths? Few are...

Have you tried making two exposures, one with green, one with red? That's the first thing to try.

Making color holograms is not easy, and attention to detail is essential.

You might find some details in my thesis on holography useful:

[http://www.focalimage.com/public/kaveh-PhD.pdf](http://www.focalimage.com/public/kaveh-PhD.pdf)

Kaveh Wed Jun 21, 2006 11:54 am #9
First of all, do you see the object more or less in the correct colors, when you illuminate with the yellow (red+green) light with no other light around?

What you are doing is superimposing two hologram made with two wavelengths. To get the original colors, you need to make sure your emulsion is not shrinking, so if you make a green recording, then using white light to reconstruct show you the image in green.

So the Green light will reflect well off the Tweetie, but not the red wire. On the other hand the red will reflect off both well. These will add together in the right proportions to give you the correct colours.

I strongly suggest you do not complicate things by adding the blue laser till you know what is going on with the two colors. Blue is usually the more difficult too.

I think that the hologram should not re-play in pure light of a yellow wavelength. However, if the yellow re-play light is a combination of red+green and thus looks yellow to the eye (but is not really yellow), it should play back the yellow image.

Yes, the hologram is a combination of a red and a green hologram. You could even expose the plate in red and green in separate exposures. But I don't know if that is advantageous to the final result.

Ed Wesly Thu Jun 22, 2006 3:28 pm #13
Sounds like you need to calibrate your materials. Try to make a series of exposures of the subject with each laser individually. The exposure series should be logarithmic; i.e. since you think lower exposures look brighter, expose for 500 microJ, 1 mJ, 2 mJ, and maybe even 4 mJ. Keep your processing times constant. Do it for both lasers.

Once you see what is the brightest for each wavelength, cut the exposure dose in half for each and try both lasers on a single plate. Then you can play with balancing them to get the best color rendition.

How much power do you have with your Gre-Ne? I think the biggest I ever saw was a 5 mW from Melles Griot! Is it polarized?

Good luck and keep us posted.

Hans Fri Jun 23, 2006 7:16 pm #15
> We are still noticing color changes depending on the incident angle of white light. We made one with just the red laser today and noticed that it looks green at shallow reconstruction angles, yellow at less shallow ones, and red when it's near the reference beam angle. This is normal behaviour of a hologram. If you look up this link: [http://www.eserc.stonybrook.edu/ProjectJava/Bragg/](http://www.eserc.stonybrook.edu/ProjectJava/Bragg/), it will become clear why this is so. Imagine the interference planes in your hologram as being thin mirrors that are all spaced apart approximately half the wavelength of your laser. When you shine white light at the original angle to your hologram, only the red component of the white light will be able to be reflected back to you because only the red light from all the parallel reflection interference mirrors will interfere constructively. All the other wavelengths will interfere destructively with reflection from the deeper interference planes (mirrors) or not at all.

Now, when you change the angle of the white light that illuminates the plate, the distance that the light travels between the parallel reflection planes is different. This allows light a different to interfere constructively.
