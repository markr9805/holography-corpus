I've read that Brewster's angle is 56 degrees, or between 56 and 58 degrees, for common glass. I did a test, and it seemed that my glass (common window glass) was closer to 55 degrees. I wonder if anyone has measured Brewster's angle, and what kind of range there is for common glass.
BobH Thu Sep 10, 2009 2:02 pm #2
The range of angles that are Brewster's Angle is as varied as the refractive index of "window" glass is. Brewster'a Angle is not a rigid requirement for reference beams. Anything near it will work just fine, but I always stayed between 50 and 58 degrees to keep the fringes on the larger side of the practical range.
Ed Wesly Thu Sep 10, 2009 3:26 pm #3
Bob is correct, the "polarizing" angle as Brewster would humbly call it, is related to the index of refraction of the material, more precisely it is the inverse tangent of the refractive index.

Don't get math phobic and let that turn you off! To calculate the Brewster angle, enter the index of refraction of the material into your scientific calculator, then press inverse, or 2nd, then the TAN button, and voila, there is the angle, out to way too many decimal places. And yes, since the index of refraction of a material varies with wavelength, the Brewster angle will vary with the color of the laser.

Of course finding the angle empirically by tilting the plate so that the polarized laser beam is minimally reflected works best, as what kind of a protractor has any precision beyond a half a degree or so.

Thanks for the responses Bob and Ed. The reason I asked about Brewster's angle is because I'm curious about the range of angles for common window glass. In particular, I'm wondering how large this range is, because I don't know. Of course, since I'm not using a collimated reference beam (I don't want to either), only one ray of light can actually be at Brewster's angle (the center of the plate). But that's just an extra reason to be careful of getting the center ray at Brewster's, since the other rays (those near the edges of the plate) will be veering off somewhat, which might cause some internal reflections.

I'm building a plateholder (actually a sculpture/plateholder which is a single mass), to rest in a roughly horizontal position on the table. I want to angle this assembly so that it's "natural" position is at Brewster's angle (in relation to the horizontal path of the laser beam).

I measured the angle for my glass using a handy little device from Harbor Freight, which seems to opertate on a "bubble level" method to swing a needle to show angles, and it works very well. With my HeNe, the reflected light off the glass was at a minimum at about 55 degrees, which is a little less than expected.

I'm glad Ed mentioned the dependence on wavelength, I forgot about that. Since I will be using 532/633 combined, I guess I will need to make an averaged angle. By the way, I finally got my two beams together and through a spatial filter. Larger pinholes didn't work, actually I needed a smaller one, 5 microns, with the Edmund singlet.

Here are some indices of refraction for BK-7, the “water-white” glass used in lenses, courtesy of Melles Griot’s Optics Guide:

476.5 nm = 1.52310

532.0 nm = 1.51947

632.8 nm = 1.51509

Regular window glass is probably in the ballpark.
