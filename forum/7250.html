A silver coated mirror maintains polarization just like a shiney object. UNLESS...you start taking your geometry out of the 2 dimensions (either fully horizontal or verticle with respect to the table top) and allow your beam to move in the 3rd dimension.

See this technique on rotating polarization with two mirror taken from Kaveh's thesis. I was blown away by it when I first saw it because not only did I need to rotate the polarization of my laser BUT IT REALLY HELPED ME UNDERSTAND POLARIZATION.

[http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustration ... irrors.jpg](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/PolarizRotateWMirrors.jpg)

Another important fact to remember is that two beam will only not interfer if they are 90 degress off polarization with respect to one another. So if the object randomly polarizes the light some of the light is exactly polarized as the light hitting the object, some is exactly 90 degrees off (this light is totally useless) but most of the light is in between these two exteems meaning it will interfere to some extent, more so the closer it is to the original polarization. BobH Fri Mar 28, 2008 5:26 pm #6
> We all normally use a transfer mirror, silver front surface flat or parabolic. Doesn't that scramble polarization as well?

"Scramble" is a bad word. Polarization orientation is affected by all reflections in the system. You just need to keep track of it as you bounce the beams around while setting up, or be prepared to adjust it at the end so that it's whatever you want at the recording plane.

Management of polarization around a complex system is a real benefit of working on a solid top table instead of sand.

JohnFP Thu Apr 03, 2008 1:01 pm #15
Polarization is needed in order for the waves to interfere. Thus a laser with 10:1 polarization will only be able to use 90% of it's light to make a hologram with an object that maintains polarization. A 100:1 -99% and 500:1 99.8%. So the higher the polarization ratio the better.

But this gets a little more complicated with diffusing objects. The object randomly polarizes the light and thus all of the light from the laser no matter what it's polarization ration is, is used to create interference patterns. But I am not sure what effects the different polarizations all creating their own set of interference patters have on different types of emulsion in creating the fringes. I have heard of others on this forum making a hologram with a very low polarization ratio, even a randomly polarized laser.

Your 500:1 will do perfectly concerning polarization but I am not familliar with the rest of the laser's specifications like coherence length, stability, etc.
