Yes full marks Colin!

The gelling takes place through hydrogen bonding between either the acid

-COOH or amine parts throughout the layer. If it gels from a concentrated solution each available hydrogen has had more neighbors from which to choose the best. When gelled from a dilute solution each active H is more likely to be stuck with just its nearest neighbor for the lifetime of the gel.

When thick wound Meyer bars are used to coat viscous liquids like PVA

solution there is usually a multisecond period while the bar lines flow out and fill in.

But typical gelatin solution is different, provided it is hot enough around 60C its viscosity even in concentrated solution ~ 15% w/v is comparatively quite low. It also has the subtle advantage that it acts as its own surfactant unlike PVA say. This means it is good at wetting and flowing over dry surfaces like warm glass. So as the meyer bar travels down the sheet the lines left by the bar fill themselves in in a couple of seconds immediately behind the bar. If the bar is pulled down rapidly, you get a thicker layer than if you pull it down slowly. -- Yes I know I should define the velocity in centimetres per second but there are just too many variables involved. You have to find out for yourself and make a note for that particular gelatin concentration for that particular gelatin at that particular temperature with that particular bar gauge. It's all too much for physicists, --- they should stay out of the kitchen and let the artists get on with it!

A few points:

Typically, you're hand starts to hurt at about 60C. So if you have to juggle with the rod after picking it up, and having to go: "ouch! ouch! ouch!", it's too hot! If it feels warm but not uncomfortably so, it's about the right temp.

We've coated 7x10 because our rods are 8" long. It's possible to get longer rods, bearing in mind that the rods have to be longer by enough to be able to grasp the ends. The longer the rod, the more spare "finger room" you need. I suppose the limit occurs when the rod is too long to keep it "straight" (ie perpendicular to the direction of coating). It's important to sweep cleanly and unifromly. This may be obvious, but when we were first starting out, we were examing flaws as we swept the rod. This caused a "jerky" kind of coating. We had to get into the habit of not looking for flaws until after the coating.

If you're coating more than a few plates (~5 or more) then it's a good idea to keep a container of hot water and plenty of paper towels and wash the work area down. As the overfill gathers and solidifies over the work area, it gets more and more difficult to lay a plate down flat or pick it up cleanly. The back of the plate gets stuck on the half-hardened goo. You can also throw the used rods into the container so the gel is washed off quickly.

Then, when we find it's wrong we decide how it's wrong. If a simple multiplication can fix it, we multiply by that number and call it the "Padiyar constant". If it depends on something (eg tempertaure), we call it the Meyer Bar Function. If none of this works, we say, "There are no exact solutions, however "best fit" numerical methods have been useful in providing a model."

It is a little tricky switching wavelengths. How I accomplished this is as follows. My set up has a rectangular aperture after the spatial filter prior to the ovehead transfer mirror and the plate. It is important to not only center the beam on the plate but the beam has to be centered in the aperature also. I leave the room and open the shutter with a computer program using the 514 wavelength. After the shutter closes I go into the table room and place a large black card blocker between the aperature and mirror/plate. I then open the shutter and dial in the laser to 457. The beam is not hitting the plate but is going through the spatial filter and aperature such that I can see the beams retangular illumination on the large black card. By tuning the x and y on the laser I can get the new wavelength to be centered in the aperature by looking at the illumination on the card without touching anything on the table. I then close the shutter, remove the large back card blocker, leave the room, wait 5 or 10 minutes and expose with second wavelength. I then process for narrow band and a shift toward red. It's a little work but I think worth it.

JohnFP Sat Jun 11, 2005 12:19 pm #22
No, I get no spillage at all over the three edges and I have gotten pretty good at getting really close to the edge. When you come up the side it allows the edge bead of the emulsion to be as close and your skill allows. Then as you go across the top being as close and you can the bead on the edge keeps the emulsion from leaking over the side edge (surface tension maybe?). Then the same holds true for the far edge, going down it allows the edge of the emulsion to be as close as you want. Then I take a paper towel and fold it up and wipe the bottom edge about 1/4 inch in.

The thing about spinning is to even out the top edge. It works well. As you notice in my holograms there is a collection of emulsion all around the edge of the emulsion line but does not cross it to leak on the edge and is very thin (the white edge area of the emulsion in the processed hologram). I lose very little holographic surface area and that area is scraped with a razor so that I can run a bead of epoxy to seal the hologram with a cover glass. These plates have not been scraped or sealed yet. As you can see in the first and third hologram, the non straight edge (which is the bottom of the plate in coating, the wiped edge) is at the right in the first and the left in the third. There is no image loss on either edge. This is also evident in some of my other hologram.
