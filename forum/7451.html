Hello everyone.

After a lot of struggling I finally managed to consistently get bright and clear holograms with MBDCG. Three different gelatines were tried and only one of them works. The non-working gelatines were one supposed to be Rousselot Photo Gelatine and one local variety (Haenseler) bovine gelatine with a bloom strength of 128-192 and an isoelectric point of pH 4.7-5.6. The latter produced some but irritating results.

The only working variety i found was Gelita photo gelatine.

A Sony SLD1239JL-54 658nm/100mW Laserdiode was used at a power range between 20-35mW for the exposures. The wavelength of this LD is very close to the absorbtion maximum of MB.

Jeff's original MBDCG papers (applied optics, HI7) were the base for all attempts. TMG was replaced with Guanidine Carbonate, which is supposed to result in 2-3 times less sensitivity, and this equals to 100-150mJ/cm2. The exposure level i need for bright denisyuk holograms is 300-400mJ/cm2. And even this is only achieved after breathing upon the plates shorthly before exposure. The plates are a nice clear blue color before exposure.

Upon Jeffs suggestion i tried bleaching test with MB/GK/Gelatine but could not get any photo reaction, again, unless breathing upon the plates.

So I assumed that the humidity level of the plates was too low and followed Jeffs suggestion and added 10% glycerine to the MBDCG mixtures. No improvement was achieved this way.

To get some better idea of the RH conditions i'm now using a hygrometer, freshly calibrated. The last batch of my plates was dried at 55% RH and used at 60%RH.

In one of the papers Jeff discusses in some detail the dependency of sensitivity and RH. According to the numbers given there it's not too likely that the small deviation of my RH conditions from the ones suggested would cause a threefold lessened sensitivity. And also the fact that the (as I learnt in the meantime) large amount of 10% glycerine did not lead to an improvement suggests some other cause for the small sensitivity. Also the fact that even 2400mJ/cm2 does not result in anything (without breathing) suggests to me that the (slightly off) RH is not the problem. How dry a plate has to be for being that blind?

As the light source i'm using far better fits the absorbtion maximum of MB than the laser employed in Jeff's text, (Ruby 694nm) i was actually expecting to need a substantially lower exposure level and not one several times higher.

A few of my test holograms were broadband but most are narrowband and show a very nice golden-copper colour. As TMG was replaced 1:1 weightwise with GK i would have thought to get some color closer to green. The good thing about this is that i can add some more GK and thus enhance sensitivity and still get some visible color despite the blue-shift.

I don't know if adding more GK would also require to add more potassium chromate and MB to keep the proportions equal. Any ideas about this?

Any ideas about possible causes for the way too low sensitivity i'm experiencing and why i have to employ my breath before each exposure? The more i think about it the less likely it seems to me that RH is the cause. It's more like the humidity is more or less ok, and that the "sensitivity boost" triggered by the breath masks away some other problem.

What are other's experienced exposure levels with MBDCG and maybe in conjunction with Guanidine Carbonate?

Thanks,

Ahmet.

I would suggest taking a look at two early papers by Kubota and others (Applied Optics 1976 and 1979). In the first one (Hologram formation with red light in methylene blue sensitized dichromated gelatin) they give the exposure level as follows:

"The energy required to obtain 80% diffraction efficiency is 150-400 mJ/cm2."

Perhaps more significant is the other paper called "Methods of increasing the sensitivity of methylene blue sensitized dichromated gelatin." They use the same film forumla as in the earlier paper, but got a further increase in sensitivity by either heating after expsoure, or by allowing a lapse of time after expsoure (i.e., an accelerated dark reaction by heating, or natural dark reaction by ageing). I think it would be of considerable benefit to rely on the dark reaction as much as possible, because MBDCG is so slow.

jeff-blyth Wed Jul 30, 2008 5:46 am #19
That white spot......a simple explanation?

Ahmet, if you take a reflection silver halide hologram on glass , chill it and then with the gelatin side unprotected you breathe heavily on it,

you get the classic red shift effect over most of the area . But that visible effect from say a green to red replay only lasts a few seconds (depending on room temperature and humidity) but over the final second or so of this visible effect of moisture swelling you get the original red shift shrinking away from the edges to a final circular patch of redness in the center. Now here you have a fringe structure on hardened gelatin that is demonstrating what must happen when you have a softer gelatin in your exposure set up that you have just breathed on prior to making your MBDCG. That last bit in the middle is still shrinking and destroying the fringe structure you are trying to make. So it finishes up as a dead white spot of jumbled up fringes after you have processed it.

Hey Jeff, that's interesting. I had not thought of it that way.

My line of thinking was as follows:

Back to my old philosophy of when we process a DCG hologram we are actually causing an effect or a change on the unexposed gelatin not the exposed gelatin. We are processing the unexposed areas. This is not to be confused with the "non object" areas.

I would suspect in his descriptions that the "springs" have broken in his object area. In what he describes as the non image part of the plate, there is exposure. In fact it averages as much exposure as the image area minus the object beam (full reference beam). Quite a bit of light. Now in the image area we have alternating no exposure and exposure. It is in these non exposure areas (destructive interference) where I suspect the problem. Those destrucive fringes are getting way less light then the non image area. The gelatin is soooo soft there that when it is swollen in the fixer/water, the loose bonds break apart due to swelling before the fixer can harden them into place. Thus on drying the destructive fringes collapse onto the constructive fringes. The gelatin in the destructive fringes is simply way to soft and underhardened.

This seems to be an intersting thought because in this case, no amont of additional exposure can remedy the problem. The destructive fringes will always recieve no light and be too soft. The only way I see on correcting this problem is to preharden or post harden the plate in such a way that the entire gelatin and more specifically the unexposed, destructive fringes get some crosslinking. If this theory is corret then either prior or after the exposure, if the hologram is exposed to the laser light while being held in the hand and being moved so no fringes are formed, the unexposed destructive fringe areas my get enough crosslinking to support the fringe structure. I would do it for about the same time as the exposure, or at least as long as it takes for the non image areas to become clear after processing.

That's my take anyway.

> . I did follow many your threads and could extract quite a bit of useful information from them. And i'll try your express drying method with cold/warm hair dryer. How long by the way did this treatment take to sufficiently simulate the dark reaktion?

Using a cold hair drier before exposure helps a lot to equalize the humidity of the plate with the humidity of the lab. This helps prevent movement of the emulsion during exposure. It helps to dry a plate that has just been coated with a hair drier because it just speeds up the experiment so much and there is a bigger chance of still having a lot of TMG in the plate.

I am still not sure if the hot hair drier after exposure helps much because I have never observed any dark reaction without hair drying. And the times I got good results with alternating hot/cold drying before developing could be because of pure coincidence. I just kept on doing it to keep one variable constant.

> What gelatine are you using and what are the the exposure levels it takes for you to get nice holograms?

With normal TMG MBDCG any exposure above 40mJ works fine with a diode at 658nm. A HeNe at 632nm needs a little more because the MB absorbs less at this wavelength. I found no difference in sensitivity with different types of gelatin. If your lab is very humid, you will find that with some types of gelatin the plate will loose it's blue colour before exposure has started. That is bad. And that is what most of my tests were for at the time. Now I have my lab humidity and temperature controlled and there is no such problem anymore. Just make sure that the humidity stays below 65% and the temperature stays above 20C and you'll be fine.

Just to expand on this: It really is difficult to over expose MBDCG. If you expose longer, you can still get a similar result by preswelling with warmer water. Often it is easier to get an even coloured narrow band hologram with longer exposures. When exposures get longer it seems as if the water temperatures where you start to see a narrow band hologram and the temperature where the hologram starts to be broad band and the temperature where you reach the yield point are further apart. I have found that this combination works with nearly any type of gelatin of similar bloom.
