Hi Ahmet, As I see it you ideally need to have the TMG or plain quanidine, free and unattached to an acid, but it can be too strong a base and can hydrolyse the gelatin. So you can rein it back a bit with a weak acid so that there is still free base there in equilibrium. Tetramethyl quanidine or plain Quanidine salts of strong acids (nitrates, chlorides, sulfates) are not likely to be of any use because there is in them no significant free base available.

Jeff

Hans Thu Jul 10, 2008 3:45 pm #33
> All plates were transparent and slightly blue before exposure. Some plates had a very uniform matted glass like look after processing.

After you took off the cover plate and after drying, but BEFORE exposure, did your plates look less blue than when the cover plate was still on. This would be a big clue. If the plates loose transparency during drying that means that the MB did not stay dissolved. This usually happens if the environment where one dries the plate is very humid. In that case the plate looses nearly all sensitivity.

I think there can only be one of two things happening with your plates:

- The plates were dried too long and most of the TMG was lost from the gelatin. OR

- The plates were dried in a too humid environment and the MB did not stay dissolved. This can be easily seen if the plate looses much of it's blue colour.

I have had trouble with this too in the past (now my lab is moisture controlled). I found that it can be solved by drying the plates in a box that also has a sponge in it that has been soaked with some 25% TMG solution. This solution does two things: There remains more TMG in the plates + the MB is less likely to get out of solution because there is less loss of TMG.
