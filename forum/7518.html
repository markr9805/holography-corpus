Ed Wesly Thu Sep 11, 2008 10:40 pm #9
I've finally gotten a chance to chime in again. I think that if what you want to know is what is the smallest thing that can be resolved at a certain distance, the same calculations apply to finding the resolution of a hologram as do finding the diffraction limited resolution of a lens! It's all about the angle between the extreme rays from the edge of the optic, or the f/number, which is like the formula for finding fringe spacing. d = lambda/sin theta, theta being the angle formed by the top of the hologram to the object point to the bottom of the hologram. This angle could vary depending if you used the big side, the short side, or the diagonal of a rectangular hologram. But as the object point gets further from the holo, the angle will become more acute, causing larger fringes or speckle, meaning less resolution.

This is just off the top of my head, as I am proctoring a test right now, without access to my library, but I think it should suffice.

you are talking about the MTF of any particular optical system (in this case its a hologram), the way to test the MTF(modulation transfer function), is by taking an object (like a 1951 TARGET,see below) and send the image thru a collimator, so its plane waves are entering the hologram parallel(as if from infinity), when the target is refocused by the hologram or lens, whatever your testing), that target image is resolved ,preferably under a microscope to visually see how low you can see these line pairs (radically and tangentially), this is the LPPM (line pairs per millimeter), or diffraction limit of the optical system. in the old days i had to test these monster lens systems, in order to sell them to the DOD (department of defense). the system needed to resolve 220 line pairs per millimeter (which is like reading a license plate of a car from five miles distance)!in some of the older holography books they used the 1951 target to check resolution.

Joe Farina Sat Sep 13, 2008 10:07 am #12
Thanks Ed and Dave. The question of resolution versus distance from film is an interesting one. Like Colin, I think this is very much worthy of discussion.

It really is a noticeable effect in the DCG holograms I've seen and made. For objects touching the emulsion, or in very close proximity to it (say a few millimeters) the resolution is incredibly high. Anyone who has worked with DCG can verify this. However, this high resolution falls off quite quickly as the distance from the film increases. The objects start to look a bit grainy. For a Denisyuk hologram, at about two to three inches back from the film, the graininess is quite noticeable.

Personally, I would approach the problem from a design standpoint rather than a technical standpoint. That is, I wouldn't include (in the same hologram) an object that nearly touches the emulsion, and other (significant) objects which are way in the back. For example, say you have two small human figures in your hologram. One is right up against the film, and looks incredibly clear. The other is three inches back and quite grainy. If there is an obvious visual connection between the two figures, this would be pretty much unacceptable in my opinion.

So to work around the problem, I would first include objects that are neither too close to the film plane, nor too far away. I would probably cluster the most significant objects in the zone that is between 1 and 2 inches back. Objects further away would be fine, but it would be better if they are of the "background" type.

These are good reasons to "think small" and make judicious use of forced perspective.

Joe Farina Mon Sep 15, 2008 1:06 pm #18
Thanks for the link, Martin. Maybe if more holographers would get interested in perspective, they would also be more interested in making their own sculpture. Above all else, this is what holography needs right now.

A great deal can be learned from the literature on perspective in painting. But it will need to be applied to three dimensional objects (this is commonly known as "forced perspective" and is seen very rarely, such as on a stage set). But while it's comparatively simple to put some background objects into perspective as a backdrop on a stage, it's much more difficult to put a whole scene into forced perspective.

But to simplify things, I visualize a forehortened three-dimensional space as box in the shape of a four-sided pyramid. The viewer looks at the inside of the box towards the inverted point of the pyramid. This is the vanishing point. (By the way, the viewer's eye must be "locked" into a particular position, and a particular distance from, the "base" of the pyramid, which is also the "picture plane" where the holographic emulsion would be located).

This box can be imagined to be a "room" or other area which, in real life, has 90 degree angles between the floor, walls, and ceiling. But to "force" the perspective we need to change this situation, hence the box begins to assume a pyramid shape. If the sides slope inward at only 2 degrees, then you would have a very slight amount of forced perspective, and a very long pyramid.

Now is where it starts to get complicated. Imagine that in the "real world" box (with 90 degree angles) that the interior is divided up into a number of small "cubes" that are true cubes. So if we had a box that was 10 X 10 X 10, we would have 1,000 cubes. If we insert our sculpture into this hypothetical space, there would be no perpsective or foreshortneing whatsoever.

But since the objective is to have a shallower sculpture, and have the scene appear much deeper than it actually is, we need to force the perspective and change our box into a pyramid. I mentioned above that a 2 degree angle (for the floor, walls, and ceiling, all going to the "vanishing point" of the pyramid) would have only a slight effect. But if we increase this to 5 degrees, the effect will be more substantial. By the time we get to 10 degrees, we receive a huge benefit, and the actual depth of a foreshortened object in the scene may only be 1/2 of its unforeshortened depth. As a result, we can compress very large scenes into very small spaces. The price we pay is that, as we move our eye outside of that single "locked position" that I mentioned above, the forced perspective becomes noticeable. The degree to which it becomes noticeable depends on the degree of foreshortening used, and the available viewing angles.

The reason why I said that it gets complicated above is because each of those 1,000 cubes in the "normal" box gets modified in a different way when the box becomes a pyramid (i.e., when the perspective gets forced). They will be much "flatter" than normal cubes (going back into space) and their four "sides" will be angled in innumerable ways. This is where the books on perspective need to be consulted, and where a lot of thought and experimentation will be required. But if you know the shape of those "foreshortened cubes" then you can fit your sculpture into them without any problems.

To clear up confusion, or at least what I think of as the crux of the problem, is how small of an object point can you see in a hologram? Practical testing would be done with a USAF (or NBS) target as Joe notes above. If many targets are placed at various positions in depth in the hologram, how far down in the chart would one be able to see at each location? Higher spatial frequencies (more line pairs per millimeter) would be seen in the closer targets, and the deeper targets would have larger bars lost to speckle.

Which is the point of the formula I had given above, the basic interference fringe spacing. And it's all about the angle between the extreme rays that define the point. As Gregg points out a couple posts above, resolution at lower f/numbers gets worse. (Depth of field does increase in photography as the f/# gets bigger, iris getting smaller, but the smallest things one can see get bigger! Lenses that are used for some sub-micron lithography for computer chip masks run at f/#'s less than unity to get incredible resolution, but obviously there is very little tolerance in the image plane for mis-placement.)

Objects near the hologram plane will have lower f/#'s. F/# in lenses is defined as focal length divided by diameter of exit pupil; in holo it would be object distance divided by the height (or width or diagonal) of the holo plate, which is the hologram's exit pupil. An object 10 cm away from a 10 cm tall holo plate would be at an f/1 position. There would be a big angle between the extreme rays for that object point, like 53 degrees, and at 633 nm that would give about .8 microns of resolution capability!

For an object one meter away from the plate, the calculated fringe spacing would be over 6 microns before the object points would be lost in speckle.

To prove to any doubting Thomases out there that resolution gets worse as exit pupils get smaller, just look at anything lit up with laser light. Make a sphincter with your thumb and forefinger and "stop down" your eye and watch the speckle get larger as the diaphragm gets smaller.

This stuff is theoretical, but it does show what resolution is possible with a hologram. The resolving power of the holographic recording material is kind of a moot point, its requisite resolution having to be capable of recording the holographic fringe pattern. The monkey wrench it can throw into the mix is its noise factor, reducing contrast, leaving the legibility of the target up in the air. And of course distortions of the layer during processing would also muck things up. This might be why the test of recording materials cited above would vary, as it might have been more of a signal to noise issue rather than true holographic resolution.

This would apply to any style of holo, reflection or transmission, and of course the ultimate resolution implies single wavelength replay, with shorter wavelengths capable of higher resolution.
