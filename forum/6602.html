Joe Farina Fri Dec 21, 2007 11:54 am #3
This is how I built my previous table, and it worked very well. However, there are some important differences in the way I did it. First, my table was small, only about 18 inches wide, by about 5 feet long. Since I was using large "flue blocks" (which are rectangular concrete blocks used for building chimneys) this means that I used a single "row" of blocks, with VERY heavy (threaded) steel tie-bars to hold them tightly together. If struck, the assembly would "ring." Secondly, I placed a large granite slab on top of the whole thing (the kind used for kitchen counters). Below the blocks I placed innner tubes (between the blocks and concrete floor).

On the downside, the table was at a low level, about 2 feet off the ground, and it might be difficult to re-inflate the inner tubes (now I use raquetballs which need no inflation). But on the plus side, you can disassemble the whole thing and move it. My current table has an understructure made entirely of steel, with the granite on top of that, so the working surface is raised up to a more comfortable level.

> ErichRose: Next big question: can anyone list the pros and cons of automobile innertubes versus truck air springs versus "handballs?"

The main con to innertubes is they leak. I constantly have problems with mine.

A truck air spring is expensive.

It never occured to me to use racket balls.

The most important thing I find is pressure. Too much is bad. Too little is bad. The right amount seems to be dependent on your table weight and innertube contact surface area. The pressure you need for 4 innertubes is different than the pressure you need for 6. I think it was Lon who told me you should inflate your innertubes to raise the table 2". That number works for both of my tables.

I am sure your table's fundemental resonant frequency has to fit into this calculation as well. So since you can not change the pressure of racket balls, you would need to get the right number of them. I try to stay away from rubber on my table. However I am using innertubes.

BobH Fri Dec 21, 2007 8:51 pm #10
I've described my 4x8' tables made of cinder blocks with steel plate and breadboard tops here in the past, floating on three or four inner tubes (from fork lift tires) or on tennis balls (an idea I got from Ken Haines). They all work fine if you can live with the excentricities of each type. I always rubbed the burrs off the cinder blocks before building the tables to decrease their stress when cinched together, and made the top of the blocks as flat as possible to minimize the thickness of filler material between them and the top.

Lay a piece of plastic film between the filler material and the top so you don't stick the top to the blocks for easier disassembly. I never used hexcel because the skin is too thin to adequately support heavy loads locally (in my opinion). A 4x8' optical breadboard with the threaded holes is best for a top as it's 3/16" skin is locally rigid, the surface is flat, and it can be moved by two men with piano dollies.

I like laying the cinder blocks out interlaced like a wall on its side, and running the threaded rods parallel to the short dimension of the table to minimize their length.

I thought I'd post some pics of old tables I described earlier in this thread. The one on the right above is the first I built in a bedroom of my apartment in East Palo Alto in 1982. I was on the ground floor slab in the corner of a three floor apartment building, and routinely did exposures up to around five minutes if I remember correctly with a 30mW HeNe laser.

The pic below shows the table I built in 2003 in a metal sculptor's studio in San Jose. The rods run across the table instead of lengthwise, and I used three legs instead of four. I fully supported the plywood base while laying out the cinder blocks to make them as flat as I could before squeezing them together.
