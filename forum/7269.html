Wow Sweet. When it is done I will put it in the Wiki. Hopefully all the DCGers out there will contribute. I will do my best to answer all of these and agian hopefully others will chime in with corections or additions.

AmDi concentration - Lower redder, higher bluer

Emulsion thickness - thicker more narrow banded, thinner more broadband

Emulsion temp while mixing - too low deforms on emulsion, too high pits, optimal centered at 120F

Film freshness (film age) - young broadband with milky tendeny, old narrow band and clear tendency

Room Temp (exposure) - ?

Room Humidity (exposure) - ?

Exposure Time - longer harder bluer narrower banded, shorter softer redder broader banded

Dark reaction time - longer harder bluer narrower banded, shorter softer redder broader banded

Fixer Time - too short milky soft , too long harder bluer

Light or heat fixing (if applicable) - low light/heat softer broader banded milky, lots light harder bluer narrow band

H2O rinse time - too short non clear film

H2O rinse temperature - hotter broadband, lower narrow band

IPA concentrations (ratios) - faster increase in ratios (35, 100) broadband, slow increase (30, 50, 70, 90, 100) narrower banded, modifying 50% from 35- 50% shifts color towards blue.

IPA time - shorter broadband, longer narrow band

IPA temperature - higher broadband, lower narrowband

Blow drying - too slow uneven blotches, to short degradation of holo over time

Joe Farina Wed Apr 09, 2008 4:29 pm #4
I'm kind of bogged down with work at the moment, but regarding the "moisture content of the emulsion" I think it's well-known that a final DCG layer should be quite dry prior to sealing. According to Rallison, if moisture is sealed in, then the hologram might have irregular color-shifts over time. Also, Jeff Blyth has indicated that a very dry DCG layer doesn't really need sealing at all. (But in my case I would seal it anyway to protect it from water-droplets, and just to be on the safe side.) I can confirm this, as I have an old DCG hologram (of some coins) that I didn't seal, and it has been stored in my damp basement for a long time, without any adverse effect. (The kind of dryness that Jeff was referring to was leaving the DCG hologram in a desiccator for a number of days.)

Really there is a lot of stuff in the literature reagrding the questions you raised, Tony, and it would be quite a project to really get into it. The most valuable information is from fellow DCG holographers.

You have to think about what brightness is. Take for example making a master and making a hologram for viewing. The master would like to be very narrow banded tuned to the wavelength you are shooting thus making a very bright and clean replay in the laser. This would not look real bright in white light if you were viewing it. On the other hand, a viewable hologram can be very broadband and look extreemly bright but may not perform so well as a master.

If you are trying to get the brightest reflection hologram try for a broadband hologram. I would have to say the holograms that "look" the brightest to me are the broadband gold/yellow holograms. Even though common sense says a white hologram should replay brighter in white light, I personally don't see it. I think it may have something to do with the wavelengths of light. Joe Farina Sun Apr 13, 2008 10:01 am #24
I suppose "dissolve" would be technically correct, but since gelatin molecules are so long and threadlike, the word "disperse" would also be good. Of course, ordinary gelatin won't dissolve (and form a clear liquid) until it's heated.

I find it unnecessary to soak the gelatin overnight. However, I do find it important to thoroughly mix the gelatin in cold water (I use magnetic stirring) before turning on the heat. Maybe the term "disperse" would apply to this stage. (On a side note, my 3-30-200 mixture has such a high gelatin content, that my magnetic stirrer can't handle it, even though it's powerful, and I use a large "star" spin bar. I use a plastic spoon for manual mixing, until all the gelatin is thoroughly wetted.)

After all the gelatin is wetted, and there are no clumps of dry powder, I place the mixture in a water bath on top of the hot plate/stirrer, and turn on the heat (thermostatted) with constant stirring. At first my spin bar can't handle the viscosity, and wobbles in an uncontrolled way, but as the gelatin in the bottom of the beaker begins to melt (i.e., completely dissolve) is can start to turn normally.
