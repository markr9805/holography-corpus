Dinesh Mon Jul 05, 2004 10:34 pm #7
My experience is more with shearography than interferometry, but there's not much difference.

[quote:dd43c9acfe]My original planned setup would be to take a double-exposed hologram of a ball (metal or plastic) attached on the floor. I would shoot the laser from above.[/quote:dd43c9acfe]

Where would the plate be? If you shoot the laser from above and have the laser at the side, very little or no object light will hit the plate. You could place the plate between the source and the ball, ie a Denisyuk. But...

[quote:dd43c9acfe]I havent decided yet on the configuration whether it should be a reflection denisyuk or a transmission.[/quote:dd43c9acfe]

In principle it doesn't matter, but in practice, I'd say you need to do it in transmission. The original position of the object must co-incide exactly on reconstruction to within half lambda when the plate is put back, otherwise the results will not be accurate. With reflection, the major problems are a shift due to shrinkage during processing and aberrations due to the finite width and angular spread of the reconstruction source. You can always reconstruct a reflection using a laser source, if there are geometric constraints, however, it's easire making a transmission. Having said this, the same problem of ffilm shrinkage also holds true for laser transmission. The film must be developed with zero, or known, shrinkage. For this reason, shearography is slightly better.

[quote:dd43c9acfe] is it possible to determine the directions of in-plane displacements by just evaluating the fringe pattern on the interferogram?[/quote:dd43c9acfe]

I don't believe so. The interference lines are due to phase shifts caused by the displacement. So, whether the phase shifts from k to k+delta(k) or k-delta(k), as long as delta(k) is the same, the lines will be the same.

There is a technique whereby you can shoot a hologram of the ball and reconstruct it so that the pseudoscopic image of the ball superimposes directly on the real ball. Then any movement of the ball will result in "real time" fringes. there was a paper given at SPIE about this around 89 or so. I or someone else can try to dig it up. In this situation, you might be able to monitor the ball with some other independant system while capturing the fringes on a ccd. This way, the independant system would give direction, while software exists that can analyse the fringes.

There is someone who used to regularly give papers on interferometry but hasn't shown up in the last two or three years. I'll find the papers and post his name and contact info. He migfht be able to help.

Dinesh Tue Jul 06, 2004 5:49 pm #9
[quote:8219c4b69c]The direction would not matter. Is my sketch correct? [/quote:8219c4b69c]

The actual shape of the fringes would depend on the kind of light you're throwing at it, ie collimated, diverging or converging and whether or not you're on- or off-axis. If not collimated, then the exact fringe seperation would follow a square law, perhaps elliptical. Basically, the fringe seperation is the phase difference between two points. The phase profile of a diverging, on-axis beam from a point Z is:

phi = (1/(pi)(lambda)(Z))((x^2) + (y^2) + other stuff (sorry can't remember off the top of my head!))

You can see that the radii of equal phase are therefore proportional to the root of the radii. If the above is the phase profile of one point and you superimpose another point displaced along the Z axis to a new point Z_0, then you simply replace the Z with Z_0 and take the difference for the profile of the "beat" frequency. That is, if the phase profile of the first point (from Z) is phi_0 and the phase profile of the second point (from Z_0) is phi_1, then the "beat" frequency profile is proportional to cos(|phi_1 - phi_0|). If the beam is collimated or diverging, you need the phase profile of these beams and then the 'beat' frequency profile is given by the cos function of the phase profile difference, as usual.

[quote:8219c4b69c]How about if I use a cube instead of a ball?[/quote:8219c4b69c]

What you use depends on what you're trying to measure, surely? It seems to me that you need to calculate the displacement of an object relative to the expected displacement of the earth in the direction of the plate. For instance, in the case of the sphere, you'd need to calculate a delta(r_0), with the vector r_0 pointing at the plate for a surface relative to a displacement delta(r), with r pointing in the direction of the movemement of the earth. The phase profile would then be a function of both delta(r_0) and delta(r) and the "beat" frequency would be:

phi ~ cos(|(phi_1(delta(r_0), delta(r_1)) - phi_0(delta(R_0), delta(r_1)).

[quote:8219c4b69c]Im not yet familiar with emulsion shrinkage, how does this happen? [/quote:8219c4b69c]

The fringes within a standard hologram made with any volume material are equi-phase surfaces within the material. When wet processed, depending on the material (although it's pretty universal effect), the material itself tends to shrink and expand due to the water content and other effects. For instance, when Silver Halide is fixed, the unexposed Silver Halide grains are washed away leaving 'holes' in the gelatin. The gelatin collapses under the strain of the gelatin surrounding the 'holes' and there is a net shrinkage. In terms of the iso-phase surfaces, there is a torsional effect such that the surface twist, since they're connected to the base at the bottom of the material and nothing at all at the top. This twist in the iso-surfaces causes a displacement and some aberrtion in the final image field. In display holography this effect is mostly too small to be seen, but when you're trying to measure down to half lambda, this may become important. One alternative is to use polymer, another is to process bloody carefully!

Dinesh Fri Jul 09, 2004 1:59 pm #11
[quote:be8b144f27]Is this a practical approach? [/quote:be8b144f27]

Interferometry is practical if the expected range of movement is small. This means movement in the range of microns or roughly about 10 lambda. The problem is the interferometric fringe density. In order to be able to count them, they must be fairly large. If the cos term caused by the movement is too small, the fringes either get too small to count or too small to show contrast. The trick may be getting a good geometry to maximise the fringe contrast.

If you're doing a study of a time dependant phenomenon, perhaps the real time approach may be more feasible. The papers I'm sending describe the technique, but basically, you superimpose the holographically reconstructed image of the object on top of the real object. Then when the real object moves, the fringes change. By using a video camera trained on the fringes, you'll get time and space variation.

[quote:be8b144f27]Im a bit confused if you'll refer to "beat frequency", but I could easily picture out if I used the phase or wavelength differences.[/quote:be8b144f27]

Sorry. Most people are confused by the word 'phase' so I tend to use musical or audio analogies, which I find people are more comfortable with. In the audio realm, you probably know that if two notes are very close, you hear a third rising and falling note whose pitch is the difference between the frequencies of the two notes - a beat frequency. I believe they tune musical instruments this way. In holographic interferometry, the spatial frequencies of the object and it's displacement are so close in spatial frequency that a third spatial frequency is created on reconstruction. This is the fringe set of the phase differences. However, in order to see this, the two spatial frequencies must be pretty close, hence the range of movement must be also pretty close.

Yes you can do it.

forget reflection. the color shift will make it not work at all.

Make a single beam transmission of the ball, the easiest of all holograms. One lens, one plateholder, maybe 45 degree reference angle, beam hits ball and plate.

epoxy everything in place so that you can develop the plate (not film) and put it back EXACTLY in the same place in the plateholder. This CAN be done.

readjust the ratio (wiggle the lens) so the holo image and the real ball are approx equal brightness. Look at the fringes.

Tapping your foot, shifting your weight, doing anything to change the floor with the ball glued to it should be obvious. Just watch it for a while and note all fringe variations.
