Colin Kaminski Sun Jan 06, 2008 4:44 am #9
Here is a hologram that is "drippy" or has "rainbow lines". It was caused by laser light entering the edge of the plate during exposure. Either design your plateholder to block light entering the edges of the plate or tape the edge off with electrical tape.

![Drippy.jpg](https://holographyforum.org/forum/download/file.php?id=5989)
Colin Kaminski Sun Jan 06, 2008 4:51 am #10
This hologram shows "woodgrain". It is caused by the laser light reflecting back and forth from the front to the back of the plate. Make sure you have the corect polarization of the reference beam and make sure the reference angle is somewhere near 54 degrees (Brewsters Angle).

![Woodgrain.jpg](https://holographyforum.org/forum/download/file.php?id=5990)

> It has been a question brewing in my head for a long time now as to why these holograms that moved have narrow band viewing but are ultra bright.

Hummm, this gets me thinkin and rememberin. Way back when, when I was doing resist with long exposures, I noticed the same thing. My theory at the time, and I haven't thought much about it since then, is this:

You have a set of fringes that consist of dark and light areas. And these fringes are not digital they are analog. That is, they are not black and white, they are black to dark grey, to light grey to white and every step of grey in between. The fact is that a very narrow portion of the light fringe is actually light. Now if the hologram moves 1/8 wavelength, this may increase the width of the actual light part of the fringe allowing a stronger diffraction.

So the question is, how can we make a standing square wave instead of a standing sine wave interference? Wouldn't it be better to have a distince line between full light and no light in the fringes? I think I will reflect on this for a while and come up with a crazy idea...LOL!
