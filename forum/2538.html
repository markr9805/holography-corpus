Tony Wed Aug 04, 2004 12:20 pm #35
John-

On your post about testing-

There's two issues in table testing - one is how stiff or rigid is the table, and the other is how good is the isolation from outside vibrations. I'd argue that 'testing by stomping around' is more likely testing the isolation than the stiffness. Further, if you are testing tables that are different weights, then the isolation characteristics are changing. When I was testing my hexcan table, I initially found it much worse in regards to isolation due to it's lighter weight than my old concrete table. When I equalized the weight, it was similar in isolation from external vibrations.

A more interesting test of laminate technology tables is to directly test the stiffness. I did this by watching the fringes walk as I carefully applied pressure to one corner of the table, with a calibrated finger. I expect your 2-layer hexcan table would be much better than a single layer table in this test.

On your post on the can/pvc lattice-

I'm not sure what this would buy you. If you want more strength than cans and are willing to cut PVC, why not make it all PVC? And if you want the cheap/easy use of cans, might as well make it all cans. The trick is to get every can to form a nice tight, thin glue joint with it's six neighbors. Also, the skin thickness will have a lot to do with supporting the optical mounts. You want it stiff enough so that it doesn't flex much in between support points/edges.

The hex design is tough to beat - honeybees have been using it for eons!

Dinesh Wed Aug 11, 2004 12:19 pm #47
John's right. It's vertical and p polarised. When it comes out of the laser vertically polarised and hits a transfer mirror taking it upward, the plane of incidence is vertical and parallal to the laser. That is, if the axis of the laser is labelled x and straight up is labelled y, then the plane of incidence is the x-y plane. Since the plane of polarisation is vertical out of the laser and the plane of incidence is also vertical, the beam is p polarised. When he hits his second mirror and takes the beam along an axis perpendicular to the x-y plane, ie in the z direction, and down to the plate the plane of incidence is now the z-y plane, also vertical. Since polarisation states maintain, the beam will remain p and so will remain vertically polarised. If the plate is laid down in a horizontal plane and beam bounces off but continues along the z-y plane, the plane of incidence is still vertical and the polarisation is still vertical. If he were to twist the plate so that the beam off the plate were to be perpendicular to the incoming plane, ie into the -x direction (parallal to the laser), the plane of incidence would be a plane inclined downward at whatever his ref angle is. In this case, I suspect that to maintain p, the polarisation would twist into the plane of incidence and so the polarisation would be a combination of vertical and horizontal in the ratio cos(ref angle)/sin(ref angle).

I'm finally no longer on the road and have a full-sized keyboard again, so I can post lengthier replies.

The SSC (Slab of Soda Cans) honeycomb was assembled can-by-can in the plywood frame. I used Polygem paste epoxy applied with an enormous syringe. One bead was applied to the rim of the open end of each can, leaving a gap to allow air leakage so the cans would sit in contact with the base and not get lifted by air trapped inside by the epoxy bead. Three more beads were laid up the side of each can where it was to contact the adjacent cans already in place. Each can was then tipped into position and pressed into contact with its neighbors. The epoxy was very thick, so there was no noticeable tendency for the cans to drift out of position. This took a couple afternoons as I recall.

After the can core set up, epoxy was applied overall to the steel top with a notched mortar trowel. The top was then flipped over and set atop the honeycomb. A bathroom scale was placed on the steel, and the poly dropcloth under the whole operation was then gathered over the top and sealed up with duct tape, one corner being sealed around a garden hose. The other end of the hose was attached to a shop vac in the garage (for noise isolation!) The scale served as a vacuum gauge and allowed me to find leaks in the plastic and seal them with tape, each fixed leak raising the force a bit. I recall calculating the total force on the slab during the cure was something like 3000lb. After several hours of cure, the vacuum was turned off and the slab was unwrapped.

I used it for perhaps five years until Agfa dropped 8E75 plates, and other things took over the space before I managed to try BB or Slavich materials.

If I had to do it again, I think I'd assemble it upside down, using the floor to level the steel. The plywood sides of the SSC's box were about 3" taller than the cans and extended down past the bottom to hide the innertubes. I'll confess that the bottom surface of the honeycomb was not steel but varnished 3/4" veneer plywood. It must have seemed like a good idea at the time, but I'd probably use a second sheet of steel which could drop into the frame "next time"! Even so, I seemed to have pretty good luck with it.
