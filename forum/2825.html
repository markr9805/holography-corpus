Dinesh Fri Oct 22, 2004 4:43 pm #14
The polarisation of the two beams affects the fringe visibility, which in turn affects the efficency of the hologram.

There is a quantity called fringe visibility, V, due to Michelson, which measures the fringe intensity:

V = (I_max - I_min)/(I_max + I_min)

where I_max is the intensity of the lightest part of the fringe and I_min the intensity of the darkest. If the darkest part of the beam is totally, completely black, then I_min = 0 and visibility is at it's maximum at V=1, no matter what I_max is, for a given fringe structure. In real terms, this is never so, so V usually is somewhere between 1 and 0. The value of V in turn is a measure of the efficency of the hologram (among other things). The nearer V is to 1 the better the fringe visibility is and so the brighter your hologram.

V depends on the the coherence of the laser (effectively, the coherence length) and the polarisation between the beams. If the polarisations of the two beams are parallel, then V drops as the path length varies. If the laser has a long coherence length (or, if the difference in path lengths is very short compared to the coherence of the laser) then the drop in V is usually not noticeable. It's there, but it''s very small. As you get to the edge of the coherence length V drops more and more until finally V=0. Apropos of another thread, V actually will increase again after some multiple of laser tube length, ie it goes to 0, then, if you keep increasing path lengths, it comes back up again but never to the same value it was for zero path length.

Assuming that the path length is zero, then V depends on polarisation angle between the two beams. Specifically, V is modulated by the cosine of the angle between the polarisation states. So, say V=0.75 for your particular setup (this will be dependant on a number of factors such as path length difference, gamma of film, gamma of developer, exposure etc, etc - maximising V is the holy grail of holography!) assuming perfect parallelism of polarisation states. Now you twist the polarisation of one beam by 30 degrees. Now the value of V will decrease by cos(30)= 0.866 and so the new value is V=0.65. The efficency of your hologram has dropped by (0.75-0.65)/0.75 = 0.13, 0r 13% of what it was for parallel polarisation. It's not quite this cut-and-dry, since the gamma of a bleached or DCG hologram is non-linear, but as a back-of-an-envelope calculation it's not far off.

If the polarisation of both beams is random, then unless you're bloody lucky, the fringe visibilty will be modulated by a rapidly varying cosine difference. If we slow down time and watch the interference happening, also assume that "vertical" is the "zero angle", One beam, beam_1 say, comes in at 0 while the other, beam_2 say, comes in at, say 30 degrees. V drops by 0.866. However this lasts for only about 1 gazillionth of your exposure time, now suddenly the beam_1 comes in at 25 degrees and beam_2 comes in at 63. Cos(63-25)=0.788, so for this gazillionth of the exposure V has dropped by 0.788. A gazillionth of the exposure later, beam_1 is coming in at 47 and beam_2 at 0. Cos (78-0)=0.21 and so your V is now dropped to 0.21 of its maximum. Hey! Look at that! They're both parralel for this gazillionth of exposure time! V is maximum! OK, it's a bit fanciful, but you see what I mean, if both beams are random, then the value of V dances all over the map as the exposure happens. This is also true if one beam is polarised and the other is random due to reflection off a diffuse surface.

A detector will not "see" polarisation unless you put a polariser in front of it.

There is a technique known as "polarisation holography" I haven't heard of it being used in a long time but it was meant as a way to determine the exact depolarisation of a particular object. The idea is to have two reference beams coming in from opposite sides of the plate, each at 90 degrees from the other. Since cos(90)=0, there's no mutual fringe density (V=0) between these references. However, the efficency of each hologram (or, rather the reconstruction from each reference direction) will depend on the degree of parallelism of the polarisation off the object to each reference. So, if you make such a hologram and looked at with reconstruction_1 and reconstruction_2 and they looked equally bright, then the object is truly randomising the polarisation. However if the hologram looked brighter in one recon than the other, you knew that the object was throwing the polarisation more in one direction. If you got zero for one recon and pretty bright for the other, you knew the object was not depolarising at all.
