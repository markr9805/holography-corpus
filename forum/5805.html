I have been continuing to play with presoaking DCG in water prior to fixing and I am really liking what I see. I rarely get greenies and get very uniform processing. I have found that no matter how long I keep the hologram in the water prior to fixing I still need to fix for the full time as if I did not presoak the plate in water first even if there is no evidence of AmDi present. I am able to get uniform greens, yellows, and very nice white hologam with this technique.

I will have to bring up again that this must be due to the hardener and not the fixer that is needed after the water soak. So back to Jeff's suggestion, which I have not tried yet, I want to presoak in water, then instead of fixing, put the plate in a solution of Alum only to harden it.

The benifits are a big cost advantage (a little Alum is cheaper then Kodak fixer) and as 35mm photography is going away (oh yes it is), the fixer will be harder to obtain. Right now there is only one store within a 25 mile radius that has Kodak Rapid Fixer with hardener.

Now back to discuss what is happening with a water presoak. The gelatin and fringes can be swelled to any size desired prior to hardening the gelatin using different temperatures of water. Then depending on hardening concentration the swollen fringes should be able to be hardened in a more swelled or less swelled state. This may make it possible to control color without changing alcohol concentrations. It may even be possible to use consecutive concentrations of harderns to initiate a differtial of harding throughout the emulsion or a high concentration done quickly to harden the surface more then the depth of the emulsion. Lots of room for research in this area.

Hi Dave,

Yes, the haze you have got there is quite easily understandable.

Yes, either the bake step would be needed or alternatively some small amount of alum.

Just to try to clarify the value of the sodium metabisulfite to others.

It is not at all a hardener by itself. As Martin said, it is its chemical reaction with the chromium compounds that then produces the hardener “CrIII” (or “Chrome Alum”) that does the hardening. The reaction between unexposed dichromate salt in the dark fringes and your metabisulfite is pretty rapid but not instantaneous, so that means the dichromate has just enough time to shoot itself into the surrounding water due to the strong osmotic pressure difference between the dichromate-salt filled gelatin film and the low concentration of other salts (bisulfites) in the water.

These points are discussed in our recent Welsh symposium paper , the gist of it given here:-

…………… So to now maximize the fringe differences in the wet step using the Kodak Fix, what is required is for the light induced and immobilized Cr(V) species to be much more quickly reduced by the sulfite ions than the unbound Cr (VI) species in the adjacent dark fringes. We can assume that this situation is helped by the probability that any Cr(V) intermediate produced in the dark fringe would not be bound to the gelatin as it is not produced by the oxidation of the gelatin but instead by the oxidation of sulfite ion .I mean by this that the laser light caused dichromate to apparently get reduced to CrV by oxidizing the gelatin and simultaneously attach itself permanently to the gelatin in the light struck fringes only.This would mean that unless any unchanged dichromate in the dark fringes is reduced down to Cr(III) it will not bind to the gelatin. On this point it has been shown that the speed of reaction between gelatin and Cr(III) is not slow ( 9 ) . ............................................

So one may now ask what the purpose is of adding Kodak’s alum hardener to that first bath? Al 3+ ions will increase the overall hardness bias requiring a higher processing temperature to maximise fringe contrast. A freshly coated DCG layer can be too soft in its dark fringes to maintain good fringe integrity. So a certain minimal level of hardness is necessary……………………

The explanation that fits observations is that the haze is caused by the chromium in the dark fringes being removed from the gelatin where it is held less strongly than it would be by EDTA. Originally the hardening in the dark fringes may have been from CrIII reacting with the gelatin as chrome alum is well known to do to form a chromium- gelatin complex. Alternatively,

any aluminum alum complexes would probably be removed just as well by EDTA too. So that left the dark fringes soft and deformed . This apparently causes scatter, like Dave’s dog image example. However the really interesting point is the state of the chromium- gelatin bond in the light struck fringes. This is simply not removable by EDTA which is why the diffraction efficiency is maintained inspite of the haze. In the light fringes the original dichromate must have oxidized the gelatin ( this is the driving force behind this light induced reaction.). This oxidation results in a much stronger Cr oxygen- gelatin bond initially involving Cr V and eventually getting subsidiary binding as the CrV finishes up as a form of “CrIII” apparently (Bolte et al) , but this Cr bond cannot the same as the CrIII binding was in the dark fringes or the image would have been removed by the EDTA.

So I rest my case until someone comes up with a better explanation.

I am certainly being woolly about the nature of the chromium bonding in the light fringes. But all the papers I have come across also seem woolly on this point as they all suggest it is just a CrIII “complex” type of bond. The experimental evidence is that this is not the case. Get some EDTA and see for yourselves!

Dichromate or CrVI is an oxidant (electron grabber) there is always a net gain in energy in the system when it can do this. It is a particularly strong oxidant but there is a feature called activation energy needed to kick start many reactions. (Oxygen itself fortunately for us, needs activation energy from some thing such as a lighted match before it can start burning things up, activation energy can be reduced to near zero with the aid of catalysts such as certain iron compounds eg haemoglobin in our blood.) Dichromate does not oxidize gelatin rapidly unless it is given a kick start this can be done by heat above 100C or far more slowly at room temperature. A somewhat peculiar feature of dichromate is its ability to get the required activation energy by absorbing light energy in the violet -blue -green region and to use it as we know how.

Other coloured metallic compounds such as permanganates and ceric salts just dont behave with this specificity. Very fortunately for our art only the CrVI ions that have absorbed light get over that activation energy hurdle, none of it is apparently transferred to neighboring CrVI ions that are in the dark so there is no chain reaction.

Dichromate on its own in the light has got nothing to oxidize to gain energy so it stays unaffected. Although in the case of a small pile of dry ammonium dichromate out in your yard a really hot wire applied to the top of the pile can start a chain reaction to produce an impressive volcano effect where the ammonium part gets oxidized to nitrogen, water and the chromate gets deoxidised to a voluminous green Cr2O3 oxide which is in the stable CrIII state. This is of course an impossible reaction with potassium dichromate.
