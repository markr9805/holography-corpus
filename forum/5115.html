For your first MBDCG holograms just make Single Beam reflections with the plate setting rigtht on the object. Like the illustration below. You will not need a split beam or a vibration isolation table. You can rest the object on your garage floor then the plate right on the object. This is a very stable set up and only the plate and object have to be relatively stable to keep the fringes. Pick on object that has 3 points that you can rest the plate on (this seems to work best for me) have the beam come in from the top as in the illustration.

For another technique (I actually used this one for the Jesus with open arms hologram in the gallery section) stand the object up against a rigid backing (I used a brick). Then lean the plate up against the object standing almost verticle (a little lean). This too is a very rigid set up for single beams. The Jesus hologram was done that way and is a single beam.

You could probably use a small flat (not shiny) white object or figurine and lay or lean your plate (micro slide) right against the object.

> Hans: But I don't have a suitable pinhole to test it with. I have not worked out yet how to determine if my aluminum foil pinholes are too big or too small.

Just put it in your spatial filter and try different pinholes or objectives and note what you see.

If the pinhole is too small you lose some light around the edges and you may get some rings around the edges. If the pinhole is too big the beam may not be as clean as you want but it will mostly be around the edges again. Since I thow the outer part of the beam away anyway to me the pinholes do not have to be percise. I think I only have two pinholes and don't know the sizes. I only use one with my 5x and 10 x objective.

Your best bet is to try them and see if you like what comes out.

DIY HOLOGRAPHY RECORDING MATERIAL

This is a remarkably simple way of making a holographic recording material. This is the way that we make a lot of holograms in our lab, and we find it to be by far the easiest method for making holographic plates.

In essence, we treat a glass surface to make it chemically 'sticky', we coat that with gelatin, and harden the gelatin with chromium or formaldehyde. Once we have the gelatin film we then soak into it a silver salt, and subsequently soak in potassium or lithium bromide to precipitate an ultra-fine grain precipitate of silver bromide. The bromide solution also incorporates a dye to make the plate photo-sensitive in the required wavelength range, and with addition of a little sensitiser we can produce by this method a holographic plate of quite high standard.

A worksheet (dated Nov 2000) is given below. It gives results which have good diffraction efficiency and photosensitivity compared to ultrafine grain proprietary material. But this is for the fun of doing it all yourself and getting bright results. If you are particularly concerned about marks from bubbles, dust and blemishes then you may prefer to use the proprietary material.

The material on this page is based on the following article, with some changes that we have made to our protocols since publication, and any differences between the original article and the text below are solely due to those differences.

A simple way to make silver halide hologram recording plates by Diffusion

By Jeff Blyth, Institute of Biotechnology , University of Cambridge Tennis court rd. Cambridge CB2 1QT

The Basic principle

A coating of pure gelatin on a glass plate is treated with silver nitrate. The coating is then immersed in a bath of bromide ion and dye. This then precipitates extremely fine grains of silver bromide in the gelatin layer.

Materials

1. Presubbed glass plates . (You can use old holographic plates with the gelatin removed with the aid of household bleach.)

2. Gelatin of bloom strength between 250 and 300 (eg. 300bloom from Aldrich cat no. 27,162-4) , (You can use culinary gelatin without any sugar or flavourings.)

3. ascorbic acid or Vitamin C,

4. Silver-nitrate , (A 1N volumetric standard solution is a useful form)

5. Potassium Bromide

6. Chromium acetate , (you can use chrome alum instead)

7. dye(s): Pinacyanol Chloride (for HeNe 633nm exposure) or 1,1 -diethyl -2,2 cyanine iodide or exposure at 532nm .

8. Sodium hydroxide

9. (for new glass plates) 3-amino-propyltriethoxysilane

Concentration of Solutions

(quantities will need to be judged by you to suit your requirements)

Silver nitrate : 6% w/v in (DI) water , (or the 1N volumetric standard solution diluted by 1volume to 2 volumes DI water.)

Stock dye solns : for 633nm 1g. /1000 ml Methanol, but for 532 nm dye, 1g. per 500 ml methanol.

(In practice you would probably only need a hundreth of a gram to make up a few ml of these somewhat expensive dyes.)

Potassium bromide 4% w/v in 3/2 methanol /water (3% lithium bromide gives a finer grained hologram than the equivalent concentration of potassium bromide, however 4% potassium bromide works well).

Chromium acetate solution 1% or Chrome Alum 2%

Gelatin solution. 15%: (see 2 paragraphs down)

Ascorbic acid 1% solution in water , adjusted to around pH 5 with any alkali.

Preparation of plates

Glass plates usually need a pre-treatment step or the gelatin coating will peal off. You can use old holographic plates by simply giving them a 10 min. soak in neat domestic bleach solution and then rub off the old gelatin layer under tap water. After a final rinse in distilled water, no further subbing step may be required.

However with new glass plates, I leave them soaking overnight in a 100% bleach (Domestos or Parazone). After the plates are dry I rub them over with a 1% solution of 3-amino-propyltriethoxysilane in acetone on a tissue until it has evaporated, and leave them in air to interact with the silane for at least two hours before coating. (The silane solution has to be freshly prepared for each batch of plates).

Preparation of coating solution for a 10 x 8 plate

Add 30g gelatin to 170ml cold distilled water and mark the liquid level on the beaker. Place beaker in a water bath and heat while stirring constantly until gelatin solution is between 60 and 70o C.

Stir until all granules have cleared. Top up level to the mark . To remove skin and surface foam, pour through a fine mesh (nylon stocking works fine) into a preheated beaker. Then immediately proceed to next step:

Coating (by the old Victorian curtain method)

Hold the beaker in your right hand and with you left incline the presubbed glass plate (preheated to around 70oC) at an angle of about 30o to the vertical with its bottom edge in a clean tray. Pour the gelatin in a line about 1 cm from the top of the plate. The pouring rate must be continuous until the furthest edge of the plate is reached . (You may have to accept the tendency of the coating to not completely cover the lower part closest to the furthest edge. ) Lean plate against something for a few minutes while coating gels. Run a knife along thick layer at the bottom to free plate rather than risk tearing the delicate coating. (Since no hardener is involved yet the gel can be readily scooped up and recoated if you are not satisfied.). Put plate in cold solution of chromium acetate for 1 minute. Shake off drips and then (without washing away that salt ) blow plate with cold air until dry. Once the layer is dry leave the plate to complete the chrome hardening effect overnight in a warmer. (Preferably at around 60oC for several hours). Rinse the hardened plate in DI water and dry in a warm air flow. If you want to cut plate up for the next step then after scoring the glass on the back and cracking it, it is best not to pull sections apart before running a scalpel blade along the gelatin side first so that it is cut and not torn apart..

Alternatively a Meyer bar can be used. About 7 turns per cm.

AgBr loading operation.

1. For a 5 x 4 plate place approx 3 ml 6% silver nitrate soln. in the centre and at once squash it with a clean flat cover plate (preferably transparent plastic so that you can see the air bubbles are squeezed out). Leave for 3 minutes. Safelighting is not strictly necessary here but white lighting should be subdued.

2. Remove cover plate and immediately remove the excess silver solution on its surface by gently brushing over the plate with a soft squeegee (windscreen wiper blade).

3. Blow dry plate with cool air. Once dried, the plates can be stored for a short while in a cool, dry, dark location until needed.

4. Under safelight conditions , add 2.5 ml of dye solution per 100ml of potassium bromide solution , add about 0.5 ml of 1% ascorbic acid solution (this is the same solution as is used in the final sensitizing bath ) agitate the bath and plunge plate in while maintaining the agitation for about 2 minutes (although with softer gelatin this could be reduced to 60 seconds, otherwise unacceptable grain growth can occur. Expect to spend a little time optimising this step for your own application). This solution can be re-used a number of times, until such a point as the dye starts to come out of solution or the brightness of the resultant holograms seems to be diminished; the dye used for 532nm exposures (see above) is far more re-useable.

5. Rinse well under running tap water (any AgBr only on the surface can be removed by gently rubbing with ungloved finger.) Plates usually come out this bath beautifully clear under the green safe light, without any surface deposit.

6. Sensitizing bath step

The plate can be immersed for 1 minute in 1% ascorbic acid solution adjusted to pH 5 using a little sodium carbonate or hydroxide . Alternatively the well known triethanolamine pre-swelling technique can be used with the advantage of increased brightness at a shorter wavelength. (prolonged settling period may then be necessary however to avoid creep while the exposure is being made).

After exposure the plate is then developed as per the first part above.

> JohnFP: Here is how I do it.I place my Distilled Water in a tall plastic clear cup.I place the cup in a tray that is as high as the water level in my cup.I put a thermometer in the water that is in the cup. I use this to stir also.I add my gelatin to the water in the cup while stirring the whole time. The gel gets so thick it is almost solid.I then turn the running water on such that it drips/pours in the tray but not in the cup. If the tray is as high as the water line in the cup the water in the tray should reach that level before overflowing.I adjust this running water till it is just luke warm. I let the cup sit in the tray with the warm running water for about 5 - 10 minutes stirring occasionally but if it is too thick do not worry about stirring too much.I then increase the temperature of the running water till it is almost hot but not scalding to the touch, this will be about 95 – 105 degrees F.I monitor the gelatin and you will see it start to become liquid around the inside edge of the cup.This is when you want to stir about every 5 minutes for about 1 minute straight.I keep and eye on the thermometer and make sure the temperature stays below 115 F. After I monitor and stir for a while if the temperature is too low I increase the temperature of the running water a little more. Stir and monitor. Repeat if necessary.Make sure the gel has cooked for at least 15 minutes at 115 F (which is usually 45 minutes after I first added the gel to the cold water).If you look at the gelatin at this point it should be very homogeneous and completely dissolved. It will look like a clear honey.Then I add the Ammonium Dichromate and stir for at least 5 minutes to make sure it is all dissolved.I then let the mixture cook another 10 minutes, still at 115 F. stirring every couple of minutes.About 2 minutes before I will used the emulsion I do not stir it. This allows the bubble to dissipate,I then pour the mixture very slowly into a baby bottle.I double up a Mr. Coffee filter and place it over the top of the baby bottle and press a crater down in the center (If you do not do this the paper rips for some reason). I then screw on the top with nipple which I cut a bigger hole in. About 1/8 in diameter. Too big is ok.I then tilt my pouring cup at 30 - 45 degrees and squeeze the baby bottle slowly and the emulsion starts to come out. When it first starts to come out I am just next to the pour cup so the first bubbles do not go into the pouring cup.I then move over to the pouring cup and the emulsion drips on the inside on the side of the tilted cup and streams into the bottom of the cup.It is very important to get a good two handed grip on the baby bottle and try to get all of the emulsion to come out in one long slow squeeze. Usually takes a good minute or two for 100ml. If you have to let go and get another squeeze the air that is drawn in fills the emulsion with bubbles.If done carefully you will have a very nice, clea,r bubble less, emulsion.If there are a few bubbles on the top scope them out with the back of a spoon.

Boy that was from long, long ago.

I now have a heater/stirrer. I know the setting in which it keep the gelatin at 115 degrees F. I take the room temperature water place it on the heater/stirrer and add the gelatin to it while still at room temp. The gelatin gets very thick and clumpy. I usually hae to set the stirrer up to full speed just to get things to keep moving. I then let it set for at least an hour stirring and heating but I will turn the stirrer down to about medium. Just so it mixes well but does not suck air down the center of the whirlpool. After an hour I add my AmDi and let is disolve for a good 15 minutes.

At this time there are air bubbles, mostly on the top. I have a funnel that I put a coffee filter in. The funnel is setting in another clean beaker such that the bottom of the funnel is just off the bottom of the beaker, this keeps the drip bubbles from forming. After the mixture has filtered I remove the funnel and there is almost no bubbles at all. Any small bubbles that I see I pull out with a Q-tip. I leave the emulsion in the beaker and pour from the beaker onto the plate over a heated plactic tray. I use a heating pad for theaputic application for your back as the heater. I set the plastic tray on the pad which is on high. The tray is a cheap brand sealable sandwich container bottom. Heating the tray provides warmth to the overflow gelatin such that I can pour it back into the beaker to continue coating. This is very helpfull when I make a small amount of emulsion. If I make 100ml of emulsion I can usually coat 3 or 4 plates with the amount in the beaker. To do more plates I simply pour the emulsion in the container back into the beaker without allowing bubbles, this is similar to pouring beer in a tilted glass to keep the head down. And coat more plates. The key for me is not letting the bubbles form in the filtering process.

About gelatin reuse, I made plates with no sensitizer then sensitized them three weeks later and had no problems. I think the key is to immediately refrigerate them after they are coated and dry (about 4 hours). Bacteria should not form in a Frig.

I used to do the same thing and found I ruined more plates by trying to conserve the amount of emulsion I poured. With the veil method being liberal is better then conserving the amount poured on the plate. I get a cleaner veil. But I was always doing 4x5 or 5x7 inch plates. Gelatin concentration, glass temperature, gelatin temperature, spin vs no spin and application technique all play a role in final emulsion thickness. So if it works at your concentration the way you are doing it then great.

> dave battin: john ,how did you sensitize your plates?can you give us any info about this?did you harden the plates ?

I have made plates with only gelatin and water. Filtered, coated, spun and let dry (I now spin for at least an hour to keep the air moving over the plates). After at least 4 hours of dry time at room temp (about 68F.) I store at 55F. When I want to sensitize I let the plates come back up to ambient temp of 68F. I then make a mixture of AmDi and water and place the plate in the mixture for a couple of minutes. Then take out and put horizontally in my rack on the spinner and spin for 15 minutes or longer until dry.

I have done this without any hardener and use the Kodak fixer after exposure.

I have not had a problem with emulsion lifting off the plates during sensitizing.

I have ofter seen it mentioned to add photoflo to the gelatin to increase adhesion but since I have never had a problem with emulsion lifting off the plate I have never used it.
