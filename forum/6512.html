JohnFP Thu Nov 01, 2007 10:23 am #3
Hey Terry, welcome. I was waiting to see you post in the begining section but since I did not, I will try to answer your question here.

Any transmission hologram can be made to project. In fact, that is how most image planed holograms are made. The projected image is captured on another holographic piece of film and because the object is now a projected image, you can place the object (projected object) behind, in front or bisecting the the film.

But I must state, that simply projecting a hologram onto a flat screen will not give you a 3 dimensional image. On the screen you will only see the slice of the object that is focused at the distance of the screen. The screen should be the same distance away from the plate that the object was in recording it. As you move the screen a little closer to a little further away from the plate from that object distance, you will see different depths of the object come into and go out of focus.

Tell your friend to retry projecting his transmission on a screeen, but place the screen away from the film the same distance that object was from the film when it was recorded. You/he will have to find the correct side and angle to get the image to project. You need to illuminate the plate rotated 180 degrees with respect to the original reference beam to project the real image.

Look at the "Slit Beam Transmission" section in this link

[http://www.holographyforum.org/HoloWiki ... php/Setups](http://www.holographyforum.org/HoloWiki/index.php/Setups)

As you can see in thefirst diagram the Transmisson is created. Then in the second diagram the plate is flipped with respect to the reference beam. That is, specifically in this diagram the plate is rotated top to bottom and bottom to top. If you study it you will see that the image is swung all the way around to the other side and will be upside down, inside out and backwards. But it will be projected when illuminated with a laser coming in from the original angle.

This is easy to do if you recoded the hologram and simply put it back in the plateholder exactly the way it was but then rotate it. For an already made hologram, hold the hologram in laser light which you allow to come throuth the plate at and angle, but your eyes are parallel to the plate. If you are doing this correctly, the laser light will not be going in your eyes. DO NOT LET THE LASER LIGHT GO INTO YOUR EYES!!! Now rotate the plate until you see the virtual image. It will be behind the glass. Now remember at what side and what angle the laser light is hitting the plate. Also rembember about what distance the object is from the plate. Now flip the plate front to back, back to front as in the link, and place a white card where you eyes were (parallel to the plate). Move the card every so slightly away and nearer to the plate and you should see the projected image.

If you need further explaination or diagrams, I would be glad to ellaborate.

JohnFP Mon Nov 05, 2007 1:51 pm #9
Well Terry, that make more sense. Lenses are/can be used for a variety of reasons in making a hologam. The top reason is to expand the beam such that the beam will cover the entire plate. Obviously a raw laser beam usually does not expand and the lens is needed to get full coverage of your plate. Another reason a large lens may be used is to place at the focal length of the lens in the expanded beam to collimate the beam, that is, have the light rays all running parallel and not diverging or converging. Another application of a lens, a large one again, is to place between the object and the plate such that the object is being focused onto the plate. Because a hologram is being made of the real image of the object, the plate can be moved closer or futher away from the lens to allow the image to float in front of, bisection or behind the plate. The object/lens combination in this set up is very similar to and H1 in and H1 to H2 set up.
