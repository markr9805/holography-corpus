walschuler Fri May 23, 2008 2:47 pm #7
I would think you might want to combine the beams because of the increased possibility of setup motion when you move mirrors in and out of the beam, and also because of the possibility of object motion simply with the passage of the time to do the adjustment. (John might well disagree). If you do beam combining, then you can deal with the need for various exposures, due to varying beam brightness or to non-uniform emulsion sensitivity in two ways: one, a separate shutter before the combiner for each beam each with its own control, or separate continuously variable neutral density filters before the combiner and a single shutter after the combiner. Shutters and variable filters are both expensive items,as are the combiners, and I have not looked into a cost tradeoff between them.

Bill
Ed Wesly Fri May 23, 2008 5:46 pm #8
Why am I always the party pooper? Three points I want to make:

#1: If you are going to go multi-wavelength, you really should combine the beam so that you can see all the light all at once. If you do exposures sequentially, like John suggests, and Hans Bjelkhagen has done in some of his earlier work, if there is any misalignment of the beams there will be colored lines in the shadows cast by objects onto the background. If you can see good alignment, so much the better!

#2: Polarization needs to be considered when using beam combiners. One beam will pass through a combiner, so its polarization vector would need to be aligned for maximum transmission. But the other one will be reflected by the same optic, so its polarization should be oriented orthogonally for max reflectance! So you either suffer the losses when putting them both in the same plane, or have a half-wave plate after the combiner and rotate the vectors to be the same between each exposure. Maybe there are non-polarizing dichroics?

#3: Why cut down laser power with attenuators? Make two separate exposures at different times.

I have the dubious distinction of being the first to make some RGB exposures on the panchromatic DuPont photopolymer for SPIE around ’90 or ’91. Although they made the material, they had 3 separate RGB expos of different objects on one sheet, nothing true color.

The object I used was a Russian Nesting doll that Denisyuk himself gave to my #1 son. The hologram was the size of a 35 mm slide, approximately 1” by 1 ½”. At that time their red exposure dose was DCG-like, like a hundred mJ^cm2, so even with a trusty S-P 125 the expo was about a minute! The other two beams were 515 and 476 from two separate Argons. I was encumbered with the shuttling mirror system, and if I had my druthers I would never use it again!

Again, whatever you can get to work with the stuff you can get your hands on is the right solution! As Danny mentions, the beam splitters from 3 chip video cameras are usable, with the polarization proviso, and another doodad I have (but alas and alack, not the other two color lasers) is a beam combiner from a 3 LCD video/digital projector!

This is an amazing device. It’s about a 2-inch cube, and from its top you can see that it is fabricated from 4 right angle prisms glued together with dichroic coatings at the seams. I shudder to think what one of these things would cost on a onesy-twosy basis. One beam passes straight through, the other two enter through the sides, so one is polarized differently from the other.

One of these years I’ll get to play with all these toys I’ve accumulated!
Joe Farina Fri May 23, 2008 6:10 pm #9
> Ed Wesly: #3: Why cut down laser power with attenuators?

No matter how you look at it, your exposure time will be determined by the "weakest link in the chain" if you are using multiple lasers. In my case the weakest link is the HeNe, which is a 38mW SP-127. So, say for example, that I will need to do a 5-minute exposure considering the HeNe component. So I have no choice, I need to go 5 minutes. I might as well have the other, stronger beams exposing the emulsion at the same time, and reduce their power by attenuation. Otherwise, if I do other exposures, I will be increasing my overall exposure time which is bad.

Also, I want to look at the actual combined beam as it's reflected off the object before making the exposure, to see what the colors look like beforehand.

Why not have fun making your own holographic beam-combiner using DCG:

Expand and collimate the 457nm laser beam until it is ~12mm in diameter.

index match a DCG substrate to a mirror, and position it so it intercepts the 12mm dia beam at 45 degrees.

Process the exposed plate, wavelength tune and laminate a protective coverglass onto DCG- you have a DIY combiner.

Some tips:

You want a relatively thick (12 to 20 micron) layer which is low bandwidth. This means you'll need low dichromate concentration and low development temperatures.

At 45 degree incidence, such a combiner would reflect the 457nm beam and transmit the 532nm beam.

You may be able to get a number of exposures on a 5x4 plate- you then pick the best one. Also, the small area of the beam means you'll only have to expose for a few seconds.
walschuler Thu Jul 03, 2008 5:08 pm #18
Some additional ways to make beam combiners:

1) Using a telephoto lens, aim the beams into the front end of the lens at different locations off its axis. You would play with the entry angle to achieve colinearity and parallelicity, which likely won't be perfect, but may be made good enough for your purposes.

2) Use spectral dispersion in reverse. Consider that a white light beam passing through a prism or diffraction grating will be spread out into the spectrum, and that all light rays are in principle reversible. Thus aim your beams at the disperser and adjust their angles and incidence locations until they come out coaxial. Prisms give non-linear dispersion, while gratings give linear dispersion (i.e., the change in wavelength per change in angle is constant across the spectrum). For the prism, it would be good to pay attention to the orientation of the polarization vectors of the beams, which should be parallel to the plane of incidence, since they will enter at angles reasonably close to Brewster's angle.
