BobH Tue Feb 01, 2005 7:41 am #2
You got it straight!

By certain the film plate is VERY stable. Pretend the film plate is a large, heavy, and very expensive glass window. Imagine it to be 4' square instead of 2" square. You would set it gently on its edge and lean it against a support (probably a wall). You wouldn't want a corner of the plate to be touching the wall, you'd probably adjust it so that it was parallel to the wall for best stability. Better stability would result if it were set on two pieces of wood (to prevent it from rocking on the floor), and leaning it against the wall so that it only touched the wall at a single point close to the center of the top edge. The plate would be at rest, and unstressed.

Although the film plate is very small and light weight, treat it the same way. If you want it to be held vertically, support it at two points along its bottom edge and one point along its top edge. If you want it mounted horizontally, be sure it is supported by three points ONLY. A good example is to glue two short pieces of 1/4-20 threaded rod to the table (so the edge of the plate sets into the threads, approximately 1/4" in from the edges), and let the plate lean in to contact the object somewhere near the top center of the plate.

Triangulation is the key to stability. Three legs are more stable than four.

Some recommend clamping the plate with large paper clips which hold a small plate vertically. Again, think triangulation. Don't let the entire bottom edge of the plate touch the table. Clamp the plate so that ONLY three points contact the table surface ... the two outermost points of the clamp and one point along the bottom edge of the plate.

Dinesh Tue Feb 01, 2005 1:15 pm #4
Up to a point, you're right, assuming you're doing split beam. The hologram is a photographic record of a series of very fine lines and whorls all across the plate which are formed by the intersection of object and ref from two different paths. As in any photographic recording, if the subject moves during an exposure the photograph will blur. Since the reconstruction of the hologram requires that these lines and whorls be cleanly seperated, any blurring of the lines will cause the hologram to be dim. Now, a small movement of either of the two paths will cause a large change in the line structure itself. However, if anything moves before the beamsplitter, the entire line structure moves and the line structure itself does not change. You can see this by drawing a set of fine lines on two seperate transparencies. If you superimpose the two transparencies, you'll get a moire pattern. If you rotate just one transparency, the moire pttern changes and, if the lines are very fine, the pattern changes a lot for a pretty small movement, but if you move both transparencies simultaneously, the moire pattern itself does not change, it just moves. The overall fringe pattern that defines the hologram is still there. However, you don't want to move it too much. If the entire pattern moved so that a dark line superimposed on a light line, the pattern is obliterated and you get no hologram.

As for the moire analogy, that's exactly what interference is! The interference structure is a moire pattern. The phase variations of one beam form one set of fringes with a particular spatial frequency and the the other beam has it's own spatial frequency. Dinesh Wed Feb 02, 2005 1:36 am #9
I couldn't get a picture so I couldn't see your movie.

Consider this:

A mirror at distance d from a cube beamsplitter launches a beam into a Michealson,let's call this M1. The beam then goes through a particular part of the cube beamsplitter, let's call this coorrdinate x_i and splits and then hits each mirror. If you now rotate M1 by theta, I assume you will see the beam move to a new position x_m. The difference x_1-x_m is now the total displacement of the beam. It's obvious then:

x_i - x_m = dtan(theta)

If the beam is expanded and causes a disc of radius r_m on the wall, then the disc will clearly be displaced from it's diameter d_i to a new position d_m such that

x_i/x_m = d_i/d_m.

If this did not happen, then we would have to assume that the beamsplitter is itself a constant of motion. If the spatial frequency on the wall is sigma, then:

|d_i - d_m| <= 1/sigma

will give a "no-John-Thump condition". ie you can thump it from now to creation and the fringes won't move. If however

|d_i - d_m| > 1/sigma

then the fringes will be equally displaced. Considering the incredibly low spatial frequencies on your wall, I suspect you'd have to thump it bloody hard to get the "thump" condition. Hence, quite correctly, the laser off the table before the beamsplitter will "work", up to a point, as I said. However, I suspect that if you thumped the laser continuously as the film is exposing, you'll find the "John-thumping effect will produce a different result! This is because the spatial frequency of the fringes o the film are several orders of magnitude higher than the ones on your wall.

It's always tempting to take a metaphor or model outside of it's realm of validity and then apply the results thus gained into a different realm where physical conditions are no longer realistically modelled. You're "thumping"excercise will work just as well before as after the beamsplitter if the spatial frequency is low enough. Take the limit to sigma=0 and clearly you have exceptional stability!

Interference in general is modelled by Moire fringes. I refer you to p9 of Nils Abramson "The Making and Evaluation of Holograms" Of course, you're free to argue with Nils Abramson, but I somehow believe that he's a more relaible source. What I said had nothing to do with before or after any beamsplitter. The relative displacement of two fringe systems is more sensitive that the absolute displacement of both fringe systems. In the prior case, the fringe equations show a sinusoidal dependancy, while in the latter the dependancy is linear. The sinusoidal function can be "linearised" by use of the Taylor series, such that

sin(theta) = theta - theta^2/2! + theta^4/4! ...+(-1)^(2n)*(theta^n)/n!

If we now restrict the Taylor series such that theta^2/theta < epsilon

(Cauchy condition) we can approximate theta as a linear function. From this we obviosly gather that at low angles, we can ascribe absolute motion with relative motion. However, at higher angles (hence higher spatial frequencies) relative motion is a different beastie altogether. Unless, of course, you "thumped" the Taylor series!

Be a little careful with this type of advice. It's a little simplistic and can easily be misconstrued. You can't put a plate on top of a cloth doll or a fuzzy toy and shoot on a kitchen table, even though it appears "stable". Putting a plate on a balsa wood figure on the kitchen table might seem stable, but it isn't. Stability means stability down to the sub-micron level, not the appearance of stability in the macro sense. From what I saw of the Liti film, this may have been a problem. Their instructions were very similar - simply lean the plate against the object. As Bob has said, this is a "hobby killer". A lot of newcomers try this and get nothing simply because they don't have any sense of the kind of stability required in holography. I know that some have made holograms in impossible situations (I've even made one in the midle of an earthquake), but these are the exceptions and not the rule. It's best to start simply, but correctly. The more less problems you intoroduce, the less problems you need to troubleshoot if something goes wrong.
