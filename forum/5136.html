John, the basic idea of combining DCG and silver halide has already been investigated. It was documented by a Bulgarian group in Optical and Quantum Electronics14 (1982), pages 311-315. However, I don't think the silver and DCG were "combining" to produce some kind of cumulative index modulation. I believe the function of the silver was simply to increase the sensitivity of the DCG. (?) It's been a long time since I read that paper, though. Martin is also familiar with it.

The title is "Dichromated gelatin for volume holographic recording with high sensitivity. Part I"

Here is the abstract:

"Dichromated gelatin would be the most suitable irreversible medium for holographic recording but for its low sensitivity. The efficiency of the photoinduced reduction of the chromium ion increases considerably if superfine grains of silver halide crystals are present in the layer. The light sensitivity achieved is about 10 times better than that of dichromated gelatin layers, obtained and processed in the conventional way."

This statement is not just really so John. As you know, the wonderful virtually unique way silver halide can be broken down by light relies on the formation of latent image atoms of silver being formed by light normally with sensitizing dyes involved. These freed silver atoms can then go on to infect the hole grain of thousands of AgBr ion-pairs when the plate is put in developer. The photosensitivity process is dependent on there being an easily available source of electrons present which can readily supply the silver ion Ag+ with its missing electron to make it “Ag0” ---the formula for that black metal.

In otherwords it needs a reducing environment to enable it to occur , the developer of course being a strong reducing agent . Dichromate (which becomes chromate in alkaline soln.but the same oxygen to chromium ratio is maintained), is as you know a powerful oxidant because each two chromium atoms only need 3 oxygens between them to form their strong very stable “chromium lll” arrangements and that leaves the other 4 dichromate oxygens with their missing electrons to go off and seize them back to form more energetically favourable bonds with organic substances, above all with those strongly reducing photographic developers, but they can also get electrons off silver metal, Ag0 to form Ag+ again.

The most energetic form of silver metal is the latent image spec which is made up of just a few atoms. The slightest whiff of dichromate and those latent image specs are gone! ( to reform Ag+ again or perhaps more correctly never allow the formation of Ag0 metal in the first place).

Quite by coincidence our photoscience archive plunderer Martin send me the following at the end of an email today.

Martin

“P.S. Digging in those old French journals, Bulletin de la société française de photographie (put online at Gallica), I run into some articles that raised my curiosity. There was a description of a method of forming a silver halide emulsion under daylight. The magic ingredient said to allow that, was potassium chromate, which had been added early on to the emulsion. “

So the coexistence of dichromate/chromate and latent silver image is just not a goer and neither is the necessary developer for the silver possible in the presence of dichromate. You can introduce the dichromate after the silver development step to get the SHDCG effect of course and you and many of us have discussed this at length on this forum with Joe’s post of ? and earlier.

An obvious item to me that makes rationalizing DCG processing more difficult is the use of proprietary fix/hardner and not knowing which is doing what, as you say yourself.

Would hardener alone have given you those results in your pics? If so then what hardener? If one goes for chrome alum hardener then one is of course competing with the primary process from the exposed dichromate for mainly carboxylic acid sites to crosslink along the polypeptide chain. If one goes for aldeyde hardening then one has most of the –NH2 sites along the chains available (but aldehydes like to get themselves oxidized by any unwashed out dichromate ions around).

The “fix” bit can be a real complication but only if you have an initial high unreacted dichromate level around that can oxidize it thus forming chromium lll crosslinker. Is it really needed?

I wonder if I have an answer to the above bit of your post by plugging in my experience over 15 years ago using soft gelatin with the lowest degree of hardness bias to avoid the use of hot alcohol baths and fire risk. So I was working at the other end of the hardness-bias range so to speak.

The key to the answer may be in the last sentence here taken from a paragraph from my paper in Applied Optics 30, 1589-1602 (1991)

…………..It is very helpful to keep the following mechanical model in mind. If one hangs weights on to the end of a steel extension spring, then removing those weights will result in the spring recovering its original un-extended length unless the weight exceeds a critical value. When a weight is of that critical value, the material of the spring has reached just beyond what is known as the "yield point". When that weight is removed the spring would normally never completely recover its original un-extended length but will remain permanently distorted. Had the material of the spring been a little stiffer, then that particular weight would not have stretched the spring beyond its yield point. If the temperature of the stiffer spring were to be raised however, then that weight may again take the spring beyond its yieldpoint.

DCG in water behaves similarly to the stretched spring. At a certain temperature, the stretching forces due to swelling in water may take the gelatin beyond its yield point. Had the water temperature been lower, then the gelatin would not have reached its yield point.

The art of making the most noise-free and yet bright DCG holograms, is to take the gelatin as close as possible to its yield point without exceeding it. Stretching gelatin beyond its yield point creates noise in the finished hologram. Using the onion layer analogy of Curran and Shankoff, the holographic fringe layers might resemble dried out onion with crinkled-up layers if the yield point is exceeded. A sign that you have got the swelling just right is no milkiness in the exposed area but milkiness in the unexposed area.

Do you think that there may have been a fairly precise critical exposure level needed in your film then and below that level you got milkiness? What was your object? Was it returning a good bit of light, or was it diffusing it?(in which case my idea likely to be wrong). So the light returning strongly from a nice flat bright object creates strong fringes with the reference beam in the film closest to the object.

By their nature the bright fringes may have actually had MORE light intensity than the reference beam light that first passed through the glass. (Ignoring the serious attenuation, the intensity would be technically DOUBLED at the cost of the zero intensity in the dark fringes.)

So could the reference beam light inspite of being gradually attenuated by DCG absorbance have actually been sufficiently intensified next to the object to take you above that critical exposure level while the reference beam intensity at the glass interface just fell below that level? Hence the phenomenon you got. (I have seen that effect myself too).
