I have not made a hologram right on the floor but I bet if you choose a nice rigid subject that has a nice flat to set on the floor and you can set a plate right on top the object with nice support you will have no problems at all with stability issues if your fringes are that nice on the floor to make a hologram. A good examply would be three dice laying on the floor in a triangle. Then set the plate right on the dice. Use an overhead beam at about a 55 degree angle and you shold be good to go. Keep us posted if you try it! For the length of time you say they are solid you should be fine.

It's funny you state that. While I was working in Jersey for a holographer they were looking for a new lab location. They set up appointments with the landlord or owner. We would take a laser, all the bases, mounts and optics needed to set up an interferomter. And we would set it up on the floor and let it set the entire time we were there to see how good or bad the ground stability was. The landlord thought we were a little nuts but it was a good test.

Prior to PCGI, I set up and interferomter with a fringe locker in my basement on the floor. It was the easiest way of setting up the fringe locker to see if it still worked as I wanted to take it to the PCG meeting. My floor is good but locked in very well with the fringe locker until I would shift my weight, slowly. Without the fringe locker the weight shift looked like a steady move of the fringes in one direction. With the fringe locker the fringes would pop to the next locked location, dwell still for a while then pop again etc.. Of course this was a slow shift of weight.

Anyway, it's fun to watch and study an interferemeter no matter where it is.

Remember though, you are looking for a fraction of a wavelenght move. So make sure your fringes are expanded big enough to see them move 1/4 of the distance from a light fringe to a dark fringe. If they are truely that solid for the length of time needed for an exposure, I would venture to say you could even do a split beam hologram on the floor. But I would study those fringes for a long while. It may be your floor was built as a slab on sand or a soft base and has some dampening mechanism built in.

Peace!

Ed Wesly Mon Apr 25, 2005 7:10 pm #5
Not only have I made holograms on the floor, but of the floor!

The guy who inspired me to start making holograms on the floor was Nils Abramson. In his books there is the famous study of milling machines done in the '70s by him and Hans Bjelkhagen. He had a spatial filter screwed on to an S-P 125, illuminating a complete milling machine. Near to the mill was a front surface mirror on a heavy piece of metal to reflect light back as a reference beam, directly to the plateholder which was close to the laser. He used 10E75 plates, but exposures were still in the 10's of seconds.

Not only did the holograms come out, they were used for sandwich holographic interferomtry. Two plates on top of each other were exposed to the machine at rest. Two more plates were exposed with a bit of stress induced on the milling head. The top of one set was coupled with the bottom of the other, and fringes of stress could be seen! There was a piece of heavy metal in the scene that was painted white and never moved between exposures, and the sandwich holograms would be tilted so that there were no fringes on the standard, showing only the stresses relative to the head and the tool.

Doing SBR on the floor with the laser at one end of the room with a spreader and the object at the other works pretty well. I have shot two beam reflection gratings on the floor and they worked too!

Reflection image plane transfers were routinely done by Lasergruppen Holovision in the '80's. When I worked at CFC we were shooting 45" by 45" diffraction gratings, with 10 minute exposures on photo-resist, on the floor. They were fringe-locked, but still...

As long as you don't have crazy traffic outside your house you should be able to get away with it!

Ed Wesly Tue Apr 26, 2005 5:58 pm #10
I wasn't there but I have seen the holograms. Nils used Agfa 10E75 plates, unbleached, so the exposure time would be about 1/32 of a bleached 8E75HD. I would guess that he developed them for about 4 to 6' also.

And Nils would have developed them himself! He was a real hands on guy!

It's too bad that there are no products currently available like the 10E75 or 10E56 Agfa Holotest plates of yore. Their grain sizes were around 90 nm, so reflection holos were out of the question, but processed in the Pyrochrome developer and bleach they could be just as bright as 8E in the transmission case but a tad noisier.

There was a Kodak product with similar speed for He-Ne (but not for Ruby) called SO-253 Film or Type 131 Plate. If you can get ahold of either of the Agfa or Kodak give them a try!

If anyone doesn't believe that 10E can give great results, send me a 5 dollar bill and a stamped, self-addressed envelope and I will send you a zone plate and a low frequency grating, both of which will cast a dark shadow in the zero order, plus an achromatic image plane transfer, all done on 70mm wide 10E75 sprocketed film that was left over from bubble chamber holography at Fermilab!
