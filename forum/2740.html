Since the options for a blue laser (for holography) are very limited (to say the least) Colin's post about Analog Technologies caught my attention. They have (?) a 50mW 405nm laser for a good price.

Does anyone care to speculate about a 405nm laser for three-wavelength color holography? That is, a HeNe for the red, a DPSS for the green, and this one for the blue. If 405nm works, the price tag on the Analog Technologies laser ($3,150) looks a lot better. (Assuming the laser works and they are able to deliver!)

Dinesh Sun Sep 26, 2004 12:09 am #6
There's a vast range of different wavelengths possible and these "gamut wars" have been going on for quite a while. If you look at the work of Paul Hubel , you'll see a totally differrent gamut. Each side has their own reasons for their favorite gamut. As Bob says, you can always swell anyway. Obviously the greater the gamut, the greater the range of wavelengths. There are also other factors involved. Recently I've discovered that it's not just the gamut per se. The impression of color is very different from the inclusion of color - radiometric vs photometric. The impression of color we sense is not just the radiometric combination of a specific set of wavelengths, but the inclusion of weighting factors on the photopic curve. I'm now beginning to believe there's probably not any specific "ideal gamut" at all. I think we need to get away from the whole RGB color space. To add further to the confusion, there are recent papers that seem to indiocate that any gamut must consist of more than just rgb, but must involve 4 or more. The idea being that a polygon is more representative than a triangle.

Kaveh Sun Sep 26, 2004 3:01 pm #11
> Dinesh: There's a vast range of different wavelengths possible and these "gamut wars" have been going on for quite a while.

Love it: Gamut wars!!

I am worried that 405nm might be too short, just because it is on the limit of the sensitivity of the eye. Think of it this way. To get the maximum gamut, you can use the deepest blue that the eye can see. But the eye can only just see it, so it will be very dim. So you have to come in a bit, i.e. increase the wavelength, so you can see the damn thing!!

I think people have complicated the subject too much. If you don't have the perfect set of wavelengths, it just means you'll get some distortion in the colours. Yellows will appear brown or pink, blues might be the wrong shade, etc. But you will get a colour hologram for sure. So go for it, with the caveat that 405 might be too dark to see.

And yes, you can use two wavelengths only. In fact you would start that way to begin with. The result will be surprisingly good. The eye will fill in the missing colours.

I also have difficulty in understanding some of the papers on colour holography. I tried to figure out Paul Hubel's papers, but too many integrals!!

BobH Sun Sep 26, 2004 5:53 pm #14
In my opinion, any discussion of multi-wavelength "full color" holography (as opposed to simply "color" holography ... they're not black & white) regarding which wavelengths to use, saturation or color rendering is moot unless the final hologram is installed in a display with a known reconstruction source and geometry. Furthermore, the hologram would need to be produced for that display only, and tuned for the source used.

I believe the general public sees all holograms as "color" holograms, and that for some reason the holographer chose a single color to render their subject. Again, nobody makes black & white holograms! I think this is part of why holograms are not very well accepted. The colors available (mostly burnt orange, jaundiced yellow, pea green, and dark green) are not very appealing.

Arguments about color gamut and pictorial holography are missing the forest for the trees. And speaking of trees, I made a hologram of a cannabis plant a while ago, using

a) Ron Olson's pulsed green laser for the master,

b) a He-Ne to make a ganged inter-master (by pre-swelling with TEA) which reconstructed at 514nm, and

c) an Argon Ion laser for production in Dupont's photopolymer.

The final photopolymer holograms (and the hand made copies made by me in AgX with the He-Ne) are very close to the color of the original subject. This is an example of a "full color" hologram made with a single wavelength (the different wavelengths used are academic, the image could have been produced all the way through with 532nm).

This example contradicts my opinion above only because the "green" of the image can vary quite a bit and still represent the subject acceptably. When a scene requireing the full spectrum is contemplated, I think the "reconstruction" part of wavefront reconstruction must be incorporated into the discussion.

I think this question of why holograms are not well accepted is a perennial question in holography. The problem as I see it is that the public (and holographers!) don't see a role for holography in the public eye. Image construction and reconstruction are based on some sense of utility as well as aesthetics and each method of image reconstruction is based on some ratio of these two. I think both we and the public tend not to notice this. If someone were to ask, "Do you like photographs?" I would imagine the question to be meaningless because it has no context. On the other hand we make these blanket statements about holography as if it should be judged by and of itself with no contextual background.

I think monochromatic holograms do have a role, but not simply to hang on a wall and admired just for themselves. Similarly "full colour" holograms have a different role and also not just to be hung on a wall. When we say the public has not accepted holography we tend to forget that they've accepted it perfectly well on credit cards, on magazine covers, postage stamps and even wrapping paper. However, many holographers tend to scoff and say, "Well they're not real holograms, they're dot matrix/mass produced/security or some such dismissive term. We tend to see a glass plate (usually dark) with some object or objects in it in garish colour(s) and think of that as a real hologram. We then rack our minds and go head-to-head about making it more real/less real/more abstract etc etc and wonder why the public don't like this. It seems to me that without context a glass plate with a cat or a plant or a dolphin or even a miniature dinosaur is simply that.

If a hologram is to factually and identically convey visual impressions and information, I feel it needs to do so in some context where such a representation is required - a print ad or a book illustration eg. If the hologram is to convey the essence of some visual object without complete factual information - a green dolphin eg - then it may be appropriate in some context but not in the standard context of any pictorial representation the public is familiar with. Certainly no one would buy a green dolphin just to hang on a wall and impress their friends. If the intent is to do just this, it needs to have the whole culture of pictorial art embedded somehow in it. A green dolphin or multicoloured butterflies ain't Picasso!

I've always wondered why you don't see more achromats in embossed holograms. After all, you can shoot an achromat onto resist just as easily as a rainbow and electroplate it. The average spatial frequency is about the same. Jason Sappan did a very stunning one for a magazine cover where you had a white (achromat) image against a rainbow background. But in general, you tend not to see that many achromats. We made a small (2.5in x 2.5in achromat folded into a small box. I think you may have missed the point. Of course, everyone likes photographs. However, it's always a specific kind of photograph, not a general lumping of all photographs. It seems it's pretty true of almost anything. Perhaps I could phrase the question: "Do you like food?" Of course everyone likes some kind of food, but the question as posed is meaningless. I, for example, like photographs of outdoor panoramas if done well. However tourist photos of "Our Hotel" or "The Ski Slope" have value only to the family and some friends of the photographer. We're all aware of how boring it can be to see someone's holiday snaps or pictures of Mickey on "The Trip To California".

My point was that, just like photographs, holograms need context. I think it's impossible to just "like" or "not like" holograms. I'm pretty indifferent to holograms of routine single objects, be they dolphins or glass bottles. Just the fact that you can make a hologram of a dolphin or glass bottle in orange/blue or orange and blue is pretty "bleah!". However, I like Dan Scweitzer's work a lot, also Rudy Berkhout. I liked Lon Moore's pseudocoloured rocks when I first saw them. However, my attempts at pseusdocolour back in the 80's were bleah! (except to me, of course!). Peter Miller did some pretty stuinning work on pseudocolour. I like the way Tom B presents the subject matter. Just like photographs, you can't just "like holograms". I think it's pretty similar to the public at large. They're not impressed with glass bottles in orange or multicoloured butterflies or green dolphins because to the public, this is very simuilar to "Little Denise posing with Goofy with the Disney castle in the background" It's awfully sweet to the parents of Little Denise and awfully bleah! to the rest of us! The public needs context or cultural value. A pink flamingo singing Elvis has cultural value (to some!) but a hologram of a pink flamingo has none,except to the holographer, his/her friends and family and to those who think every hologram is stupendous! wonderful! amazing! If holographers think this, I think they need to realise that not everyone does. There are holographers who've achieved some technical feat or done something in a non-standard way. This may, or may not impress other holographers, but then to say "Why don't the public like holograms?" when you parade a hologram in orange or bright colours or made with soda cans or that faithfully represent this or that colour is simply meaningless to them. Lenticulars come very, very large and in 3D. OK, there's no parallax, but there's colour, size and depth. Our main competition has nothing to do with "3D" (an overhyped word these days, anyway) but our fascination with a medium irrespective of any cultural "hook"

By the way, by "achromat" I was not referring to broadband DCG but to the transmission format that creates a greyscale image in any medium.
