Kaveh Mon Dec 10, 2007 11:11 pm #2
> Justin W: A. How does one determine the diffraction efficiency of a transmission hologram?

Generally very hard to measure, unless you have a diffraction grating, i.e. the object beam is just a plane beam. Then you can put a detector in either beam and measure the strength in each. The diffraction efficiency would be the ratio of the light in the diffracted beam to that in the beam incident on the hologram.

Problem is that in general an object and therefore image are more complex, and it is very hard to measure all the light that has been diffracted. You could take the incident reconstruction beam, then measure the portion of the beam transmitted undiffracted, and guess from there, but then you need to make adjustment for light lost in noise, surface reflections, etc.

MichaelH Tue Dec 11, 2007 4:13 pm #6
> Justin W: B. What is the optimal DE for a transmission holo created for the purpose of mastering into a reflection copy? My Spidey Sense tells me there is a happy medium somewhere... Or is brighter better? Always?

As long as you can get enough brightness to keep your exposures within the time limits imposed by your table (some people can do many minutes of exposure time, some like me can't) you're best off going as bright as you can get.

The real limiting factor when making image plane copies is the contrast levels. For most currently available low-end silver materials (I can't comment on the behavior of DCG) you will get the best results will an even-contrast master. Really bright areas in the master will tend to burn out in the copy if the other areas are bright and you've set your exposure for those latter areas.

BobH Sat Dec 15, 2007 5:16 pm #8
To measure the overall DE of a pictorial transmission hologram, I'd:

a) illuminate it with a collimated beam,

b) catch all the transmitted reconstruction light that's not diffracted into a focusing mirror or lens,

c) place a detector at the focal point,

d) measure the reconstruction beam's power when the hologram is in it, but mounted so that the beam is incident from the wrong direction so as to not reconstruct the image (this will be your "available reconstruction beam"), and finally

e) mount the hologram so that the image is reconstructed, and measure the transmitted beam power again.

Subtracting the second measurement from the first will give you the total light that was diffracted into the image, and that divided by the first measurement will give you your diffraction efficiency. All the surface reflections, absorption and scattering are accounted for.
