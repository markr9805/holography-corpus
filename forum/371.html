BobH Thu Oct 27, 2011 10:59 pm #3
I use a half wave plate on the input side of the main beamsplitter to adjust the beam ratio, and another one in one of the beams after the beamsplitter to make both beams the same again. It's always a good idea to put the input waveplate as far away from the front of the laser so it can be tilted slightly to avoid the problems mentioned above, even if it's AR coated.

Same with the reflected beams off all the other components. I love keeping all the beams as lined up with the holes on the table. Doing that invariably leads to reflections going back into the laser, and none of them like that!

Ed Wesly Mon Oct 31, 2011 1:11 pm #6
Are you using a reflection/transmission metallic coated beamsplitter? Then you certainly could put the half-wave plate before the beamsplitter to align both beams to the same vector orientation. Your ratio will change as the polarization rotates, since horizontal and vertical polarization (s and p) reflections vary in intensity.

You can see this easily by observing the reference beam light reflected from a glass plate in your plateholder on a white piece of paper. Rotate the polarization vector with the half-wave plate until the reflection is minimized for the maximum penetration and the minimal woodgrain. When you find this orientation you will find that the transmission is maximized and the reflection is also minimized at the beamsplitter, if the BS and the plateholder are in parallel orientation.

With polarizing beamsplitting cubes you do need a second one (or even a third) after the cube to align both transmitted and reflected beams as Bob notes above. Put that on your Christmas list, as 50 mW can start doing damage to metallized coatings.

And to just echo some of the above, tilting the plate just a tad so the back reflections don't go into the laser is to be preferred. I once saw a back reflection off a fiber optic go back into the output of the 25 Joule monster Ruby at Fermilab, and that backward going beam got amplified by the last two amplifier rods and the next one in took it on the chin! Even when you retroreflect a beam back into a He-Ne, you can see the output oscillate. With your DPSS I would steer the beam clear of normal.

Jeffrey Weil Fri Nov 04, 2011 8:06 am #10
Hello John K,

That's not really a good idea. If you tilt the waveplate that much its resonant thickness will change and you won't be efectivly rotating the polarazation at your target freq anymore.

I used to buy old 670 waveplates surplus and tilt them until they worked well with He-Ne red.

As you tilt your waveplate more it appears to be thicker to the beam. The beam has to go through a thicker diagonal. It's no longer resonant to the original design frequency.

It's best to have them as far as possible from the laser so the tilt to keep back reflections going into the laser is as small as possible.

Jeff W
