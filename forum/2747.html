[quote:5560b42ab6]it should be mentioned that undiverged laser beams are very dangerous.[/quote:5560b42ab6]

Oh, I agree. But it's possible to over-exaggerate the dangers of "lasers". The problem I've encountered is firstly that people seem to think that because a light source is a "laser" it is somehow inherently more dangerous than a thermal source. The eye itself is unaware of the coherence state of the incoming intensity. The difference made by a highly coherent form of light is that all of the energy is contained in a narrow range of wavelengths and, due to spatial coherency, the wavefront combine additiviely (rather than randomly). The nett effect is that an equivalent amount of incoherent radiation requires aa larger source. Sunlight is about 1 W/sq m on the earth's surface (give or take a little) or 0.1 mW/sq cm. This is, of course, spread over the entire spectrum. Therefore a small white square about a sq cm or so in bright sunlight throws 0.01 mW/sq cm of 633 into your eye (I'm assuming that about 1/10th of white light is about 633 +/- 50-odd nm. If this had been an actual calculation, you'd have to integrate over the photopic curve). Of course, a small white square of the same dimensions in a 50mW laser beam throws much more light into your eye. Therefore, lasers are dangerous. However, assume a brightly lit large white surface, such as a large white wall, in bright sunlight and you get a considerably greater light being thrown into your eye than light from a 50 mW laser falling on a 4x5 plate after expansion, collimation etc. And yet, I've never heard anyone say that it's dangerous to look at large white walls! When I was first "warned" of the dangers of lasers, at Applied by a holographer called James Copp, I carried out this calculation to show them that large white walls were more dangerous. Unfortunately, it was the first of many encounters where just demonstrating something mathematically gives the impression that you're trying to "pull a fast one".

[quote:5560b42ab6]Don't forget that the "physicists" are responsible for the radiation health policy that left thousands of nuclear weapons workers with cancer in the US Nuclear programs with their idea of "acceptable" exposure. [/quote:5560b42ab6]

Again, it's possible to exaggerate the "dangers of science" by examples like these. I've read somewhere that when cars were first available there was an impression that anyone going faster than 40 mph would asphyxiate! the air would be going too fast around their heads to be able to breathe. On the other hand, when public policy is cretaed based on faulty science and scare-mongering of the Evils of Science, you could end up going too far the other way. I think you need to balance the benefits of science against it's dangers. Certainly unknown factors in any new technology may come back to haunt you. I could mention defoliants, insecticides such as DDT, even radioactivity itself (Early workers would count alpha-particles by looking at scinitllations created by the particles on their eyelids!). But balance the danger of radioactivity against radiation therapy for cancer, the danger of insecticide against the huge increase in food manufacture that allows this planet to support some 6 billion people, unhappily not in a statistically uniform way etc etc. My argument was that if public policy and the court system are based on bogus weak science, we all end up the poorer. There are still people out there who think they have a design for a perpetual motion machine! There are people out there who fervently believe in ether! Don't even get me started on Cold Fusion To abandon any technology because of inherent dangers is, I think, to abandon science and technology completely. To embrace every "new" technology wholeheartedly with no reservation is, I think overly naive and somewhat stupid. Somewhere there's a balance, like everything else.

[quote:f727779607]Light produced by a laser is more dangerous because it comes from a point source, and therefore can be kept concentrated for longer distances and focused (by the eye) to a smaller spot size.[/quote:f727779607]

I've heard this before and I can never understand why. The "point source" of a laser does not mean it has an infinite spatial coherence. The spot size is w_0 and the divergence is generally about 5 mrad. So, yes it's spatial coherence is very high, but not infinite. It must, at least be about half a micron across, probably a lot more. Even if it were a "point", the focused beam in your eye is diffraction lmited and so has an Airy radius dependant on the pupil size. This is small, but no more so than any other small source of light from far away. As for being "concentrated, I assume again that means a high spatial coherence and so maintaining the wavefront over greater distances before dispersion. But any small, distant source is also highly spatially coherent. It may not be as spatially coherent as a laser, but I submit that when looking at a halogen bulb from about 20 feet away, the increase in intensity more makes up for the (small) difference between the spatial coherence of a laser and that of the halogen (remember now I'm talking about spatial coherence, not temporal). In other words, if the feature that damages you is dependant on the spatial coherence of the source, staring at a halogen bulb from 20 odd feet away is more dangerous. I assume there's no argument that there's no danger from the temporal coherence aspect. I think there's a feeling that lasers somehow have an "optical" component that carries some invisible and unfelt damaging mechanism that's not immediately felt, such as chemical cell damage or radiation of the alpha, beta and gamma sort. Perhaps it's felt that "concentrating" a laser beam into your eye is like concentrating the sun onto grass or paper and causing it to burn. Low power lasers don't have a lot of heat, and if they did and your retina started to heat, you'd feel it! Again, remember that looking at your object lit up in a laser beam is also laser light concentrated on your retina (it has to be concentrated otherwise you wouldn't be able to see it). If the object has a bright, reflective point on it, then once more, you're concentrating a "point source" of laser light on your retina. Also, the far field Fraunhofer pattern of any object has point functions in the zero order and so any object focuses a point on your retina to some extent.

The point is that any light source is dangerous if it's too bright. If it hurts, look away! I think these super-bright modern car headlights, especially those small, bright blue ones, are far more dangerous than lasers. Esecially since you're looking at them with a dark-adapted eye and your pupil is at a maximum. Looking at the reflection of the sun in a car windscreen produces greater intensity in your retina than a 50 mW laser after expansion and collimation. A 1mW laser expanded to your pupil radius probably produces less light than a match flaring close to your eyes. To the eye light is light. Like any optical system, it focuses to a an Airy radius and then is picked up by the retina and partly processed and partly passed on. Unless the light source produces heat or radiation, I don't see how cell damage can occur without a warning sign which causes you to squint or blink or look away. Whether the light is spatially coherent to ahigh degree or not. For me, being forced to look at incredibly bright car headlights in my rear view mirror is far more painful than looking at a 100 mW beam after expansion and collimation by a 12 in mirror.

I'm not trying to downplay the importance of laser safety. One aspect of laser safety having to do with temporal coherence is that you have a feeling of security because lasers, especially the deep blue ones, don't "seem" very bright but produce quite a bit of intensity. It's easy to bbe careless and get zapped. However, by saying "Don't look at a laser" and not saying "Don't look at any bright small source, including therma sources" gives the impression that lasers are more dangerous because they're lasers. This produces the kind of policy that bans 1 mW pen lasers as "dangerous". From this, it's one small step to the "T" word!
