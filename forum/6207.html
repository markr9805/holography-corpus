Colin Kaminski Thu Apr 26, 2007 3:57 am #2
A Fourier Transform is based on the idea that any waveform can be made from an infinite sum of sine waves. This is how the graphic analyzer in a stereo or computer sound program works. It makes a frequency "bin" that has a finite width to allow the computation using an algorithm called an Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

Here is a more detailed description:

[http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform)

A related concept is holography is that of any fringe pattern recorded can be thought of as the sum of an infinite number of zone plates. A zone plate is a series of black and white rings like this:

![Image](http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/66/Zonenplatte_Cosinus.png/210px-Zonenplatte_Cosinus.png)

A zone plate uses Fresnel diffraction.

If one wants to make a fringe pattern from a 3-D model in a computer then one would need to define each point in x,y,z and luminosity (a 3-D vector) Then a zone plate can be calculated to project the point. If one does this to a large number of points and creates a complicated zone plate then one can make a transmission hologram of a computer object. The software to carry this out would need to use an FFT.

Now, I have never tried any of this or really studied anything but the basics of it so I likely have omitted key points and what not but that is the general idea. Perhaps Ed or Gregg will refine this crude explanation.

[http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_plate](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_plate)

Now when I think of it all I like to think of the hand drawn holograms. I think this is less computationally intensive but I have not tried it. An FFT is actually a very highly optimized piece of code. Fortunately you don't have to write your own FFT anymore here is an example:

[http://www.fftw.org/](http://www.fftw.org/)

Now a much more numerically intensive problem would be to calculate the 3 dimensional structure of the fringes coming from any object/reference beam combination. Then one could take a slice of this at any point and make a hologram. This is a quite useful thought experiment when thinking of Holographic Interferometry. With out understanding it you can not make any measurements of absolute values but now I am off topic.

alesnick Thu Apr 26, 2007 7:35 pm #4
the Fresnel and Fraunhofer approximations are just that -- approximations. they are used in the context of Fourier optics to describe the diffraction of light. When light passes through an aperture, the resulting profile is a function of the fourier transform of the aperture and the original beam profile.

The Fresnel approximation is more accurate in the near field, where a wavefront differs in both size and geometry as a function of distance (as is the case when light passes through a pinhole and diffuses in a 1/r^2 type pattern). Fraunhofer diffraction occurs once the propagated wavefront becomes planar, in the far field.

If I recall correctly, the Fresnel is also accurate in the far field, while the Fraunhofer gives a much more simplified but less accurate) approximation near the origin

(all of my knowledge comes from 2-d analysis of ultrasound transducers with different apertures -- MUCH simpler than 3-dimensions . . .)

fourier, fraunhofer, whats the difference?

the fourier transform is an _piece_ of evaluating the fresnel integral -- or, at least it _can_be.

The fresnel integral is a convolution of the plane wave profile and the calculated fresnel kernel -- this integral could be evaluated by hand, but very very few can actually be calculated analytically. if we take the fourier transform (or, computationally, the fast fourier transform) of both elements of the convolution, we can simply multiply the two FT's together. taking the inverse FFT, we end up with an answer that may have been impossible to get otherwise.

f conv g --> FT --> = F * G --> IFFT--> = f conv g

basically a way around the integral . . .

Stereofan -

Did that answer your question? Or are you asking "What's the difference between Fourier and Fresnel hologram configurations?"

In any case, I think there's a great thoughtfully-written textbook that really repays the time you spend absorbing the details: Optics by Hecht. A more advanced book is written by Goodman, but that's too much of a jump into the deep end at first.

Also, Steve Benton's [MIT course notes](http://mas450.syntheticholography.org/reading/chaptersPDF/index.html) are still available online. It sounds like that would be a good place for you to build up intuition about these things.

You might want to flip through Hariharan's holography text for some quick answers too.

There are several sections on introductory Fourier optics -- that is, ways in which certain types of mathematical analysis allows you to reach some very interesting conclusions about how lenses and holograms do to light.

Don't worry if it sounds baffling. A lot of us still think it's baffling; the math is complicated (at least to non-mathematician engineers) and it's easy to get confused when non-experts use certain scientific terms in the wrong way, which happens from time to time even in books.
