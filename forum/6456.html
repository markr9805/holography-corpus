I've been going through a struggle of learning how to make the best use I can of my Coherent 315 and VRP-M film and finally hit on a regime that works well for creating transmission masters. I'll be posting this on my blog with loads of pictures but until that's ready I thought I'd put the text here as well.

----------------

For almost two months now, I've been working to figure out the exposure and processing steps to use in order to get bright and clean transmission holograms using my Coherent 315M and VRP-M film.

After blowing through roughly 68 pieces of film (mostly 1.5x1" in size) I think I've finally nailed the process to use for transmission masters.

For those of you in the same situation, hopefully the following will shorten your experimentation phase.

1. Laser controller startup is key

After doing a number of preliminary tests with VRP-M I thought that I'd hit on a successful combination of exposure time, development and bleach to use and was ready to get down to the making of "real" holograms again.

Then my holograms started coming out dim and I had to head back to square one to find out what was wrong.

During this period I'd been doing all sorts of experiments such as changing the developer from JD2, a CW-C2 variant, to TJ1 and using them undiluted and diluted to find out the effect of each change on the final hologram. I'd also experimented with bleaches from EDTA to JD2, JD4 and Potassium Ferricyanide.

Each time I got a bright hologram I thought "great, now I'm ready" but the next hologram was likely to be dim and ugly.

The dim and ugly that with my HeNe would have said that I'd forgotten to measure beam lengths and would find they were way off once I went and measured them.

As it turns out, the problem was in the controller for the 315M.

But the controller wasn't the problem. I was.

I'd discovered that if I left the controller turned on and running and instead turned off the power to it "at the wall" I could turn the power back on and the controller would appear to go through its startup routine and hit the target power level each time. Normally it ends up 10mW lower than the target setting I'd chosen and I thought that this new startup method was a good thing.

I was wrong.

It turns out that starting up the laser this way results in unstable output. I haven't tested it in an interferometer, it's on my todo list now, but I've found through hologram making that by, at the very least, hitting the target power level switch the controller will go through a complete startup and I can make consistently bright holograms as long as I do that and follow up by using the right chemicals.

Which leads me to the next section.

2. VRP-M hates EDTA bleach

The high fog levels I was seeing in the first set of bleached holograms led me to believe that the film I'd bought back in January, which has a made on date of Oct 2005, was ruined by the high humidity we had here in North Texas this Spring and Summer.

It's not nearly as bad as I'd thought now that I'm not using EDTA bleach.

I've experimented now with EDTA, JD2, JD4 and Potassium Ferricyanide and while I can't tell you why the PotFerr bleach works the best, when combined with JD2 developer, it provides the highest contrast results of the four.

JD2 and EDTA give similar results in terms of brightness and fog levels, both higher than I can work with and while an Ascorbic post-treatment increases the contrast it also darkens the image more than I can accept.

JD4 is better but even with newer film (Jan 2007) there's a bit of fog. It's usable though and with a light Ascorbic treatment the fog is wiped out with only slight dimming of the image.

Potassium Ferricyanide gives the best results of the all of them. Right out of the bath the holograms are slightly brighter than JD4 and the contrast is much better. Black areas are black and "white" areas are cleaner.

3. VRP-M prefers low light ratios

I've also found that VRP-M works very well with low light ratios. I've gone as low as 1.1:1 and couldn't see any increase in noise in the final hologram.

At the moment I've settled on a 2:1 ratio mostly for emotional reasons. I'll do some more experimentation along this line with other subjects as time goes by.

So to recap:

1. Make sure you put your laser through the proper startup procedure each time you power it up.

2. Until something better is identified for transmission holograms, at least use JD2 developer and JD4 bleach. If you've got the chemicals, use Potassium Ferricyanide bleach as the contrast is much better.

3. If you're doing split beam work, go with lower light ratios. The images will be brighter and there's no visible difference in noise level of the final hologram.

There's not a great deal of brightness difference between the 20 and 40 second pieces but still a clear difference in contrast between the JD4 and PotFerr pieces. There's actually less difference in brightness than the photos would imply.

This in from TJ...

> I had discovered last spring that when using PFG-03M and JD-4 for makingreflection holograms, a pre-soak in distilled water for just 5 - 10seconds before development does miracles!!! It completely eliminates thecloud-like fog structure. Using the presoak, even three years old PFG-03emulsion still yield good results. This discovery was arrived at byreasoning: in fast developers, the outer layers of the emulsion gets"attacked" first, then seeps through the inner layers. This can be seenby the pattern of the cloud-like fog - it depends the exact motion of howthe plate enters the developer. By pre-loading the emulsion with water,this process is slowed down and results in a more even development.

I haven't compared the visual results between soaking and not but I have seen with my small tests that when soaking I can clearly see the edges darkening before the center of the film. My most recent tests (included in the writeup) did not have the film soaked before development as it appeared to result in more even development, which goes against the common wisdom.

Michael,

In working with your Coherent , have you tried Agfa plates as a comparison to VRP-M? My own results now favor VRP-M for producing reflection H2’s as the considerably slower speed (compared to Agfa) seems to produce more uniform exposures – without burning. But in that my chemistry (Ed’s elixir SM-6) is different as are my exposure times, you may want to test for yourself and for producing H1 transmission masters – Agfa Millimask plates buy you a lot with respect to speed. I always use colloidal post-processing with my Agfa H2’s (Ascorbic Acid with a 150W halogen bulb at 1 meter for 7-10 minutes) but never with H1’s to enhance contrast and reduce noise. Regarding latensification and VRP-M: the jury is still out. What I gain in brightness, I tend to pay for in noise. If I’m working with an extremely bright master I avoid it altogether but it does help when the H1 is on the dim side. I latensify all my VRP-M transmission H1's but it's not clear that I need to - I'm just afraid that if I get a really dim master I'll kick myself for not spending the extra minute. I use a 3W green LED with a 10 degree spread at a distance of 2 meters for ~1 minute. Colloidal post-processing seems to have very minimal effect on VRP-M – not worth the bother. Just in passing, I still have yet to see any long-term ill effects from Blyth colour-tuning and am using it exclusively on all my presentation pieces - Agfa and VRP-M.
BobH Thu Oct 11, 2007 7:38 pm #21
> Has anyone taken to index matching the master to reduce noise?

I always did/do that. A large component of intermodulation noise is recorded as a surface relief, so index matching the transmission master allows it to be made with a lower beam ratio for higher efficiency. The low frequency fringes that cause "burns" on the H2 are a big part of the intermodulation noise that's suppressed by index matching the H1. Using 12x16" masters meant a large space on a bench for the index-matching workstation, and the fumes from all that decalin.
