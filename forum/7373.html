JohnFP Wed Jun 11, 2008 10:11 am #2
Well, let me see if I can explain this clearly.

We want to split our beam. We can do this a number of ways. All will work.

1. Use a piece of glass or a coated partially reflecting beam splitter. These are set in the ratio of reflected to transmitted light. Glass usually yields about 10% reflected and 90% transmitted depending on angle and wavelength but cannot be adjusted. Same with the partially reflective beam splitters. These are set to a ratio and cannot be adjusted. The benefit of these beams splitter is they are inexpensive. The glass and the cheaper ones may produce a double reflected beam which will need to be blocked. In using glass, this can be accomplished by using a thick piece of glass such that the two reflected beams are separated enough to allow one to be blocked off. Also, to change the ratio of reflected to transmitted light you actually have to change the optic. This technique can add considerable set up time when finding optimal ratios during your tweaking of ratios.

2. A variable beam splitter. They can come in graduated or stepped. They are moderately priced. The main drawback with these beam splitters is that if the beam splitter is circular then you need to rotate the beam splitter to adjust the transmitted to reflected ratio. If the mount holding the beam splitter is not a very tight and low tolerance mount (high quality), then when you spin the beam splitter, the reflected light may not stay in the same relative position. Same holds true for a linear variable beam splitter. If the slide mount is not of high quality, then when the beam splitter is moved linearly, the reflected beam can move relative to it previous location. This technique can add some additional set up time when finding optimal ratios during your tweaking of ratios depending on quality of mounting for the beam splitter.

3. A Polarizing Beam Splitter. I will try to explain the holography application. A little more costly with all three optics. This uses an optic (1/2 wave plate) prior to the beam splitter that changes the direction of the polarization by rotating the 1/2 wave plate. Because all the light is passing through the wave plate and no usable beam is being reflected, the tolerance of the rotation mount does not have to be as critical as the circular variable beam splitter. The beam then passed through the polarizing beam splitter cube which separates the P polarized part of the beam from the S polarized part of the beam, one being reflected and one being transmitted. So if the beam is polarized fully in the P direction then all (well most) of the light will follow the path of the P beam. And likewise, if all of the light is polarized in the S direction then all (most) of the light will follow the path of the S beam. Now back to that 1/2 wave plate. If we rotate this plate such that the polarization of the beam coming out of the 1/2 wave plate changes the polarization 45 degrees off P or S (right in between) then 50% of the light will follow the P path and 50% of the light will follow the S path. So as you can see by rotation the 1/2 wave plate you can change the reflected to transmitted light ratio. BUT.... since polarization is important in creating interference fringes, we need yet another 1/2 wave plate after the beam splitter in either the transmitted or reflected beam so that we can rotate it's polarization to match the polarization of the other beam. The benefits of this system are, you can easily change the ratio and both beams will not move and can actually stay aligned in your spatial filters. This can save considerable time in tweaking your beam ratios. There is also no issue with dual reflected beams.

Successful split beam holography can be accomplished with either three of these methods and it depends on your available funds, time and technique to make either of these work.

I hope this helps, have a great day!

JohnFP Fri Jun 13, 2008 12:30 pm #6
Colin, why would you need three? Where does the third one go? I have always only used two. One prior to BS and one in the incorrect polarized beam.

IE., if your set up is side lit reference beam then you want to have P polarization. The polarizing cube separates the P and S polarizations. So put the second wave plate in the S polarized beam to rotate it to P. Thus both beam are polarized correctly for brewsters.

I mentioned this on the forum and Kaveh showed me how to rotate polarization using two mirror which change one angle at a time, a really cool technique.

[http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustration ... irrors.jpg](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/PolarizRotateWMirrors.jpg)

Your garden variety BS has downfalls, but surely can work. See my comments on the three types of BS in this thread.

If you can see any optics in your laser that are set to brewsters then that will instantly tell you your polarization.

If you have the sealed type lasers, then set it on two V blocks. Then turn it on. Set a piece of glass at brewsters angle to the beam. While watching the reflected beam rotate your laser until the reflected beam is the dimmist. This will tell you which way your laser is polarized. Then mark this orientation on the top of the laser for future reference.

Ed Wesly Mon Jun 16, 2008 1:13 pm #16
Thesrot wrote:

> Still wondering what exactly would happen if I put a 650nm beam through a 514nm polarizing beam splitter?

You would not get the same range of ratios of transmitted to reflected output beams; with a BS cube tuned into the laser wavelength you are using, you can get almost complete extincton of one of the beams. With a mismatch, you never get to that point.

The trick is that the coating that is put on the interior hypotenuse of the cube tries to make 45 degrees the Brewster's angle for that wavelength. If you tilt the BS cube so that the incident light is not along the normal you might be able to get a greater range of ratios.
