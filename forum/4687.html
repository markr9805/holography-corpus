Kaveh Sun Apr 03, 2005 7:50 am #5
There are two main causes of blurring of a holographic image. This applies to transmission as well as reflection holograms.

Dispersion

If you are using a broadband source to replay, the blurring will be roughly proportional to the bandwidth of light diffracted by the hologram. So as Colin says, a narrowband reflection hologram will be sharper, but generally less bright, as less light is being diffracted.

Source size

The bigger the angle your reconstruction source subtends at the hologram, the greater the blurring. So you can move the reconstruction source further away, but again, the image will be less bright.

In both case the blurring is roughly proportional to the distance of your image point from the hologram plane.

Multiple wavelengths

I think that the blurring will be the same for both wavelengths, and moreover any distortions will be the same. In other words, the two images will always be in register even if you move your reconstruction source. So there will be no additional blurring due to the second wavelength.

To answer your question of any tricks to increase the image depth, there are tricks to move the plane of apparent sharpest focus behind or in front of the actual hologram plane, by not using exactly conjugate recording and reconstruction beams, but no way I think of increasing the total depth of field, to use a photographic term. So in other words, yes, you will have to live with limited depth.

MichaelH Mon Apr 04, 2005 10:18 am #9
> JohnFP: Is there nothing that can be done to the hologram itself to get a greater depth of focus for a reflection???

I'm not sure what you mean by doing something to the "hologram itself" but you can increase the depth of white light reflections by stepping out of single-beam geometry and using methods almost identical to that used for rainbow transmissions.

I started doing this in response to a tip from Lon Moore and have since found [this paper](http://homepage2.nifty.com/kubotaholo/emy_holo07.htm) by Kubota. (btw, I have the pdf of the full paper if anyone wants it)

The tip from Lon was to stop down the vertical dimension of the master to increase brightness but I also found that I got sharper and deeper images as a result. I've never seen anything as deep as Kubota claims (19") but then I'm not using identical geometry.

One limitation (at least with the optics I'm using) is that I can't make my slit any shorter than the subject material or I end up with a "peeking through the blinds" effect.

Kaveh Mon Apr 04, 2005 3:03 pm #11
I forgot about the Kubota paper too. Of course you are losing something, namely vertical parallax. It's the reflection equivlent of rainbow holograms.

Oh, here's another thing John. You could reduce or even eliminate the color dispersion by using a diffraction grating. I have written this in my thesis, and Kubota has written a paper on it. He has a great site and you can probably find the paper there.

Most people think of dispersion compensation only in relation to transmission holograms, but it works just as well for reflection. You shine the light though a diffraction grating, and let the diffracted beam hit the hologram. I remember trying this with a broadband DCG hologram, and got a very sharp image.

It gets a bit cumbersome though, with a grating hanging in the air. Your 'compensating grating' can even be a reflection HOE. I don't have time to draw it out right now, but I think I have put the different configurations in my thesis, and i know you have a copy.
