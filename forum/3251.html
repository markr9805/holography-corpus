It depends on what you mean by "wasted" and also what your optic is trying to do. Is it to alter a beam profile? Is it to divert light, in particular the light that's spilled over from a Denisyuk reference? Is it simply a diffraction grating?

Let's tackle the question of "waste". When your 8" beam hits an 4x5 plate , a naive figure of inefficencyof the beam is 58%. In general, if the beam diameter is d and the plate area is A, then we can give an "inefficency" rating of,

eta = ((pi(d/2)^2)) - A)/(pi(d/2)^2).

However, since the beam is gaussian, we need to moduilate this, so a more ralistic figure would be,

eta' = (exp(-alpha(r^2))((pi(r/2)^2)) - A)/(pi(r/2)^2).

where alpha is from beam characteristics and r is measured from the centre outwards. So, in order to use this loss due to this inefficency, we can redirect the beam by means of some optical system. However, the proportion of the beam being redirected is dependant on the efficency of the redirecting optical system. If the redirecting is holographic, this would depend on the efficency of the holographic system, bearing in mind that aberrations may play a role in this efficency figure. If the redirecting is done by a non-holographic optical system, then the efficency is dependant on the losses in transmission and reflerction within the system due to polarisation losses, Fresnel losses and possibly lens aberrations. Considering that the amount of light being redirected is at the edges of the gaussian beam profile, any increase in eficency due to the redirecting may be offset by the inefficency of the redirecting optics. If this redirected light is to illuminate an object in a single beam Denisyuk, then the efficency of the redirecting system would have to measured as a function of the increase in the object illumination as a ratio of the direct illumination vs the complexity and inefficency of the redirecting optics. There's also a couple of other factors: the fact that, as Gary mentions, the eye is a logarithmic system and the intuitive engineering adage that the more complex a system, the more it is to breakdown. The latter is a sort of Murphy's Law and probably difficult to model exactly.

Is it a redistributor of energy? In this case, all you're doing is to redirect light from the centre of the beam to the outer edges. What's gained at the edges is lost in the centre, assuming no aberration losses or lossses due to local inhomogenities. You've still gt an 8" diameter reference beam which still spills over your 4x5 plate. If you now use additional optics to redirect that spillover from your now-non-gaussian beam, the same analysis can be done, except that the modulating exponential has itself to be modiulated. Of course, if you altered the profile to a top hat, then the naive eta and not the altered eta' would have to be used. Of course, the system is now still more complex because now you have an energy re-distribution optical system as well as a beam redirecting optical system. The losses would proibably multiply.

If the optic is just a diffraction grating, then it's either a thin grating or a thick one. If thin, you'll get losses due to diffractive orders (unless you have still more redirecting optics to redirect the redirected orders!). If thick, you'd be pretty hampered by Bragg selectivity which would cut down on the flexibility of your system.

The conclusion would seem to be that any increase in efficency due to re-directing the beam may be offset by several factors such as increased complexity and loss in the redirecting optics and, since the eye is a logarithmic system, the lack of any signifcant effect on the "brightness" of the hologram, especially if you have a wide dynamic range.

Insofar as the beam flattening is concerned, apodization in Fourier Optics means to get rid of the side lobes in the diffraction pattern of a circular slit. Basically, the diffraction pattern is typically a sinc function, sin(x)/x (actually, it's the square of the sinc). In this case, some 95% of the energy rests in the central lobe with 4% odd distributed in ever-decreasing side lobes. Recently, Apodization has meant the removal of FT components in general. I've always thought that the photographic technique you allude to is an "Unsharp Mask", where you expose a negative through another negative, or some such technique. Not being a photographer, I'm on unsteady ground in terms of the photographer's use of "apodization".

In any case, the use of an amplitude filter is also inefficient. Typically, the central part of the amplitude modulation will have to be about OD 0.5 or so, in order to flatten the beam uniformly. This would imply a loss of some 70% from the center.

But DCG has an exponential response and silver halide is a linear response. Thus in order to convert the (exponential) functional response of DCG into a (linear) response function of silver halide (as, for example by using a mask made with a photograph of any kind of greyscale), you'd have to have:

f(alphaexp(x) + betaexp(y)) = alphaf(exp(x)) + betag(exp(y))

While simultaneously:

f(ax + by) = af(exp(x)) + bf(exp(y))

Since the exponential function is an infinite series, it's to hard to imagine such a function. This is made the more difficult since the Gaussian is a square exponential function:

g(x) = exp(-((x-a)^2)(b^2))
