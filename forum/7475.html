jeff-blyth Sun Aug 10, 2008 2:17 pm #7
Methylene Blue is a chloride salt of an organic base just as guanidine carbonate is a carbonate salt of a quite strong organic base.

If you let methylene blue dry out in with say sodium carbonate then you do tend to get a violet colouration instead of that MB cyan colour.

This is because in sodium carbonate you have quite high alkalinity due to a significant amount of NaOH present since carbonic acid is as you know pretty weak,

so in effect then this NaOH is going to take the chloride ion away from MB and leave you with sodium chloride and the free base MB or MB(OH) and this stuff is violet.

So with quanidine carbonate this must be happening too I would think. It is interesting problem because I think the MB(OH) may not have quite the right wavelength absorption (I will check this when I am back in my lab in a few days time.) So I originally did experiments arount pH 9 or let us say between 8 and 9.5 but I did find that with Na Carbonate solution with a pH around 11 you did get this other form of MB.

So while we dont want to get below 8 because of forming apparently the insoluble MB dichromate salt we also may not want to go to above 10 so that it starts forming MB(OH). I am not actually certain that MB(OH) is bad news , I always considered that the buffering action of gelatin was sufficiently great that you could not easily take its solution well above 10 without adding lots of alkali and then by doing that you destroyed its special structure so that it would not gel at room temperature or behave normally ever again. So I have not taken much notice of MB(OH) in gelatin in the past.
