Hi,

Recently I've had a run of bad holograms (sliced bread, dim ones) and it's kind of aggravating since I thought I was careful about stability. I had changed a bunch of things at once, which is probably the problem. I'm using TEA soaked BB-640 plates, trying to make a transmission hologram on a new jig I made. Maybe the jig is the problem, I've not had any good holograms on it, but the thing is made of wood and is glued together. Maybe the temperature in the room is different, and that has bad effects on the laser. Perhaps the batch of processing chemicals has a problem, although everything looks OK when developing. Perhaps I should try making a simple reflection hologram with an old jig and see what happens. The big interferometer resting on the table shows stable fringes, and when I set up an interferometer on the table, it seems fine too. I'm surely missing something that's causing trouble. Any suggestions as to what the problem might be? :p
Tony Fri Jan 21, 2005 10:54 pm #2
Been there, done that too. After my first 4 holograms came out OK, I had a run of bad ones, and as in your case, I changed a lot of things at once. Especially when first starting out, you should try to change only one variable at a time. In my case, I backtracked everything one change at a time, and 5 holograms later I found the cause to be a new laser was just awful. (It turned out to be a 'counterfeit' Infinter).

You've got the right idea - go back to what you know works, and change things incrementally. At first I thought wood was a bad idea due to expansion and humidity effects, but actually it's not a problem. All my filmholders are made out of old 5x7 wooden sheet film holders.

Sliced bread usually means mode hopping...

Also, not sure of your climate, but winter weather, with low humidity results in lots of diode-laser-killing static...

Dinesh Sat Jan 22, 2005 4:10 pm #5
You might also test all the mirrors by putting them on one leg of the interferometer. You can test transmissive elements by putting them into the beam along a leg of the interferometer. The fringes will shift (due to phase change going through the transmissive element) but should stabilise. Thus you can put a piece of glass into the plate holder and put it into the beam along an arm of the michaelson.

The temperature should not affect the setup,. However, a change of temperature will. Components start expanding or contracting. Wood has a pretty low coeff of expansion, but the glue may not.

The simplest test of holographic parameters is to make a two beam grating. Even simpler is a one beam grating. You can do this by placing a mirror adjacent to the plate so plate and mirror form an "L". Hit the beam to cover both and the mirror reflects onto the plate. This has a minimum number of components and very low stability requirements so you can isolate equipment faults. As you eliminate components, get more and more sophisticated - two beam gratings with equal path seperation, two beam gratings with longer path seperations, etc.
