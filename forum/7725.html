Another chuckle-headed mistake: As I was pouring my fresh for this semester silver nitrate bath back into its bottle, a few drops spilled into the tray of salt solution as I was cleaning up after the Talbotype Salted Paper Demonstration. As to be expected, silver chloride precipitated.

I decanted most of the liquid, and let the rest evaporate, leaving the white AgCl crystals behind, which turned dark (printed out) in a few days even under office lighting. I figured I could use a rehalogenating bleach, subbing KCl or maybe even NaCl for the KBr to get them back to the white powder. But as I mentioned above, I would much rather have AgNO3 for further demos.

What would be a method to turn AgCl into AgNO3? Would the application of Nitric Acid be the solution? Or something a little more involved?

In a similar vein, I remember reading how to recycle silver from dichromate reversal bleaches (i.e. the “Pyrochrome” bleach used in some JD kits) simply by dumping table salt into it. But then you have AgCl, which is cool unto itself, but how useful?
jeff-blyth Thu Feb 19, 2009 10:16 am #2
You need to get all your spoilt silver/silver chloride mess into a beaker and pour some strong (undiluted) developer on it and leave it several hours to all turn into silver metal, with or without the aid of light, Ed. (Perhaps vitamin C at pH ~12 will be a good developer because you know what's there and it will not contain unknowns). Oxygen will turn the developer brown- black so I suggest you cut a disk the same diameter as the beaker from a polythene bag and float it ontop of the liquid as a skin to keep oxygen out.Then decant off the liquid carefully and give several deionized water rinses. Now you need to get some dilute nitric acid from your educational institute. "Finely divided"silver is readily attacked by dilute nitric acid and does not need the nasty concentrated stuff. Also the nasty toxic brown gas nitrogen peroxide etc formed as a by product is much less of a problem with dilute acid but even so , it is best to do this step outside or in a fume hood. One awkward point is how dilute an acid can you get away with? You are going to have your silver nitrate solution of a certain concentration which you need for your experiments mixed up with excess nitric acid which you certainly do not need . You may need to evaporate or distil

off quite a bit of the water and acid to leave a concentrated solution. Now you need to neutralize the residue with say sodium carbonate with the help of a pH meter. Powdered Na2CO3 could be dropped into the stirred solution until it stops fizzing. The pH I guess needs to be brought up to between 6 and 7 but not higher. So now you will have a solution of silver nitrate of unknown concentration and sodium nitrate. The sodium nitrate I suggest you can live with , it should not interfere with what ever you are going to use your silver solution for. Now to find your concentration of silver nitrate you need to do the classic classroom titration by taking a measured sample and titrating it with a burette full of say 1.0 Molar NaCl using say a drop of potassium dichromate as indicator etc etc.
