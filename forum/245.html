Dinesh Wed Jun 08, 2011 1:17 pm #2
I think your best bet is one of Integraf's beginner kits ( [http://www.holokits.com/holography_kit_2.htm](http://www.holokits.com/holography_kit_2.htm) ). You can get these for less than 200 USD. You also need a strong, stable base, something solid placed on a solid base. A marble or stone block about 3 or 4 feet square will work, but check that the block does not shift or rock when you tap it. You'll need to make holders for the plates and the laser. The holder for the plates must allow the plate (glass slides - about 2.5in x 2.5in or 6.25cm x 6.25cm) to be rigid and stiff. When placed in the plate holder, tap the glass slide gently and check there is no motion or rattlling of the plate. The plate holder must be placed on the base (stone or marble block) also in a solid way - no motion when tapped. All of this also goes for the holder of the laser. You'll also need some kind of mounting for the object you're making a hologram of. The geometry must be such that the laser beams, when expanded, shines through the glass slide, illuminates your object and reflects from the object to the slide. The most important thing in holography is that everything must be rock solid and stable - the setup cannot move to within the width of a human hair during exposure. If you can get one, I strongly recommend hot glue and a hot glue gun. If you set all this up, tap everything and make sure it's all solid. The booklet that comes with the kit will then explain how to record (shoot) the hologram and process it. The kit comes complete with all the processing chemicals and trays you'll need. As you progress through holography, you'll realise that you need quite a lot of ingenuity to make the equipment necessary, such as holders, shutters etc. Most of this stuff is fairly expensive and is meant for professionals. Besides E-Bay, there's nothing for the hobbyists, you have to make it all up yourself. Don't expect great results in the beginning.

As for a book, besides the booklet in the kit, I'd recommend "The Holography Handbook", by Fred Unterseher and others ( [http://www.amazon.com/Holography-Handbo ... 310&sr=1-3](http://www.amazon.com/Holography-Handbook-Making-Holograms-Easy/dp/0894960164/ref=sr_1_3?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1307559310&sr=1-3) ). It's the best guide with easy-to-follow diagrams and explanations, in fact a lot of holographers got started with this book.

Don't hesitate to ask questions here.

Jem Fri Jun 10, 2011 3:21 am #6
Hi

It's unlikely that your 200mW pointer will be stable enough for making holograms. If you're going to buy a kit it's probably best to use the laser in there as it will have been proven to have stable operation.

One of the biggest causes of failed holograms for beginners is stability. I know, i've been there , I would advise you to find a good solid location, preferably on a solid concrete or stone floor, get yourself a car inner tube and partially inflate it, place this on the floor and then place a paving slab on top of it. Use this for setting up your equipment and make sure you stick everything down with hot glue or similar. Even the very slightest vibrations can destroy holograms, they either come out with weak images or not at all. It would also be a good idea to find a large cardboard box to act as a cover for the whole thing during exposures as air currents can also affect quality. Make sure there is no noise in the room, remember to switch off air conditioning units, fridges and radios etc. If you live in a busy area with lots of traffic, wait until the traffic has calmed down late at night. Also, don't forget settling time... Initially i'd let everything settle for around one hour, then switch the laser on and give it another 20/30 minutes (for the laser to stabilise) before opening your shutter (very gently if manual) and making your exposure.

I have spent a very long time trying to get my setup stable and i'm finally achieving decent holograms. I also wasted loads of film during this 'learning curve', hopefully you'll avoid some of the problems by getting your setup correct at the start. I've seen some lovely single beam holograms made from these kits, but you'll have to put the groundwork in first otherwise you'll most likely end up disappointed.

Cheers

Jem

Jem Fri Jun 10, 2011 4:09 pm #8
I understand what you're saying about the books seeming complex at first. However, don't dismiss 'Practical Holography' as it really is an excellent publication. One of the problems when you start out is understanding the terminology used in holography. I was exactly the same as you, but I can tell you that understanding the terminology comes fairly quickly. Google is your friend as is the holowiki.

Once you grasp the terminology things start to fall into place and you'll re-read something and understand it a little better. It takes a while as does anything that you learn and holography is no different. Just don't be in too much of a hurry to learn everything at once.

Best thing is to pretty much ignore all the theory for the time being and just concentrate on making a hologram. Once you see the image recorded in the plate you'll be hooked and be eager to do more. With each hologram you make you'll learn from your mistakes, and at appropriate moments you'll dip into the books to see what went wrong, these are the times when the books will start to make a bit more sense.

Above all, have fun and stick at it

Cheers

Jem
Dinesh Sat Jun 11, 2011 8:19 am #9
> Jem: Best thing is to pretty much ignore all the theory for the time being and just concentrate on making a hologram.

I agree with this. Holography is a process with certain steps. If you simply concentrate on the process and not worry about why or how it all works, you'll make better and better holograms. The Handbook focuses much more on the process, with very little theory or rationale. It simply says, "Do this, then do that". This process will give you OK type holograms. If you want to make really good holograms and continue to understand holography, ie the reasons behind the process, then you need to get into the reasons and theory of it - the science of it. If you want to have a deep understanding of it, you'll need to get into the Physics and Maths of it. A good halfway point between the rigorous science and simply the process is Saxby's book. But for now, it's like cooking an egg. You don't need to be a Master Chef or a biochemist to cook an egg. But, most of us cook OK eggs. However, if you want to cook the perfect egg and understand why the egg cooks, then you may need some of this knowledge.

erose Tue Jul 05, 2011 4:20 pm #11
When I learned holography many moons ago my teacher, Dan S., walked me thru all the math to make sure I had a good foundation. Then he said "OK, now you can forget all that and we will start making holograms." Knowing the physics, math and the geometry will make it possible to do more interesting things as you progress but the real nitty gritty is all about stability, exposure times and developing methods. The other great piece of advice that Dan always emphasized was to follow good scientific practice and only vary one thing at a time.
