Just to clear the air a little. I did say that no hologram is ever completely secure. This is well known and Jeff does bring up this point. Whether it's practical or not is another question. Certainly, the more security features you put on, the more difficult it gets and soon it's not worth the forgers time and money to bother trying to replicate. However, technically, anything can be forged. Certainly it's possible to take an impression off an embossed hologram with Du Pont. Again, is it practical? Danny's point is that it can be done, and Danny claims to have done it. Jeff's point is that it's not practical to do it for the forger because of additional security features and because the dark background of the polymer itself gives it away as an obvious forgery. That too is true. Can the imprint from the Du Pont polymer be taken through to such a stage that it looks just like a foil hologram? That's probably an open question. I would suspect that the very high polarisation of the monomer would not react well in a bath with hundreds of volts and tens of amps. Gawd alone knows what the yield would be, because I don't think Faraday's Law works for a complex monomer.

By the way, the Jeff Blyth story, the way heard it from Edwina Orr in the early 80's is that he won a free trip to New York. Apparently, so the story goes, when Visa first used the dove hologram as a security feature, one claim by Visa was that it was impossible to copy or forge. Of course, we now know that this claim was made on the basis of the fact that there were precious few holographers with the technical skills back in 82 that knew how to do it. Anyway, when Jeff heard the claim that it was impossible to copy, he promptly called Visa and told them that he could copy it (there's a side to the story that Jeff actually copied it and sent them the forged dove). Apparently Visa got into a blind panic, paid for Jeff to stay free, all expenses paid, for a week in a swanky New York hotel if he'd explain to them how it could be copied.

Now only Jeff knows whether this story is actually true, so unless he comes up and confirms or denies this we don't know if this actually happened. It does, however, highlight the fact that anything can be forged and copied. The difference is whether it's worth it to the forger. > Johnfp: And one thing I did, and I am sure most tried was to reproduce a security hologram.

> Johnfp: I found with Dupont's film its way to easy to to duplicate embossed holograms

Actually, Jeff's point is that it is easy to reconstruct an embossed hologram by contact copying. This has been done many times by many people in the past 25 years. I have not heard of anyone explicitly copying an embossed hologram with Du Pont, but there's no reason to believe it cannot be done with Du Pont, or dcg, or silver halide or, heck with Lithium Niobate! If Du Pont is somehow easier to make the contact copy because of noise, I would submit that the amount of noise in the copy is in direct proportion to the skill of the holographer. Actually I made a fairly passable copy of an embossed hologram on silver. With contact copying, you need no large steel table or even a laser. So long as the source's coherence length is less that the width of the foil and the emulsion (some 20 microns?), it will contact copy. You can actually contact copy an embossed hologram on your kitchen table with an led.

However, the point of contention here is: can it be copied relatively accurately, then can it be put in a Nickel tank and a shim made of it, then can it be mass produced also relatively accurately on foil as in a printing process of some kind (the way it's done now), then can it be applied to a real world product in such a way that both the retailer and the consumer are fooled into believing they're buying a genuine product? Take for example the Paramount hologram (because, for example, I mde that one and I love taking examples from my own work - sorry, a slight bit of grand standing and aura-fluffing!). Anyway, this particular hologram was seen on every VHS from Universal Studios movies and TV shows. Now, is it possible to buy a Paramount VHS, copy the hologram, whether with Du Pont or not, create oh about 100,000 stickers (or hot stamped foil), make a home movie of your own pet tricks, apply the forged stickers on the medium and sell it to the general public so as to make them believe that Paramount produced the medium?

Jeff's contention is that the above process is extremely difficult, if not impossible, because, quite apart from the actual copying of the hologram, there are several security features in the Paramount hologram (which I'm not at liberty to detail - unless offered a decent bottle of Merlot! For a whole crate of decent Merlot, I might be persuaded to tell you what's on the Mastercard - Joy made that one! ). In addition, even if you copied the hologram on Du Pont, can you put it in a nickel tank and electroform it? I think not, because I don't believe that any polymer can be simply electroformed in this manner. As I stated earlier, the polarisation of the complex molecule does not follow Faraday's Law, I don't think.One other issue is that the Du Pont polymer produces volume holograms, so even if it could be electroformed, it ain't going to produce a shim! Besides all this, applying your finished, electroformed and embossed forgery to the final product ain't going to be easy.

So, yes, any embossed hologram can be copied. Heck anything can be copied! There are parts of New York where I can guarantee you that I can buy a Rolex for 5 bucks that will be indistinguishable from the real thing. But, copying the hologram is but a very (very!) small part of forging a hologram.

This goes way beyond the capacity of most counterfitters budget not to metion the skill set needed (one has to be a pretty accomplished holographer to do this).

Depends on the profit motive. If the product has a high market value, large customer base and forgeries can be (or are) all that validates the product, someone somewhere will copy/forge the hologram. While working in this industry, I noticed high profile clients' holograms had been forged. Sorry, can't say which ones for obvious reasons (but see comment regarding the Merlot above...). In fact, that's why IHMA exists. One other point: You don't have to make a "good" copy, if you're going to forge. There's a story that someone removed the dove on a visa card and replaced it with a Mickey Mouse face. No one noticed. The dove is there not for the protection of the retailer or the consumer (so long as the retailer scans the card and gets an OK from the electronic scanning device, he's "clean") , it's there so that, if Visa suspects that the product is a fake, it's possible that IHMA can trace the forger.
