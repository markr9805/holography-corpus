Colin Kaminski Mon Oct 01, 2007 9:09 pm #2
I will address the question of psuedocolor as addressing all of your questions would take a book like Graham Saxby's.

You can expose, pre-swell, expose, pre-swell and expose to add red, green and blue to a hologram. I find I have to expose in that order (not blue first) and I have to use about 1/2 of my exposure time for each of the three colors. (This has to be adjusted for each hologram). You can change the objects between each exposure and end up with three different colored objects in the same hologram.

john kaufman Tue Oct 02, 2007 3:45 pm #4
Yes, Justin, you will end up with a very heavily exposed plate with multiple exposures. In the olden days, 10-25 years ago, I used nearly full exposure time for each exposure (this was with Agfa plates). Development density was the same as for a fully exposed single exposure. Beam ratios were very close.

I used a different exposure scheme from Colin. I started with blue and worked to red. The pre-sensitizing from the trieth. was more consistent if I worked that way. If you do red first, the plate should at least start in a water bath for a little bit of presensitization.

You will need to plan your final image with great care so that you can control hot spots. Control of the shape and position of the reference beam on the H1 and H2 are also important to reducing information to be recorded, and in determining color playback position in the final image.

I would suggest being able to make a killer bright and clean one color before moving on to multiple exposures.

john kaufman Thu Oct 04, 2007 10:04 pm #8
Justin, usually one does vary the reference angle of the H2 so that the colors play back together. Jeff Blyth, when he first described pre-swelling color control in the late 70's, kept the angle the same and the images did not play back together. Whatever works for your final image. See if you can find the papers that Steve McGrew, Lon Moore, and I presented in 1982 at the Lake Forest/SPIE symposium. They cover most of the basics.

Note that Lon got his colors to play back together by selectively lighting only parts of his H1 (his H1 was large enough so that he could light the top, middle, and bottom of the plate with a wide and narrow reference beam). His two or three exposures played back with three views of the object overlapping in one spot. This is a good technique when registration is not so important.

I usually controled color play back by varying the H2 reference. Remember that even when a single H1 is used, and the colors play back together, they will be in register only at the film plane. You have to plan the final image to minimize the visual effect of colors that play back together but lose their registration.

I also did a lot of work with multi-color shadowgrams. They could be very bright with the right diffuser and the color mixing effects were very satisfying. I chose to vary the reference angles but one could use Lon's techinque and light parts of the diffuser so that it would play back in the same position without moving your reference beam.
