BobH Sun May 22, 2011 9:48 am #5
I always point the meter's detector directly toward the source, as they are intended to be used. That provides consistency. Maybe the ratios calculated from that technique are a bit high compared to the other method, but who cares? The ratio will probably be optimized for every new image anyway. Plus, the fringe visibility depends on the ratio of amplitudes of the two beams. The only way to measure the amplitude accurately is to use the detector as intended.

The exposure power in each beam is another matter. It the reference beam is spread out because of its angle, there will be less energy density on the plate. But the exposure energy for optimum results has a big range, and it's on a logarithmic scale. The difference between what is read as total power at the recording plane for both methods is small compared to the range of powers that will give similar results. Also, shadowing of the detector happens so that would have to be taken into account for accuracy. But again, that level of accuracy isn't necessary for great results.

More important for good results with an "H2" exposure is a very small detector size, to accurately measure the energy density of the "burn" marks at the recording plane. This allows the beam ratio of those little areas to be optimized so they are less of a distraction.

Dinesh Mon May 23, 2011 3:11 pm #9
> Ed Wesly: what part of the curve are you measuring? And where do you plug in the reference angle measurement into the Gaussian equation?

Well, there are two possible ways.

1. Measure the beams center and at the extremes of the beam. That is, if you're measuring on a plate, measure the centre, the middle of the top and bottom and the middle of the right and left. If the extreme measurements are all equal and (very roughly) about 1/3rd of the beam intensity at the centre, then you know you're pretty well centred. Due to the uncertainties of the measurements, there's usually an error of about 10% (so a reading of 5mW may mean that the actual beam can be anything from 4.5 to 5.5 mW) and so the equality if the readings is subject to these measurement uncertainties. Another thing to watch out for is the angle of the plate to the ref. Assume that you're shooting with a collimated beam at 60 degrees. Then, there is a path difference between one edge of the plate and the other of d:

d = psin(60)

p=plate width

which means that the further edge will have a relative intensity reduction proportional to 1/(d^2). Now this fall-off may be fairly small and so you may not need to worry unduly, but there will be an inequality between the near edge and the far edge, which needs to be taken into account when you're actually measuring, in other words, the extremal reading will not agree with each other completely. The corrections in this case are for the differences in paths between the two edges of the plate and inherent measurement errors. You may be able to offset these by altering the angle of illumination of the object beam and allowing the light reflected off the object to be skewed toward the far edge to keep the ratio constant.

2. The intensity of the (Gaussian) beam is:

I = I_0exp(-2r^2/w^2)

where r = point along the radius of the beam

w = beam waist

The beam waist itself is given by:

w = (lambdaz)/(piw_0)

w_0 = beam waist at the thinnest point of the beam.

z = length along the beam

Thus, if you know z (the distance between the laser and the plate) and w_0 (usually given in the laser spec) you know the intensity at r, the points at either end (or any point from the beam centre) of the plate. Knowing these, and taking into consideration the inverse square law falloff for a non-normal incidence, you can measure the beam centre, I_0, and determine what the beam intensity should be at any point on the plate. If you now measure at various points on the plate, it should be possible to write a simple program that will take the measurement point as input and give out the expected intensity at that point. Probably you can do this in Excel. You then compare the actual measurement to the theoretical value (as per Gaussian equation), or not do any other measurements - simply measure the centre and plug into Gaussian equation for the value at any other position.

Yes. John asked: " do some kind of cosine theta correction or what?". Now why would he think you needed a cosine correction. Would it be because Lambertian reflection is cosine-dependent? Let's say that one illuminates a diffuse object by a line of light (coherent or not, it doesn't matter). Then, the reflected beam profile would be Lambertian and would give the reflectivity at any angle relative to the incoming line of light as proportional to the cosine of the angle between the measurement direction and the line of light:

R = R_0cos(theta)

However, this would only be true for illumination of a diffuse surface and certainly would be true of the object illumination (assuming the object was not metallic, in which case the reflection would not be Lambertian). However, it would not be true of the reference beam, because the beam profile of the reference beam is not a Lambertian profile, it's a Gaussian profile.

Now a correction implies a factor by which the measurement is altered in order to take into account some phenomenon that does not bear directly to the actual measuring equipment. Thus, if you measured a beam "at the plate" by a measuring equipment that had a significant dimension, then you're not actually measuring "at the plate", but a few centimetres in front of the plate. If you wanted a greater accuracy, you'd then apply a correction factor to take into account the fact that the equipment is not actually measuring the point at which you want the measurement.

In the case John mentions, you have two options, actually four options: two for the ref and two for the object:

1 and 2. You actually measure at various points over the entire plate for, individually, the object and the ref. You now have an actual set of measurements and you need no correction, or at least no significant ones apart from the small physical displacement of the actual head

3. You measure just one point on the plate. For the object, you assume a Lambertian beam profile and so at any other point on the plate, you calculate the angle between the required measurement point and the actual measured point. You now multiply the actual measured value by the cos of the angle and so the value at the required point is obtained. So, let's say you have an object 8" in front of a 4x5 plate arranged in landscape. You measure the object beam right at the centre of the plate and get a value of 5mW. The angle subtended by the centre of the object across half the plate is 17 degrees (arctan2.5/8), so the object light hitting the extreme edge of the plate, assuming Lambertian, is 5cos(17) = 4.77mW. If you assume Lambertian, you only need one measurement - the centre of the plate - and you can derive all other object illuminations by calculating the cos of the angle and multiplying, as above.

4. You again measure just one point on the plate. For the reference, the beam profile across the plate is now a Gaussian profile given by

I=I_0exp(r^2/w^2)

So, you have the intensity at any other point of the plate by inserting the value of r and w. The value of w is given by the laser specs. So, again, you only need one measurement and you can calculate all the other values of irradiance by the Gaussian function.

I think there are actually three questions here:

1. How do you measure

2. What does the measurement mean

3. What's left out of the measurement.

In the case of 1, it's pretty obvious: you point the meter and it returns a number.

However 2 is a bit more subtle. If you get a number, say 5, does this number represent the entire plate or just the point at which you measured. More subtle is the fact: does it matter? The exposure/sensitivity curve is in general non-linear. So, a measurement of 5 in the centre may fall off to say, 4.5 at the edges. But, does this 10% difference cause a 10% change in the exposure characteristics? Is the image at the edges 10% dimmer? Since the exposure is non-linear, I suspect that the 10% loss will not cause more than a 2 or 3% loss - especially where the "image" is an actual image (a display hologram) to be seen by the eye. So, do you need to measure all over the plate, or just at the centre? Some holographers I've worked with simply measure the centre and determine the exposure from that. Some holographers (My teacher Peter Miller, for one) measures at several positions over the plate. One holographer I've worked with whose work is very well known both in and out of the community, has an Excel file with Lambertian and Gaussian profile data and uses it to measure at a few locations and then plugs it into the excel file; this then determines his exposure. This, I think, bears on the original question of a function correction

I think 3 is more subtle still. No mention has been made yet of polarisation. No detector, per se, measures polarisation. But, polarisation is the most important factor in the fringe modulation. So, should one now put a polariser in the detector head and rotate it?

> Arturo: There were at least two mentions to "finger wiggling"? Is this an inside joke, or really there is something to it? I do not get it! Thanks.

No. No joke. This was a technique taught to me and I now teach it to my students.

The technique is based on the fact that an object beam is usually a diverging beam spreading outwards from a diffuse, Lambertian object. As I mentioned, the reflectivity of a rough (Lambertian) surface is proportional to the cos of the angle between the direction of illumination and the observer, assuming a direction can be associated with the illuminant. This is not always strictly true, since most objects are partially Lambertian and partially specular. Let's say that the object is a model of a human face looking at the plate, ie the nose points at the plate. Let's also say that an object light of 5mW is coming in at 30 degrees to the nose. In this case, the light off the nose is I_0cos30 ~ 0.875.0 ~ 4.3 mW. There are some simplifications because the light is a gaussian, coherent beam. As the nose is pointed to some degree, there will be a specular component also. Also, every point on the face will be a source of a point scatterer. Thus the object light from such is a broad, divergent source. Most detectors have a ccd designed to accept light directly onto the ccd, ie normal to ccd array and also have a limited aperture (which is why the actual reading on the meter will have mW on the scale - the aperture is assumed by the design). In the case of an object beam, the light hitting the plate is not normal to the face of the ccd, but diverging. The light hits the ccd at a range of angles across the face of the ccd. Also, the actual light hitting the detector will vary quite a lot as you pass it along the plate and point it at various directions. Thus to get an actual and accurate measurement of the object beam, you need to point the meter at a given direction to the face, take a vector from the ccd array center to a point on the face (the normal vector to the ccd array), and then integrate over the range of angles between the the two edges of the ccd and the end of the ccd normal vector. Now you need to move the ccd array by it's diameter and repeat the process. Once you've got the result of all this computation, you have to perform a convolution integral with the reference beam to get a point-to-point ratio and then average the convolution to the reference to get an average of the beam ratio. You can see this will be a long and tedious process and unduly pedantic to all but a few of us.

However, since the hologram is non-linear by virtue of bleaching, which converts it to a phase hologram, small variations in the ratio will not matter (except to the most pedantic of us). So a better, if cruder, method, is to look at the shadow of the object beam under illumination of the reference beam.So, you place a white card or a plate glass painted a matte white in the plate holder, block the reference beam and the look at the white card. It will now only be illuminated by the object beam. If the object beam is as bright as the reference, then the illumination by object alone will be as strong as the illumination by ref alone. If the object is too weak, there will be almost no illumination by the object beam. If the object is roughly 4:1 or 3:1, then the illumination by the object beam will be slightly lower than illumination by ref alone. Since the human eye finds it difficult to compare two illuminations when one has disappeared, and the human eye is very good at detecting edges and motion, a better way to compare these illuminations is to wiggle your fingers in the reference beam (being careful not to obtrude on the object beam). The shadow in the area where your fingers shadow the reference beam, ie where the shadow of your fingers are caused by the object light in the background of the reference, is a good indication of the beam ratio. If the shadow is jet black, there is too much refrence. If the shadow is fairly bright, then the object is too high. If the shadow is a dull grey then the ratio is about right.

The exact nature of the shadowing is a matter of experience and varies from emulsion to emulsion. I evaluate the shadowing differently when I shoot dcg as when I shoot silver as when I shoot resist.

Let's first ignore losses due to polarisation, scattering etc. A simpler, first-order calculation will give the principle, I hope.

The intensity of a beam is determined along the normal to the propagation of the wavefront. This is the k vector, or Poynting vector (given, incidentally, by the cross product of the E and B vectors: Sk = ExB ). If the beam is collimated, the wavefront is an infinite plane and the k vector is a vector normal to the plane. Ordinarily, the intensity across the plane is uniform and constant to infinity. However, a laser beam has a Gaussian profile (it'd be too long-winded to justify this, so I hope you take my word for it!) So, the intensity across the plane falls off as a Gaussian function from the centre of the plane. How can an infinite plane have a centre? Well, the plane was not always infinite, when it was a small baby plane, it was almost a point source from the laser and this is considered the centre. If the distance from the center is x, then the intensity at a distance x is:

I(x)= I_0exp((-2x^2)/w^2)

As I mentioned before.

Now, if the plate (your hypothetical magical gelatin) were perpendicular to the beam, ie the k vector of the beam and the plate normal, call it p_n (bold indicates a vector) were along the same direction but reversed, then the illumination across the plate would follow the Gaussian equation. So, assuming the beam were centered at the centre of the plate, then some point at, say, 2 inches from the centre, the intensity would be:

I(2) = I_0 exp((-24)/w^2)

The I_0 is the beam intensity at the centre of the plate and w, the beam waist does change across the plate, but it can be derived from the W_0 parameter. You can get w_0 from the laser specs and derive w from it from equations found in any standard textbooks on laser physics. Now you simply vary the exposure according to the Gaussian fall off, assuming the plate sensitivity itself does not vary. If you want to take polarisation into account, at normal incidence the reflectivity for all polarisations is given as a function of the refractive index, usually taken to be 4% for glass of index 1.5

Moving on to the skewed plate direction, you now have p_n at 60 degrees to k; I'm assuming here that Brewster's angle is 60 for simplicity. Thus the angle between the plane of the plate and the beam is 30. In this situation, the beam hitting the far edge has traveled further than the beam hitting the near edge. In fact, the beam hitting any point on the plate will have traveled either further or nearer than light hitting the centre. This increase (or decrease) in distance causes a fall-off due to the inverse square law. Light hitting any point on the plate will diminish or increase relative to another point if it has traveled further or nearer relative to that point. Now, you have to first detrmine a fixed point to which you're going to relate all other points o the plate. Then, at any point on the plate, you need to compute the intensity as a Guassian function relative to the centre of the plate. Now, choosing a fixed point, you need to modulate the Gaussian function by an inverse square function to take account of the further, or nearer, distance relative to the fixed point.

Assume that the fixed point is the centre of the plate and further assume that the plate is d units long. The centre of the plate is illuminated at I_0. Any point x from the centre of the plate is now illuminated as per the Gaussian at

I(x) = I_0exp((2x^2)/w^2).

For this same point, relative to the plate centre, the increase, or decrease, of the light path is

s(x) = x/cos(30)

So, the illumination at x will be:

I(x) = {I_0exp((2x^2)/w^2)}/( x/cos(30)}

where the sign of x will depend on whether it's to the left or right of the plate centre.

At this point, you plug in the sensitivity of your plate and expose accordingly.

The amount of power lost because of reflection off the first surface of the recording material is insignificant. Less than 4% at most. The error in a power measurement taken with some angle at the detector other than normal incidence will be larger than the simple cosine decrease caused by the smaller round sampling (by the detector) of the elongated beam spot at some recording plane because of detector housing shading and reflection losses off the surface of the detector. I'd recommend anyone measuring beams with tilted detectors should calibrate them for this error, by plotting the measurement of a beam versus angle of incidence of the detector.

When measuring the power of a laser beam, always use the detector at normal incidence. That's the law. Has nothing to do with exposure. Same law when setting beam ratio. Fringe visibility also has nothing to do with beam angles at a recording plane. The fringes exist in space without any recording material present.

For exposure calculation, however, the total power IN the recording is what counts and so I understand the desire to measure a constant area (say 1cm^2) by using a detector parallel to the recording plane.
