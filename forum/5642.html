> JohnFP: It may be even possible to elliminate the veil, bar or spin altogether and just place the glass on the heated plate and pour a puddle in the center, of know quantity, and let gravity do the rest.

That would be an interesting experiment to try. A possible problem in using a heated metal plate would be that, after the coating is made (and leveled) it will probably then gell slowly, since the metal plate will remain warm for some time. This would mean that the ungelled layer will be vulnerable to dust for a longer period. If you have complete confidence in your dust exclusion system (laminar flow station, etc.) then this won't be a problem.

I recently finished a water heated/cooled coating plate, but I haven't tested it yet. I took two aluminum cookie-trays and siliconed them together, so that water can flow through the assembly. It has an inlet and oulet for water. I'm planning to place the glass on the upturned bottom of the upper cookie tray (which fortunately has a flat bottom), as well as the Mayer bar. Then let hot water run through to heat the glass and bar. Once the coating is made and levels out, I can quickly change to cold water to gell the coating.

I've been making some plain gelatin coatings on glass, too, and I think it's an excellent way to practice your coating technique.

It has a 1/2 inch hole on the bottom of one end, and another 1/2 inch hole at the top of the other end. To these holes I siliconed some small fixtures made from copper tubing and plate (soldered) so that I could attach the plastic tubing to something solid. Water was introduced into the bottom hole, then it continued filling up until the water level reached the top hole, then it started going through the "exit" tubing and down the sink.

It worked for about 15 seconds, but I was surprised to see the aluminum surface starting to bow outward (indicating pressure build-up) then one of the silicone seams burst.

This is one of those things where it seems like a simple concept, but the application is another story. Having the top surface bow outward is a definite no-no since this needs to remain flat, to transfer heat/cold to the glass plate. Maybe nothing short of thick metal plates soldered, brazed, or welded together will suffice.

Joe Farina Wed Jul 05, 2006 10:25 pm #20
Thanks for the information, John.

I completed the new coating-table today, but I want to let the silicone cure a while longer before I test it. Per Colin's suggestions, the top is 0.5 inch aluminum plate, and the outlet is much larger (1.5 inches in diameter).

First, I took an aluminum cookie-tray, and then sized the aluminum plate to have about a 0.5 inch overhang on all sides. Then I bought the aluminum plate (14 X 19 inches) for about $80. For the water inlet, I assembled 3 parts, a short length of copper tube, a copper elbow, and a flat piece of copper plate. I soldered the copper tube to the elbow (using the standard plumbing "pipe sweating" method). The thin copper plate was cut to a size of 2 X 2 inches, and a 0.5 inch hole was drilled through the center. Then, the tube/elbow combination (the 3/8 inch type) was soldered to the copper plate. The purpose of doing this was to provide a firm connection point for the plastic tubing (where the water is coming from). So I took this assembly and siliconed it to the underside of the cookie-tray (which had a 0.5 hole drilled in it, of course). The reason for the copper plate was to provide plenty of surface area for the silicone to bond the assembly to the cookie-tray.

Exactly the same method was used for the outlet, except that a 1.5 inch copper elbow was soldered to a 4 X 4 inch copper plate (with a 1.5 inch hole cut in the middle (using a scroll saw). No extra copper tubing was used in this case, because the 1.5 plastic hose (to the drain) fits snugly over it already.

Then I had to get a 1.5 diameter hole into the aluminum plate, which took some extra time, since that diameter was too "tight" for my handheld jigsaw to follow. So I drilled through it four times with a 0.5 inch drill, then "carved" out the interior by nibbling it with my jigsaw. Then, to clean it up, I used a small drum-sander in the drill press. Of course, it's essential that the "drain opening" be at the very top of the highest water-level point (coming straight up from the top aluminum plate) because otherwise, the water won't fill the full interior volume. I guess that's obvious, but I thought I should mention it.

Three holes were drilled at the very edges of the underside of the aluminum plate, for the legs. These holes were tapped with 1/4-20 threads. These holes didn't go all the way though, but almost. I didn't have the proper kind of bottoming-tap, just the standard kind, but it worked well enough. Then I cut three pieces of 1/4-20 threaded rod, using a cut-off abrasive saw I bought from Harbor Freight. (Incidentally, I think everyone should have one of these cheap 14-inch saws. They're excellent for cutting metal rod and pipe.) Then I attached three 1/4-20 "connectors" to the threaded rods to serve as "feet." These are then rotated to level the assembly.

The cookie-tray was attached to the aluminum plate by running a bead of silicone along the upper edge ("aquarium sealant" was used as the silicone in all cases), and pressing it against the aluminum plate. A weight was put on top as it cured.

To get hot or cold water in, I simply made a "T" fitting in the hose for the spray-handle of my faucet, attached a piece of braided plastic tubing to the "T" and put a valve on the other end of the hose. I attached a plastic hose barb to the other end of the valve, then a piece of soft plastic tubing to the hose barb. The other end of the soft plastic tubing fits over the copper inlet tube.

I'll have to wait a bit longer to see if the whole thing works.
