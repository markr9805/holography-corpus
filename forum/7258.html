JohnFP Fri Apr 04, 2008 1:38 pm #2
> Julian: From what I can remember, you put the spatial filter near to the laser so that both beams are filtered it that right?

What do you mean both beams? The Spatial filter acts as a lens but a cleaning lens. You should have it after the beam splitter and have one for each beam if you are doing a split beam hologram. It would be more difficult to have it before the beam splitter and then try to stear the expanding beams. This would require large mirrors which can then introduce noise by the way of dust on those mirrors. The Spatial filter opimally should be the last optic.
ErichRose Fri Apr 04, 2008 1:40 pm #3
I've used the older Newport ones and I own a Melles Griot. Each took a little getting used to. The first couple times you try to adjust them it can be totally maddening. Especially after I watched Dan Schweitzer tweak it up in about two seconds! I think I spent 45 minutes with one once but later could do it in 5. Point being you get to know whatever you have. I also built one myself but had sketchy results. The adjustment threads have to be very fine and the movements have to be smooth and very linear. That's why the ones with micrometer adjustments are so expensive. But with that said they also have to have good pinholes and objectives. There is a thread that explains pinhole size to objective power based on beam diameter. They should be matched. I mostly used a 10X objective with a ??? diameter pinhole. I bought my pinholes but I know folks who have made their own. If I had to do a really long throw to the plate I used the 5X. I have a 15X and 20X but rarely used them.

You can run the laser through the SF and then split it but you just pick up more crap off the beam splitter and other optics downstream. The best use of a single SF is the reference beam. That is the one beam that needs to hit the plate as clean as it can be. Depending on what you are recording the object beam does not have to be totally pretty. Keep your mirrors and lenses clean and dust free and you probably can aim them so that any large rings or spots miss the object or hit it where they don't show. You can also use diffused light. If you have a good table those 25mws will go a long way.

When you start doing H1 to H2 transfers you will want a second SF. Also when doing shadow grams. But even then if you use it on the reference and carefully light the master with regular lenses you can get away with one only.

As others here have suggested look for used ones. But be careful that they have not been damaged. If the threads are not smooth and they wobble when adjusted it could be impossible (or at least take hours) to adjust.

BobH Fri Apr 04, 2008 2:13 pm #5
I highly recommend the Newport model #910. It's very compact, the adjustment handles don't stick out into beam paths, it has an iris at the input, you can take the objective or the pinhole out without loosing alignment, it has tilt adjustments, and the cost is reasonable. You'll also likely find them on ebay, but you need to watch for them closely, as they are also used as fiber optic couplers.

Avoid like the plague the older Newport model #900. They suck! Terrible. Garbage. Idiotic design. And they cost the most for some outrageous reason!

The old Jodon spatial filters are a big improvement over the old Newport units, but don't have all the nice convenient features of the Newport 910. A usable alternative. Of the others out there (like Creative Stars), they are all basically knockoffs from the Newport 910 but bigger (which is bad when trying to fit them into complex systems).

Objectives I'd buy are 20x, 40x, then 10x, in that order. Go for a 20 micron pinhole and you'll be OK with all the objectives.
