JohnFP Tue Jan 29, 2008 2:42 pm #22
Here are my beliefs on Envionmental conditions. While not complete, I believe it does have some good points. I sent this via e-mail to someone and place it here so it can be open for discussion or debate.

Environmentally, I believe Temp and Humidity are the biggest. Light would also play a role if it was excessive but only in hardening. The temp and humidity in combination dictates how much water and swell is in the emulsion prior to exposure. Too much and the emulsion is in a swollen state when exposed. Of course this can lead to a green shift or even a collapse of the fringes (greenies). Or too much water in the exposed area. I have to think of developing a DCG as actually developing the unexposed areas, not the exposed area. With the correct exposure the exposed areas are optimally hardened and there is little swelling in the water bath and minimal drying in the alcohols. But it is the unexposed areas that need to be swollen with water to maximum they can without breaking the very fragile bonds, then have the water replaced with alcohol while maintaining their rigidity and finally remove the alcohol and have air spaces where t he expansion voids are. The strands do shrink somewhat in this process but the key is to swell, replace water with alcohol, then remove alcohol pockets being replace by air with controlled shrinkage.

Now how about atmospheric pressure? Is it possible that making a DCG hologram on top of a mountain would act completely different from making one in Death Valley with all other atmospheric conditions the same? But I never studied or took that into account. I have always looked at Temp and Relative Humidity.

As far as the differences of developing 10 plates consecutively, it's not really hypothetical but is intertwined as usual. I would have to say the following. And I hope it answers your question. Once the plate is coated with emulsion the emulsion goes through a very rapid change in moisture content. Then this change slows and slows. So I think between 1 and 4 hours there is a great difference, but after about 4 to 6 hours (depending on temp and relative humidity) the emulsion moisture content has equalized with the ambient conditions and is pretty stable. This probably holds true "indefinitely" negating all other influences on the emulsions. But there are other influences on the emulsion. For non sensitized emulsion there is the attack of mold and contaminants in the air (what do scientists use as a medium for growing gunk in a Petri dish?). So it is a good idea to refrigerate the plain emulsion after 4 to 6 hours if it is going to be kept for days. For sensitized plates, the AmDi does not fair well with mold etc so refrigeration is not needed as early but the AmDi sensitized plates do go through dark reaction. Now this gets us back to your question of the 10 plates. Even though the emulsion moisture content has equalized after 4 to 6 hours and remains stable the crosslinking (hardening) of the emulsion continues to commence. Thus as time goes on the emulsion does get harder. Now what is good is refrigeration slows this process down also. So if you are to use your plates from 6 to 24 hours I would say you are going to see consistent results from 6 to 12 hours with minimal change and from 12 to 24 hours with less change but from 6 to 24 hours a noticeable change.

This brings up a good point that the process Jeff and I developed (mostly Jeff Blyth, I just did testing) for G307 may be a great way of keeping the emulsion very consistent over time. Coat some plates with plain gelatin and water. Let the plates dry for 4 to 6 hours then refrigerate. Then as you need them, sensitize them in a solution of AmDi and water and shoot. This would allow you to have a gelatin moisture content that is consistent and a dark reaction that is consistent. What is also great about this technique is you can put in a session and make a large batch of plates and keep them until needed. If the glass was fresh and the gelatin and everything else was fresh and not contaminated by any, I mean any chemicals, you could do this in your kitchen in full light and they would be safe to store in your normal fridge until needed. Hummm, now you got me thinking.

JohnFP Wed Apr 29, 2009 11:26 am #34
Tony, usually the best, or brightest holograms are highly broadband. The easiest way to achieve this is to use young film and hot baths. Setting these two variables as I mentioned, you will have a great tendency to get milky holograms. Now, expose longer (do this until you get slight burn in where the object is close to the plate) or post expose such that your hologram has a very slight milkiness where the exposure is least and you will have a great, bright, broadband hologram with no milkiness where the image is.
