Ed Wesly Tue Nov 29, 2011 12:42 pm #2
Looks to me like a classic case of rigid body motion during exposure. Check your master holder and see if it is loose, or the master inside of it, since it seems like the fringes are on the master.

One would think that motion of the master during exposure would totally ruin it, however when dealing with transmissive objects, like the master, they have to move a substantial (like many wavelengths) to totally ruin the exposure. Also coming into play is the k-vector, or the sensitivity angle. Since you are coming straight in to the master, the sensitivity is low.

It could also be other things moving in your system, but it seems that since the fringes are in space, where the H1 is, it is the offending object.

If it were a polarization issue due to the birefringence of the material, the fringes would be random. I am assuming you are using the tri-acetate film stock, T3, as opposed to the polyester, P3, which has that problem. Looking through a sheet of film through crossed polarizers would show colors due to birefringence. Shooting through H1 and H2 film scenarios would yield random dim spots.

Batten down the hatches and try shooting again!

I must say, I was a bit perplexed with your result. However Joy (wife) offered the following explanation:

"The Airy disc is not a disc, it's a sphere. On this sphere are the rings caused by diffraction. So, you have a sphere with lines on (I think she means a football circled with lines). This too is possible, but the lens would have to image perfectly in order to get the perfect Airy sphere. This leads me to believe that the light coming into the lens is not perfectly on-axis. In this case, you'd get coma and astigmatism. This is made worse "cutting off" the parts of the Airy disc. The fact that the image seems to be the negative may be just coincidence.

Well, there you have three explanations. Probably, none of them are true!

Regarding the difference between "knife edge diffraction" and the Schlieren effect, "knife edge" diffraction refers to the diffraction from a narrow edge, such as a knife edge. In this case, near the edge, there are a series of lines with varying spacing. You see this often in apertures such as irises and when you use a card to block stray light. The light hits the edge of the iris or card and produces banding. This kind of diffraction is called Fresnel diffraction. The Schlieren effect takes advantage of the fact that an invisible substance - such as a gas - may have different indices of refraction at, say, different temperatures. In this case, passing light through such a system causes the light to "slow down" when passing through an area of higher index. This causes a phase mismatch and the phase mismatch is converted to a grey scale. This shows up, for example, the flow pattern of a gas in a heated environment.

Dinesh Fri Jan 06, 2012 7:37 pm #40
I did have a thought that I could throw out and see what the reaction is:

Firstly, if the film is extruded, then we can assume that the optic axis is in the plane of the film and parallel to one of the sides (maybe not quite if the film is stretched bi-axially, but to a first approximation let's assume that the optic axis is parallel to one of the sides). In this case, when recording with an overhead beam at p polarisation (necessary to avoid woodgrain), the polarisation vector is at an angle to the optic axis. To be precise, the polarisation vector is at (pi/2 - Brewster's angle) to the optic axis, since Brewsters angle is measured to the film normal and the optic axis is parallel to the film plane. Now, this will give rise to an e wave and an o wave. Now, if Brewsters angle is theta_b, and the two indices are n_o and n_e, the o wave light hits the opposite face at

theta_1 =arcsin(sin(theta_b)/n_o)

and the e wave hits the opposite face at

theta_2 = arcsin(sin(theta_b)/n_e)

If the reference beam is collimated, the two beams will create a set of parallel fringes with a spatial frequency

f = (sin(theta_1) - sin(theta_2))/lambda

But now, here's the really interesting idea. When recorded, the emulsion is towards both beams. Since the recording occursbefore the beams hit the plastic, the "birefringence" fringes, f, are not recorded. However, when the film is reconstructed, the film is reversed. The H1 reconstruction (the H2 object) now hits the plastic of the H1 film first. This means that there are now two reconstruction beams, and two reconstructed object waves. But the object waves are so close (especially if |n_e - n_o| is small) that there will be Moire fringes on the H2.

What do you think?

Dinesh Sat Jan 07, 2012 10:04 pm #43
> Sergio: I confirm the moiré fringes but on recorded reflection holograms, but no modification appear if you see orthoscopic or pseudoscopic images

I would expect this. The H1is recorded directly into the emulsion, ie the object and reference beams hit the emulsion first, then the base, so the recording on the H1 is one set of fringes. The H1 should show no banding. But on reconstruction, the H1 is reversed. This time, the light reconstructing the H1 goes through the birefringent material before it hits the emulsion (where the reconstruction takes place). However, when the reconstruction light hits the birefringent material, it's split in two with a small angular displacement due to the birefringence. This will produce two reconstruction beams with a small angular deviation. Both reconstruction beams will record an H2 hologram, but because of the small angular deviation there will be a moire seen - an "interferogram" of the two object reconstructions. It should make no difference if you view the H2 in pseudoscopic or orthoscopic (except maybe the moire fringes will have a phase difference of pi/2).

> Sergio: Some holograms come out without moiré fringes, polymer holograms were replying at same original WL so structurally it can perform as a solid layer recording extreme high spatial frequencies.

The swelling of wet-processed emulsion leads to a bandwidth. It may be that the two moire images fall under the same bandwidth in a wet processed hologram. Polymer recordings are narrow band due to dry processing.The narrower bandwidth may "select out" one of the two images and so cause no moire.

> Paulos: Your film is strong birefringent, and the orientation of its optic axis varies quasi randomly.Without a a lambda/4 plate, I am afraid that almost all your holograms will show these lines.To overcome this problem, you should use a circular-polarized beam to illuminate H1

It occurs to me that using a circular polarisation reconstruction beam may simply generate two linearly polarised beams which will vary in brightness sinusoidally with the same frequency as the rotation frequency.

Consider a beam that's polarised at some random angle with respect to the optic axis. This will generate an ordinary ray polarised perpendicular to the plane of incidence and travelling as per Snell, along with an extraordinary ray polarised parallel to the plane of incidence and travelling perpendicularly to the optic axis. The relative strengths of the two beams will depend on the polarisation of the input beam wrt the optic axis and the plane of incidence. If you now rotate the polarisation of the input beam, you vary the relative strengths of the two output beams. If you rotate the polarisation state very rapidly (circular polarisation), you vary the relative strengths of the two beams very rapidly. So, you get still two reconstruction beams, except twice: once when the polarisation is parallel to the axis and once when it's perpendicular the axis.

I may have made an error in my analysis, so I think it's still worth trying, though.
