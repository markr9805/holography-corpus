This is aimed mainly at Ron Michael but suggestions from anyone are welcome.

I want to find a relatively cheap and reliable way to inspect q-switch pulses generated by a small ruby laser.

Ideally this would include:

- pulse peak measurement

- pulse duration

- ability to approximate area under graph for energy measurement

Doing this with my cheap 25mhz scope (!) is going to be impossible but I'd be happy to at least register the pulses on DSO and so begin work on a more dedicated system for measuring the pulse data.

This brings me to sensors/photodiodes. Q-switch pulses are in the ballpark of 50ns and peak power is very high so photodiodes will be destroyed on direct exposure. Artifical beam divergence and/or weakened reflection might make this practical but I don't know if its a good route to take. There might be much better and less messy ways.

The other thing is photodiodes themselves - I have three 1cm^2 PIN photodiodes which I think were intended for a UV photolithography application but work well in the visible. However - I think PIN diodes are good for low energy readings but unsuitable for q-switch pulses because of fast saturation and long response time? Basically I am not sure if they are any use for this app. Otherwise I have to go and find some more suitable diodes.

I'm looking for advice and suggestions on a good way to perform the measurements physically, and then perhaps electronics for limiting the need for a very high frequency scope (peak hold, energy integrator etc.). Basically the simpler and cheaper the method, the better!

Thanks,

Doug.

ron olson Thu Nov 09, 2006 5:59 pm #3
Fast photo diodes (risetime<1ns) are not expensive but without a 400MHz scope, you won't be able to resolve details of the pulsewidth/pulseshape. You don't put the laser output directly into the detector without a suitable neutral density filter - I typically look at a diffuse reflection (off foam core or aluminum). All you'll see with your slow (25MHz) scope is the integrated pulse and for that you'll need a 1M ohm input (as opposed to the 50 ohm input you'd use to look at the realtime pulsewidth). The primary use for such a diagnostic is to check for free-running in the mS time regime: the leading edge will be clean (vertical) if properly Q-switched; ragged if free-running is present (lasing outside the Q-switched envelope). As a secondary use, the integrated pulsewidth allows you to examine pulse to pulse energy stability assuming you stay in the linear regime of the diode (avoiding saturation). You'd be well advised to borrow or rent a fast, storage oscilloscope for a week to characterize your pulse during set-up.

I agree with Ron that you need a bit faster scope, and I would add that looking at the reflection off an optic would be much nicer on the diode than putting it in the direct beam.

I will get some faster photodiodes though. Thanks for the tip.

The main thing I've been looking at is acquiring data directly using a bunch of op-amps and reconstructing an approximate pulse profile from the data. It doesn't require super-expensive digital equipment but it does still require very fast components and careful building.

I came up with the following plan:

- Use a very fast (800mhz!) op-amp to amplify/invert the charge 'drop' on a small capacitor, using a reverse-biased photodiode to discharge the cap (assume the cap is initially charged to a known voltage). The amount of charge lost through the photodiode should (with the help of J = 0.5CV^2) be proportional to the amount of energy falling on the diode, and therefore represent the integrated pulse. Or at least I hope thats the case. If I got that bit wrong, the rest is going to be off too.

(..and I can use a peak-hold circuit to provide a reliable output for measuring the integrated pulse reading.)

- Calculate the pulse magnitude by amplifying the instantaneous diode response and using peak-hold to record it.

- Calculate the pulse duration, by integrating a constant over the time spent above some threshold. I think this can be done by integrating using a comparator instead of an op-amp, or an equivalent hack.

- Calculate the quality of the pulse, by integrating the in the frequency domain (picking up ragged edges, noise or multiple pulses). This probably means integrating using another op-amp, with an ac-coupled signal using a well chosen RC combination.

With these 4 things I can make a fair estimation of what the pulse must be like, without actually seeing it and without having to record 400mhz samples. And assuming the pulse is good, it should provide sensible energy readings.

A good pulse would have short duration, high peak, low noise and the energy measurement should represent a good proportion of the peak/duration rectangle. Multiple pulses would show up as a frequency component. Lasing outside the q-switch would show up with high peak, long duration.

I do admit it will be hard to build a working one without a reference to check it against. It will be reassuring if the integrated pulse as picked up with my 25mhz scope correlates with the output of my circuit.
