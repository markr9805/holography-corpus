Ed Wesly Thu May 19, 2005 10:46 am #5
442 is such a wonderful color! If you have red and green already you can get a decent white by combining them all. But the best is red on top of blue for a magenta beam!

I don't know if you will like it if I share my experiences with these types of lasers. Omnichrome is no longer in business; both it and Liconix, the other American manufacturer of He-Cd's, were bought by Melles Griot, who still supports the tubes. I saw the Omnichrome style on the M-G site, but not the Liconix. The only other manufacturer is Kimmon in Japan.

Re-tubing this one will probably be about $3k. It will need it eventually. The lasing is done by the cadmium, which is vaporized in a little cooker. You will see the Cd Heater light cycle on and off during operation.

Eventually the Cd gets all cooked out, ending up on the inside of the tube and worst of all on the windows. Beam quality deteriorates during the lifetime with the jazz accumulating on the windows.

Some of the Omnichrome models have a button, which turns off the cooling fan (it looks like yours has the fan in the head; the 74 model I am familiar with had a remote fan, so you might be able to rig something up with the fan and dryer hose!) for a moment, so that the tube gets really hot and some of the accumulated Cd melts and gets back into circulation. This will extend the life of the tube.

As the tube gets older, the Cd heater light will cycle on more often, and power will drop. Eventually the light will stay on, and there will be no more output.

We used a 74 series in a Dot Matrix machine (DOTZ! registered trademark) at CFC, 24/7. Warranted lifetime was 1600 hours; the tube was dead by 3000. Re-tubing was an annual affair, at about $6k. That laser was replaced by a Power Technology 405 nm diode.

Tweaking the 74 and maybe yours requires a special wrench. It had a socket at one end, and a screwdriver handle at the other, a nut driver might be a term for it. But they cut the tool from the hardware store in half, made 45 degree cuts in both pieces, and welded it together in a right angle. This way you could reach the nuts on the mirror plates through the access holes, and have a decent amount of torque on it. Hopefully they will ship you one with the laser.

The e-bay description says that it is a single line laser. They also misspelled Omnichrome, so I am not sure if this means that this is an extended coherence model, or that it is a model that emits only in the visible, as there are variations of these devices that could emit 442 and 325 simultaneously.

The only He-Cd that I know about that ever had an etalon cavity was the Spectra-Physics 185. Same kind of chassis as a 125 He-Ne, but different tube and cooling vents on the cover. (I've got one in the Laser Museum in my garage.) He-Cd's have notoriously short coherence lengths, like a few inches. To overcome this limitation, Liconix and maybe even Omnichrome started using in the early '90's (your laser's vintage) more expensive isotopes of Cd to narrow the linewidth. This might double or triple the cost of re-tubing.

Have fun!

BobH Thu May 19, 2005 2:08 pm #8
For a very short time I worked at LiConix as a plasma tube technician, responsible for putting the gas and Cd into the tubes. Besides the Cd coating on the windows causing the power to drop, there could also be Cd stallagtites (sp?) growing into the beam path off the walls of the tube. These would cause the light to be scattered out of the path and reduce the power output. Overheating the tube might clear these up as well.

I'd recommend cleaning the optics and thorough re-alignment before trying to clear the tube of deposited Cd, though. He-Cd lasers are very expensive to operate because you have to consider the tube as an expendable. Output power drops continuously over its lifetime, and the drop-off curve depends greatly upon how evenly the initial Cd was deposited on the inside surfaces of the tube.
