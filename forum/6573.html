> Joe Farina: Here's my two cents:Mix dichromate, gelatin, and water using some kind of double-boiler method, and keep it under 60C. Coat plates using whatever method you prefer. Let them dry under a gentle air flow for 4 hours. Store your plates in the refrigerator (use lock & lock or some other kind of airtight container). Make as many plates as possible to enable lots of tests. Do not desiccate or do anything else to them. (By the way, use Knox gelatin from the grocery store.)Now, do tests at your lesiure. Just take your container out of the fridge, let it get up to room temperature, and take a plate out (or you can immediately take it out if you hit the surface of the gelatin hard with warm air from a hairdryer to prevent excess condensation). Expose Denisyuk style with 100mW at 532. (I would say 2.5" X 2.5" plates would be good.) Let them set in the dark for 5 minutes after exposure. Rinse under cold tap water for a minute until they yellowness goes away. Soak in room-temperature water for a minute. Soak in 91% for a minute with agitation (room-temperature). Soak in 99% for three minutes with agitation (room-temperature). Then dry with hot air.If there is milkiness, you will need to harden the gelatin more after the dark reaction. You can user fixer or a 100W light bulb 6 inches away (for varying time periods) to do this. I prefer the light bulb method.See how your plates age, and how they perform over time. Change variables to see different results (well, I don't need to tell you this, since you probably know better than I do about trial-and-error work in holography). Just use the same principles you use to get such good silver halide holograms. One last word: don't try to pre-plan things too much. Just use the simplest DCG technique possible (it is really very simple if you have blue or green light). Don't make it any more complex than it has to be. End of lecture

JohnFP Wed Dec 05, 2007 5:36 pm #11
> I bet I only use the center 25% of the light.

Bet you don't....

But that is an interesting statement.

Let's start with a beam diameter of 10" and average power of 100mW/cm^2

And let’s use the inner 5" of beam which should be 1/4 of the energy or 25mW/cm^2 BUT............

Due to the Gaussian profile the inner section has way more light then the outer section thus you may be using 50mW/cm^2 or more. Someone with calc can probable determine this exactly but anyway....

The only reason I bring this up is to emphasize to the beginners the benefit of expanding your beam past your plate and realizing you really don't lose as much light as you would think. 10” to 5” may be extreme with low power lasers but using 8” out of a 10” diameter beam… well… you get the point.

Joe Farina Wed Dec 05, 2007 9:11 pm #13
> Colin Kaminski: The only thing stopping me from dcg is coating.

Colin, my coating system was one of the few things that I've done in holography which was immediately successful, thanks in part to your suggestion of using drinking straws to make a laminar flow station. I know you requested a photo of mine earlier, which I neglected to do, but I can send you some photos and you can post them on the Wiki if you like. I didn't send them before, because photos will not really indicate how it was constructed, it will just show the exterior. I would have to take it apart first, and then take photos. They are already built into the lab, and I can't take good photos of the backside of the units either.

Actually, you can build a laminar flow station in about a day. I have two, one for coating, and the other for drying. They have met my expectations 100% after a number of coating sessions. All you need to do is build a box out of melamine-coated particle board, then put a "wall" of tightly-packed drinking straws at some location in the box. The only thing is, you need a very large quantity of straws. But these should be obtainable from a restaurant supply house, which is where I got mine. Each of my stations required about $60 worth of straws. On the backside of your wall of straws, just attach one or more hepa filters, taped to each other and to the interior of the walls, using packaging tape. Then behind that place your fan (I use a window-fan with two small fans in a single unit), which will pull in air from the rear of the unit, push it through the hepa filters, through the wall of straws, then out through the front of the box. I have two of these boxes placed side by side, on a rather low level, so that coating is done in a sitting position. That way, I can use the top of the boxes as my processing table!

You still have to use normal laminar-flow procedures when you use these, however. In other words, do your primary activity well within the box (not near the edge), and do not allow dusty contamination to get in when the unit is turned off. I carefully cover the front of the units with a plastic material when not in use. Also, I run the units for quite a while before doing any work inside. I did the candle test on these, and they gently bend the candle flame outward when it's held in front of the units, without any variation in intensity as far as I could tell.
