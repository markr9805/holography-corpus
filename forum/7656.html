Hi,

For the past ten years, I worked in “synthetic holography” (computer generated holograms). In research and development as well as in production of holograms for museums and other applications.

My researches and experimentations have led me to think that analog holography and synthetic holography present enough differences, in their techniques, approaches, effects and perception, to be considered two different mediums.

I would like to know if there’s many of you involved in CGH and how do you perceive the differences between analog holography and synthetic holography.

Thanks...
BobH Sun Dec 28, 2008 5:12 pm #2
Welcome here, Jacques. I understand the point you're making but disagree. Holography is a technique, not a medium in my opinion. the differences between synthetic and analog holography are only differences in how the object is created for the recording technique. There is no difference between a reflection hologram of a CG image of a cube and a reflection hologram of a sculpted cube except for the "look" of the final image.

When casting in metal, does it matter how the original was created? Is a casting of an object made by hand in wax a different "medium" than a casting made of an object machined in wax from a computer generated image?
Jacques Sun Dec 28, 2008 7:43 pm #3
Thanks BobH for your reply. Your contribution is right in the middle of my questionning. Nevertheless, I would like to bring a small variation on this definition. I would rather say that holography, analog or synthetic, is a process, that is using diffraction to produce a 3D image on an emulsion. This process can use different techniques (reflection, transmission, rainbow, emboss. etc.). The result may look similar but the limitations and possibilities can vary.

Your analogy with sculpture is right. Although, if you compare video and cinema, you will get a different analogy. These are considered two different mediums. The techniques are very similar, only the technology differ. The tools, the language, the final results are almost the same.

There's some computer generated holograms that I can't imagine how I would make this with analog holography. Animations, different content from different points of view, narrative content spread over space instead of time, etc. On the other hand, I think that 3D reproductions of objects, portraits, chromatic effects, etc. are still better in analog holograms. There may be subtle differences that would make us classify two mediums coming from the same process.

While many are contemplating a supposed death of holography, maybe we are seeing the transformation of a medium, cause by the advent of new techniques and new ways of creating.

Tom B. Wed Dec 31, 2008 11:34 am #5
Hi Jacques - I take the "textualist" point of view - that it is the total effect of the work of art that matters and how it was produced is less important - with the exception of obviously cruel methods such as slave labor, hologram contests etc. In principle, all analog effects can be achieved synthetically, and vice versa, but except to show off - why bother trying to be something you are not? Analog can achieve at low cost incredibly rich depth and textural detail on limited subjects, but synthetic holography is only held back by computational capacity and imagination. Ultimately synthetic must win, but I think there will always be a few non-textualist people aesthetically attracted by the stunning simplicity of classical holography.

Jacques Tue Jan 06, 2009 1:48 am #7
Hi,

Your arguments made me think a lot. The similarities between analog and synthetic holography make holography a wide, diverse and prolific field of work. Nevertheless, there are some differences between analog and synthetic holography that makes me wonder, which is why I decided to start this topic of discussion. Thanks for your insightful contributions, and thank you BobH for the reference to Paula Dawson's work. Very interesting.

Following BobH analogy to sculpture and Tom B.'s"textualist" approach, a hologram is a hologram. However, the image making process may have an impact on the final effects and perception. When I'm preparing an analog hologram from a real 3D scene, I am working in a space similar to the final result. On the other hand, when I'm preparing a 3D computer graphic scene for rendering and output as a hologram, I'm in perspective space. Sure it's virtual 3D space and there are already similarities and differences with real space, but that's not my point. What I mean is that in analog holography, I'm sculpting, assembling, gluing, cutting, etc. In 3D computer graphics, I'm setting up a multiple viewpoints perspective image. This opens a whole different mindset, relationship with working tools, space and process, and eventually a different kind of images.

In a way this is paradoxical. Holography is an optical approach, it is bringing imaging beyond the limits of the traditional single point of view perspectivist approach. But computer generated holography, a technology that seem to expand the possibilities of holography, is bringing back geometrical perspective as an holographic image making process, with many singular effects, possibilities and limits. Is it possible that there are two sides to holography, regardless of technology, one being the generation of the wavefront as in analog holography and "electro-holography". And the other, being the prolongation of perspective drawing in multiple viewpoints perspective images?

Did you experiment with synthetic holography?

I disagree with your interpretation BobH. In analog holography you are setting up a field of view with continuous parallax. The “infinite number of viewpoints” you refer to is natural perspective. On the other hand, in synthetic holography we have a limited set of viewpoints that we can manipulate individually, both in their content and parameters, with more or less distortions. This is artificial perspective. The “viewing zone” and “image lighting” you talk about are common to every imaging technique, 3D or 2D.

BobH Sun Jan 11, 2009 5:30 pm #10
Hi Jacques,

I understand your point, but I was really just pointing out that for analog holography, you listed physical action items (sculpting, assembling, gluing, cutting) and for synthetic holography you list only a goal.

My experience with synthetic holography is technical. I've designed and build equipment to record the holograms because that's what a holographer does. Doesn't much matter what kind of system it is, really, analog or synthetic, because an artist of some type usually supplies the subject matter. The craft is in setting the system up so that it records great holograms. The art is in the creative use of the equipment and/or techniques to result in a visual or statement that can only be made holographically. Whether the original subject matter is rendered by photography, sculpture or computer is beside the point for me, as a creative holographer.

I'm certain that artists see it differently. They probably see the recording system as simply a camera or in the case of synthetic holography, a printer. Nothing creative there! I understand that perspective, and have been happy to simply operate the camera or printer for them, but the result of that perspective has lbeen to confuse the art world about holography's place. Is it 3-D or 2-D? Is it a picture (it's in a frame) or a sculpture (it's in a frame on a tripod)?

> Perhaps we could now combine them, a la the animation/live action superposition pioneered in "Who Killed Roger Rabbit?" Has anyone already produced such images?

I made a hologram in '91 I think that combined them. It was a vertically animated stereogram of images taken in a wind tunnel of a helicopter blade in a point diffraction interferometer I worked on. The set of 15 images showed the onset of dynamic stall, as the fringe pattern separated from the airfoil. As you pitched the hologram back and forth, the image oscillated. I made a double exposed reflection hologram using that stereogram transmission H1 as the first exposure. the second exposure was a straight reflection recording of a theatrical type curtain a few inches behind the plate and plane of the stereogram image. Like the animation was "on stage". Never showed it to anyone or did anything more with it, but I still have it.

I'd love to do more with that kind of technique, but maintaining a stereogram printer along side an analog system, a transfer system to combine them, and coordinate the artwork is a very expensive deal. Then everyone wants full color too. Let's do it! Anyone have a very rich uncle?

To clear up a few points:

Bill, you may have bought the hologram from Loren Billings but I can assure you that she personally didn't make it. I don't know what the image is, but she has had the habit over the years of taking credit for all holograms. It may have been shot there, however their holographer, John Hoffmann, seems to no longer work there. Since they are so secretive, no one will now the true story.

When I ran the Holography Program at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago we did have the capability of making holographic stereograms. (If anyone would like a complete description of the system, I will send them pdf's of a couple of papers if I am contacted through this Forum.) It was a 16 mm film based system, against most holographic conventional wisdom, and it worked well. Every student in the class got to make a holographic self-portrait! And if you didn't mind the painstaking step of photographing a monitor with the Bolex, you could make a synthetic holo!

I left there in 1997, and my successor, Eduardo Kac, the Bloated Holo-Poet took the set up down for whatever reason. He recently pulled a fast one on John Bannon, who worked his way up the ranks to become an Assistant Adjunct Professor, and replaced him with a grad student with no previous experience in holography! So I have a feeling that the system will never be operational again. But back in my day we could make rainbow, achromat, and reflection copies from 30 by 40 cm masters.

A system that I had experience with afterwards was at CFC/Northern Banknote, designed by Craig Newswanger. It used a Texas Instrument DMD, and was capable of pretty sharp images up to about 6" by 8", after that it got pixelly. It is still up and running as far as I know since CFC got bought by ITW.

Other stereogram systems that are operational are the usual suspect, Rabbit Holes, Geola has their system in Lithuania, a similar system, or at least track camera and software are at OpTIC Tecnium in Wales. There's gotta be more, but I don't know off the top of my head.

As far of today's technology, I still see a difference between digital and synthetic holography. For me, a digital hologram would be a computer generated wavefront. A synthetic hologram is a grid of holographic cells containing the visual information for reconstructing the 3D image. I consider Multiplex holography to be the entry point of synthetic holography. Impressive holograms are made with these systems. I saw a few Liti holograms (they are of a very good quality) and I like a lot Dutch Holographic’s works. The approach that we had at XYZ Imaging was to blend computer graphics, software corrections, the direct-write method and the pulsed lasers from Geola. As far as I know, Zebra also produce direct-write holograms but with CW lasers.

Obviously, there was a lot of developments and additions to the system at XYZ Imaging, but I think that direct-write and pulsed lasers opened the door to a straightforward process (computer-imager-developer-display). No vibrations problems, a large format full colour hologram in a few hours. If you are interested in the manipulation of the holographic process, this may not be as fun as you would like, but someone like me who is mostly interested in experimenting visual effects and composition of true 3D images, this system is very efficient. Now RabbitHoles and Geola use this technology.

BobH, I would love to see your “combination” hologram. It’s not an easy task.

"Synthetic holography" has its basis in analog holography. For the synthetic to be developed, the analog has to be imaged (think about this--you can't realistically take a planar image and take it to film, no matter how esoteric the method may be). The analog may certainly be recorded as a digital pattern via the camera, USB and computer, but ultimately, the hologram started its life as an analog, real image.

This differs from CGI, and CGI in video creation and recording. If I am wrong here, please correct me.
