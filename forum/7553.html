walschuler Wed Oct 08, 2008 5:51 pm #6
I looked at that thread also, and it was illuminating (though marred by invective); especially pertinent were the last 2 posts, one by TomB and the other by Jean. I think the heart of the matter is that spatial coherence is not a great benefit for image formation, though it might be noted that back in the 60s Hanbury-Brown and Twiss used intensity interferometers, working off the limited spatial coherence that starlight acquires in its long path from the stars to to us to find the angular diameters (not images) of a number of stars the were too far or too small to be reached by Michelson's earlier interferometer used at Mt. Wilson. More recently Labeyrie used a somewhat related technique of Speckle Interferometry to get (crude) images of some stars.

The temporal coherence is the big issue. Supposing TomB's calculation is correct, that for 8 inches coherence length you need 0.001 nm bandwidth, and a 25 second exposure (this seems optimistic). To improve the coherence length to 25 feet, the bandpass would need to be about 30 times narrower, and thus the intensity passing through the system 30 times lower, and the exposure 30 times longer, or about 15 minutes. It is actually worse than that due to a film effect called Reciprocity Failure: in film, each grain requires 3-4 photons to be converted from unexposed to a latent image grain. If the photons arrive too far apart in time, excited electrons created by the earlier strikes fall back to lower energy and the exposure process is reset, so exposure times can be stretched considerably beyond a strictly proportionate increase. If the desired scene depth is 250 feet, say a landscape, the bandwidth must be 10 times narrower and the exposure time 10 times longer, about 150 minutes. Not a snapshot!

And then how do you split the very large beam of light involved???? This scheme can probably only work, if at all, in a darkened room, as mentioned in a post above.

As a matter of interest, it might be noted that (Leonard?) Wolf at U of Rochester or RIT back in the 60s or 70s carried out an interference experiment that amply demonstrates the weirdness of quantum effects. He set up a standard interferometer and placed a tv like detector or phototubes, I don't remember which, to record the fringes. Then he inserted a neutral density filter in the beam and adjusted the filter absorption until he was getting just one photon through the system at a time. He then left the system run for a long time. Eventually the single flashes accumulated and when he added up all the frames they totaled up to be the same interference pattern as with the normal beam. This result was in complete agreement with the QM equations. But classically, you might have to say that, since only one photon was in the system at a time, each photon interferes only with itself! On it's face, that seems to violate common sense. But that is the best we can say...

What I divine from the proposition is that the reference beam is laser derived, yet the object light is from the scene. It kind of begs the question can you get light from two different sources to interfere?

It is usually understood that two lasers can't interfere, since there may be a slight difference between their wavelengths. But with a broadband source, like sunlight, there must be some 633 nm components (using He-Ne as the laser) and will they interfere with the coherent light?

The best I can come up with as an experiment would be to use some red-filtered light in one leg of a Michelson Interferometer and a laser in the other. Would there be any fringes, as long as everything is not moving? The fringes might be low contrast, with all the unused wavelengths, but the coherent superposition does make the fringes brighter than the background.

Danny,

Do you know the bandpass of your notch filter? Compare it to TomB's calc or the equation (wv lngth)*2/delta (wv lngth) to get its coherence length, and let that guide you with respect to available path length difference.

Then I think you should try 2 experiments:

Get the brightest possible non-laser white or green light source and then

1) Use the throughput of the notch filter to make both reference beam and object beam, in a "normal setup, maybe a Denysiuk with the object flattish and in contact with the emulsion

and

2) Use the notch filter output as the object beam, and the laser as the reference beam, adjusting the laser beam brightness to be approximately equal to the laser brightness (probably means filtering the laser power way down)..
