In response, there seems to be a continuing concept that damping is good for isolation tables. I'll repost my longwinded explanation why I disagree:

There seems to be somewhat of a misconception as far as the techniques used to make a useful optical table, as far as vibration control is concerned. Some of this might be in the terminology, but some of it begs clarification.

The purpose of the optical table is to provide a stable platform to set up optical elements like mirrors, lasers, film, etc. to make holograms. The requirement is that the optical elements remain in stable positions relative to one another within a small fraction of a wavelength over the time period of the exposure.

The stability requirement is usually obtained by constructing the table to:

First, be isolated from sources of vibrations, and

Second, to be rigid so that any vibrations that reach the table will affect all the optical elements equally, thereby inducing no relative movement.

The usual practice is to:

1. Find a stable place to put the table, like on a concrete floor in a basement.

2. Use a stable pedestal to position the table at a convenient height - like a stack of concrete blocks.

3. Use a springy element such as inner tubes or bubble wrap.

4. Use a heavy, stiff table top, such as concrete.

The structure formed by this is a simple harmonic oscillator, right out of the freshman Physics text. The spring constant of the springy element works with the mass of the table top to form an oscillator with a natural resonant frequency. This is the frequency at which the table top will bounce up and down if disturbed.

The input energy to the oscillator is the undesired source of vibrations from the floor, coming from appliances, furnaces, people walking around, vehicles, etc.

By using a springy element with a low spring constant (low deflection force per unit length of deflection), and a large mass, the resonant frequency is made very low. Strategically, we want the resonant frequency much lower than the typical frequency of the energy from which we wish to isolate the table.

The amount of isolation of the table movement will be increased if we make the table resonant frequency much lower than the source of vibrations.

Each of the optical elements on the table, such as a lens on the top of a rod mounted in a stand, itself is also a harmonic oscillator. We wish to avoid allowing energy to reach these near their resonant frequency. This is why we try to make the optical elements as stiff and rigid as possible - to make them have a higher natural frequency.

If there were no losses in the system, and some undesired vibrations were introduced, the table would start oscillating (bouncing) and never stop. But in real life, there are losses, and the energy imparted to the table does get dissipated. The oscillations gradually die down. This is called damping. Damping can be due to air friction, or frictional losses in the system, especially the springy element.

It might be thought (as has been alluded on this forum) that more damping is a good thing.

If we intentionally added another element designed to provide additional damping, the oscillations would die down quicker. This would be in the form of something similar to an automotive shock absorber connected from the floor to the table (in parallel with the inner tubes). The down side is that this also provides a more direct path for vibrations from the floor to reach the table. As the stiffness of the shock absorber is increased, the vibrations damp down more quickly - but the initial displacement of the table in response to the undesired vibration is greater!! This may not have been what we had in mind. The frictional element (shock absorber) reduces the effective isolation between the floor and the table, at low frequencies - right where we wish to obtain isolation.

It’s my personal opinion that as long as the table and optical elements are rigid, some table motion is tolerable. So I believe that what works most effectively in this case is to have the most compliant, lowest spring constant spring element that can be found.

[quote:1ba2988f47="Tony"]The structure formed by this is a simple harmonic oscillator, right out of the freshman Physics text. It's not a simple harmonic oscillator. A simple harmonic oscillator is given by:

d2x/dt^2 = -(w^2)x

And the period is:

T=(2pi)sqr root(l/g)

Not there's only w. Also, in SHM, the mass is assumed to be a point particle. In more complicated harmoinic motion, the mass has extnded dimensions. Thus, the mass will also have a Moment of Inertis, I, and so:

T = (2pi)*sqr root(I/g)

Also, with an extended dimension(s), you'll have modes of vibration. It may, eg, swing from side to side, waves may travel through it, it may oscilate in one direction at on end and another at another end. A more realistic model is a series of coupled oscillators with energy transfering between the various modes. The w would have to be replaced with (S_i(w_i)) where I've used S to represent the capital sigma, and where the i's ae the various oscillation mode frequencies.

[quote:1ba2988f47="Tony"]The input energy to the oscillator is the undesired source of vibrations from the floor, coming from appliances, furnaces, people walking around, vehicles, etc. [/quote:1ba2988f47]

Actually, you'd also have the resonant frequency of the undisturbed system, w_0. Adding a disturbance converts the above to:

d2x/dt^2 + (S_i(w_ii)) = S_if(w, t)

where the f's are functional relationships of the various driving forces.

[quote:1ba2988f47="Tony"]By using a springy element with a low spring constant (low deflection force per unit length of deflection), and a large mass, the resonant frequency is made very low. Strategically, we want the resonant frequency much lower than the typical frequency of the energy from which we wish to isolate the table.[/quote:1ba2988f47]

[quote:1ba2988f47="Tony"]It might be thought (as has been alluded on this forum) that more damping is a good thing. [/quote:1ba2988f47]

It depends on the energy transfer between modes. The larger the table, the more the modal energies. The best thing, as your table increases, is to be very careful not to make it symmetric. The greater the symmetry of the system, the more the chances are that one or more modes will be driven and, worse, driven into resonance.
