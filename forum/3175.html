must have been a dozen or so), the Ultimate turned out to be the

better deal...

Roughly, photometry is the study of incoherent "everyday" light, the kind that comes from incandescent sources such lamps, and it's effects on vision. The light is characterised by it's "brightness" more than the set of wavelengths. It's measured in units such as candela, lumens etc. You often see these on bulbs. The "60W" or "100W" refers to the electrical power, not the optical power. Radiometry is the study of the passage and behaviour of light as radiation. In radiometry, light is seen as having a single, or narrow, set of frequencies and the effects of this is dependant only on the frequencies and their physical effects. Thus, the photoelectric effect, response of ccd chips, that sort of thing is radiometry. Radiometry is measured in W/sq cm.

"Efficency" in a radiometric sense makes no sense when used in a photometric circumstance. Hence, you can talk of the diffraction efficency of a grating by passing a beam through it and measuring the intensity of the first, or other, order(s). This is the radiometric diffraction efficency. However, you cannot talk of the "diffraction efficency" of a reflection hologram seen in white light since this is essentially dependant on the "brightness" of the source and the "brightness" of the image, as sen by the eye. It's essentially photometric in character. ( [http://www.optics.arizona.edu/Palmer/rpfaq/rpfaq.htm](http://www.optics.arizona.edu/Palmer/rpfaq/rpfaq.htm) )

This is largely speculative. However, the way I see it, the noise in a holographic plate is itself some proportion of the reconstruction beam. It's asumed to be caused by scatter from the grains. In this case, the scatter intensity would be proportional to the number of grains, the size of the grains, the density of the grains, the scattering cross section of the grain and the wavelength of the reconstruction beam. I'm here assuming that the scatter is not caused by straightforward Lambertian or Fresnel reflection, but by excitation modes within the crystal caused by the e-field of the incoming (reconstruction radiation). Since the grain size is far less than lambda, this seems justified. There is one problem for me. At these high matter densities, there should be no scatter! Any radiation from a particular scattering center would be k*(lambda/2) from another scattering center. So, first, what's causing the scatter? It's possible that the scatter cross section is dependant on the shape of the crystal, which is reasonable.. In this case, you'd expect statistically, that the scatter is very dependant on the processing scheme. Rayleigh scattering requires a low matter density. Lambertian or fresnel reflection requires that the scattering volume be about 700nm or more, not 10. My feeling is that a large proprtion of the "scatter" is actually low frequency Bragg or Raman Nath fringe structures caused by internal reflections within the emulsion during recording - spurious low-frequency gratings - due to inhomogenities within the emulsion. Since these gratings will have their grating vectors pointed at random with random magnitudes, the appearance of the resulting diffraction will give the appearance of an overall haze - noise. However, this noise is not now Rayleigh, but more Raman Nath diffraction. Perhaps even the Bragg planes themselves are diffractive structures, since they must contain discontinuities. Thus the wavelength profile of the resulting haze is uniform across the spectrum. But since the photopic curve makes us more sensitive to the area around 500nm, we tend to see this "noise" predominantly.

As I say, purely speculative.

Dinesh Fri Mar 11, 2005 2:51 pm #14
Yes AH would help. It helps because it kills the glare coming back off the plate. Unless your plate were super clean, the reference beam would cause glare which reacts with the incoming object beam and creates spurious fringes inside the emulsion. These fringes are reflective fringes, but there are still surface fringes and they can be seen in transmission mode as an overall haze.
