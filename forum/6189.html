JohnFP Fri Apr 20, 2007 11:39 am #3
Raul, I will take a stab at the things I am familiar with.

The final color replay of a reflection hologram in DCG has to do with the brag planes. The orientation, spacing and size all play a role. If the brag planes are all the same (which is more easily obtained in thinner emulsion to negate too much differential swelling during processing) then you get a very narrow band hologram with strongest replay at one illuminating angle. Moving this angle does not cause a color change but a dimming of the image.

Now if you process to achieve a more broadband hologram then you have brag planes that have swollen more then others throughout the different layers. As in a reflection hologram most planes are lying close to flat but at an angle, this differential of swelling causes a reinforcement of the different wavelengths but at different angles. So as you move the illuminating light from a larger to smaller incident angle, the brag planes suited to reconstruct that particular wavelength are reinforced, thus you see that color. As you change that angle the color changes.

I am not great at explaining this but I think I have stated clear enough to understand.

I ALWAYS, get an image when the DCG hologram is wet in the last alcohol bath. In fact even if I use only 10 seconds in each bath I leave it in the last bath until I see a hologram. And to a beginner in DCG it would seem bright (compared to silver halide). But after drying the hologram gets much, much brighter. There are a couple of things that may be causing your hologram to disappear when dried.

It could be simply that you have not dried the hologram completely. I use two methods to dry my DCG holograms. One, I take a hair dryer and put it on its hottest and hardest blow. I then keep the hair dryer just a couple of mm off the emulsion and dry with what I describe as an ironing method. I start off in center and quickly move in a circle to the outside to get the majority of liquid of the plate. Then I go back to the center and dry it hot and heavy until the image appears. You will see dark red regions, these are regions that are not yet dry. Continue to dry these and move outward and in a circle until the entire hologram is dry, visible and bright. I basically iron out these red undry regions from the center out. One mistake I used to make when I first started DCG is thinking there was no hologram, when in fact I simply did not dry it long enough. Sometimes it takes 5 to 10 minutes or longer to dry. The second drying process I use is I blow off the majority of liquid again after the last bath for just a few seconds. Then I take the hologram and put it into an oven for 1 hour at 200F. This takes longer but does a very good job of forcing all moisture out of the hologram.

It could be that the fringes have been processed past their point of no return. That is, the fringes are supported and ok in the alcohol but upon drying they collapse completely. This is usually caused by too warm a water soak bath (after fixing or hardening) in conjunction with the emulsion age or bloom (low bloom is softer longer, young film is softer, higher temperature water on soft gelatin stretches the fringes to the point of tearing which destroys the fringes). Usually the hologram looks very milky white after drying with this problem and there is no fix other then to make the hologram over again.

It could be that your ambient humidity is high and as you are drying the hologram the water in the air is being forced into the emulsion through the drying process. Alcohol and gelatin are hygroscopic and I have found that if the hologram is dried too slowly in a humid environment then the alcohol actually pulls moisture in from the air and you are trying to force the alcohol out.

What does the hologram look like after you dry it? Is it milky white? Does it look all crackly? Do you see any colors at all? Can you post a photo?

> besides that, when recording angle is too small, fringes may become so closelly spaced that there will be an important shift towards UV

Processing of DCG can be done, although hard to master to get repeatablility, such that there is no shift in either direction. Thus the replay as the same angle as the recoding reference angle yields a hologram of the wavelength made to record it.

> but when dry, they shrink again to its normal, non visible state?

The normal state of fringes recorded with a particular wavelength is that particular wavelength in reconstruction. Unless you are using an invisible laser (uv or infrared) the "normal color" of the reconstruction will not be invisible. It is the processing the take the fringes away from their normal size and causes the shift, possibly into the invisible UV or infrared.
