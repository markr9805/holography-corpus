Dinesh Mon Jun 06, 2011 12:34 pm #2
> Justin W: I'm curious first whether an HOE sheer plate would even work for checking reference beam collimation

I don't think it would. Consider that a shear plate (we used to call them optical flats) works by virtue of the fact that it has two faces. The light hits the front surface and partially reflects and partially transmits. The transmitted light hits the back surface and (partially) reflects. At 45 degrees, the two beams overlap to give, or not to give, fringes. The visibility of the resulting broad fringes is high because the intensity of both the reflected beams is approximately equal. A holographic version would require that incident light at 45 to the plate normal hits the hologram and diffracts at 45 degrees from a multiplicity of Bragg planes, not reflects from one surface. Depending on the thickness of the emulsion, the zero order light, presumably analogous to the partially transmitted light of the shear plate, hits the back of the emulsion, travels once more through all the Bragg planes. The intensity of the return beam would be quite a bit lower than the primary diffracted beam. You would probably not get broad interference lines after the light has passed through all the Bragg planes. In addition, there would some dispersion, so the diffracted light field would be a smear of colour, which would hide any fringes.

> Justin W: and second, whether it would be possible to even make an HOE from a sheer plate...

You could certainly make a hologram that would carry out the same function. If you used an actual shear plate to generate the object beam, by placing it in the path of a collimated beam for instance, then the object field would have a series of lines which would record. However, if you hit the plate with two beams from either side of the plate, both at 45 degrees to the plate normal, ie symmetrically with the plane of the plate, then provided you developed the hologram with absolutely no swelling or distortion then an incident collimated beam at 45 degrees would produce a diffracted collimated beam at 45 degrees. However, you'd have to choose your medium with care and no matter what medium you chose, there'd be some issues. If you use silver, then you could minimise swelling. In this case, you'd get a collimated beam out at roughly the same wavelength, but the efficiency would be too low. If you use dcg, it's very difficult to avoid swelling. Even a narrow band dcg would be about 30nm or more wide. This would first not diffract at the same wavelength, and secondly, would not diffract at 45 degrees.

Having said all that, if you're doing display, you really don't need this kind of accuracy. These devices collimate to within a few lambda and your eye certainly can forgive a deviation of a few lambda when looking at a display. Simply allowing the collimated (or not) beam to travel a significant distance and measuring its diameter would give you an idea of how collimated it is. You don't even have to use the entire collimated beam. Let's say you have an 8inch collimator. have the colimated beam hit a 1 inch mirror, which then hits another 1 inch mirror at the other end of the table, which hits another 1-inch mirror at the other of the table etc. With about 3 or 4 1-inch mirrors, even with a small table, you can throw the beam several feet. After several feet, if the beam is still 1 inch in diameter in both axes, you're as collimated as you'd get by measuring with a shear plate.

Hello Justin and Dinish,

The funny thing is, if you could actually hold a piece of holo film flat enough (not really possible in the real world) it would act like a shear plate with NO exposure at all. Just like a real optical flat has two sides, so does a piece of film (or glass plate). The light would bounce off those two sides and interfere just like the flat.

I'm not saying it would be bright, or with good contrast, or really tell you if your colimated, but technically it would work. The resulting image would tell you much more about the flatness of the film than the collimation of your beam though.

Now, back to the real world. There are a few ways you can check collimation. 1, the way your planing, see if the footprint of your mirror changes size as the beam travels.

2, put something of a known size in the beam and see if that shadow changes size. That way is sometimes easier than measuring the mirrors footprint. If you use this method make sure you put the object into the beam after the collimating mirror.

3, which I like best sometimes, bounce the collimated beam back into the mirror and back to the microscope objective that your using. When the beam gets back to the lens it should be a spot. Let the reflected light hit the microscope lens just off to the side, so it doesn't go back into the lens but hits just next to it on the barrel so you can see the size of the reflected spot.

Move your stuff around, the closer you are to collimated the smaller the spot will be. You don't need to use a mirror to do the reflecting back to your collimating mirror. Just use a glass plate in your holder and turn it to normal incidence to the beam so it bounces back to the microscope. A piece of plain glass will probably be enough to see the spot, even with weak lasers.

Since the glass is imperfect so will be the spot. Don't worry, just look for the point where its the smallest or it "switches over". Sometimes you'll be changing the distance and the spot will be like a squiggly line that's rotating or moving in some other fashion as you move the mounts. When it changes the direction of rotation, that's your spot.

BobH Mon Jun 06, 2011 6:59 pm #5
If you're parabolic collimating mirror is being used off axis, I'd be careful with your measurements. If off horizontally, measure the height of the collimated beam in the center of it. Make that constant over distance. Use exactly the same off axis tilt and the same mirror when replaying a transmission H1 to make an H2, and all the aberrations will cancel themselves out.

And no, a simple HOE of a shear plate won't work. Only buy a shear plate if you're using a collimating lens, or using your mirror on axis. Then, it's a great help and time saver. Well worth the money if bought used.
