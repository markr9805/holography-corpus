BobH Sun Mar 20, 2005 4:35 pm #4
I believe the priority of equipment for making holograms is:

1. a laser with adequate coherence length and stability,

2. a power meter to tell you how much light there is, and

3. a plateholder that is stable, repeatable and convenient to use.

Everything else can be improvised (with knowledge regarding how to make interference fringes hold still), and successful recordings will be the result. The power meter should be able to accurately measure nW to mW with as little pointing sensitivity as possible.

There are many variables present when making pictorial holograms; such as film characteristics, beam ratios, hot spots off the object, beam wandering, laser output power, processing performance, stability, etc. An easy to use and accurate power meter helps keep track of many of these variables and is therefore worthy of serious expenditure. In other words, don't skimp on the power meter. "The joy of high quality remains long after the pain of high price is forgotten."

Joe Farina Sat Mar 26, 2005 2:55 pm #20
When I make holograms, I measure light intensity in two ways: the output of the laser itself, and the amount of light at the film plane (for the beam ratio). (I don't use a meter to try to determine the amount of exposure needed, just trial and error.)

As for the power of the laser, I think the only real way to be confident about it is by buying a calibrated meter. I bought a LaserCheck some years ago, and even though they cost about $300, I think it's well worth the price. All visible wavelengths can be measured. I think Bob said that he considers power meters to be essential tools for serious holographers, and I agree. It's important to know the output of your laser, and it is also important to know how lossy your optics are. (These losses can be suprising for the variety of optics we use. Say for example, you have a transfer mirror straight from the laser. Already, maybe 10% of your laser power is gone due to absorption losses from the mirror (of course this depends on the quality of the mirror). And so it goes for all of the other optics down the line. The other day I measured the loss through my microscope objective/spatial filter, and only 3/5 of the light appeared to be getting through.

The second thing I use a meter for is measurement of beam ratios at the film plane. Probably from the earliest days of split-beam holography, a simple analog multimeter has been used with a cadmium sulfide photo cell. This works quite well. The only problem is that analog meters are becoming pretty scarce these days (almost everything seems to be digital, which I suppose could be used, but I would rather see a needle instead of digits). It's important to get one with as wide a variety of "ranges" as possible (so that many different light intensity levels can be measured). For the cadmium sulfide cells, the resistance ranges are used. For a solar cell (which Kris suggested), the DC voltage ranges are used. By the way, I tried Kris' suggestion this morning and it appears to work well! It would be an improvement over the small-sized cadmium sulfide cell, I think, because the solar cells can have a large surface area (about the same size as the holographic plate as Kris mentioned) so that an average reading can easily be taken over the whole surface of the plate. I suppose a digital multimeter can be used (the autoranging type) with the photocell or solar cell, but I would rather use an analog meter, because I want to see how the needle is behaving. (I think this would be especially true if a small photocell is used.) Here's one with a big needle and a wide variety of ranges, for $53.25:

[http://www.lashen.com/vendors/protek/An ... meters.asp](http://www.lashen.com/vendors/protek/AnalogMultimeters.asp)

> Tony: If you measure an incident beam parallel to the plate, you are measuring the energy that hits the plate in the measurement area. But if you measure it perpendicular to the beam dimension, you get a higher measurement, because you are getting more of the beam inside the measurement area.

If the reference beam hits the plate at the Brewster angle, and you measure its intensity with a probe facing right into the beam, you get an error of ca 50% (there is a cosine factor here, but let's avoid math . That's already taken care of in the curves on my site. In principle you get the right reading when having the probe parallel to the plate, but, depending on the probe, it may not be accurate.

However: it doesn't make much sense to try to be too accurate here, as the lighting is very uncritical and the variations across the plate can be pretty large anyway. That's why I think a computer interface is overkill - if you measure a beam ratio at one point, and then move 2 inches away, say, it will in general change a lot, lets say 50% to say some number. I think at this point it is a matter of experience how to gauge the right exposure. I usually consider an estimated average of the readings over the center zone, and just make sure that the ratio doesn't fall off too much in the corners, and that the brightest spots do not overexpose.

Sometimes I try to compensate a reduced object intensity in one corner, with a reduced ref beam intensity in that region, in order to keep a good ratio. This is a matter of case-by-case judgement, and a computer wouldn't help here. (Though I use a computer for timing the shutter).

Thus I think that accuracies of better than 25% or so would not improve anything, and that a single spot measurement in the center is not too meaningful. But I am eager to lean about the experience of others.

wler Sat Mar 26, 2005 6:57 pm #28
> Jim McPherson: Tony,I'm not convinced the OPT101m will be good for high power readings, but I really am not experienced enough to know what the maximum power reading holographers need is.-Jim

They stop to be useful beyond a mW or so [didn't check more precisely].

Actually I was looking some while ago into building a power meter for the 0-100mW range, to form a bridge between some other meters. I did some research and experimentation, and found that this is extremely hard to do if one wants to get an accuracy worthy to speak of. There is good reason why commercial power meters are so expensive.

First thing is that photo diodes saturate in the mW region, means the reading becomes non-linear (esp if the power density exceeds a few mW/cm^2). This necessitates the use of an external absorber, like a neutral density filter. This brings in an extra factor of calibration uncertainty, and also has power limitations (depending on the filter, the limit may well be under 100mW, see the other thread). Metallic filters typically will create interference effects (from reflections off the diode) which means the reading will jump when turning the detector by a slight amount. Absorptive filters are better in this respect, esp AR coated ones, but also have limited power handling and, gasp, may bleach out when illuminated strongly (see Sam G's remark in alt.lasers). I found that my OD1 filter, which supposedly transmits 10%, actually transmits 12%, which means it introduces an error of 20%.

All boils down that it is quite impossible to build such a thing which would have decent accuracy without calibration by another power meter. The calibration needs to be repeated for each wavelength separately (that's why thermal sensors are much better). And even then, it's hard to get reproducible results. I was recently putting such a thing together, using an UDT PIN diode and a good AR coated ND filter, and I get readings which differ by about 10% between measurements even for the same setup (and that's for ratiometric measurements, for which the laser power is factored out). I don't think one can do much better than that in practice, but for hobby uses that's fine. But at any rate the absolute calibration needs to be obtained by another power meter, there's no way around that!

As for thermal sensors, also these need to be calibrated but then

they are pretty wavelength-independent. While one can easily

illuminate a blackened thermistor and measure its temperature change

(or better, measure the absorbed power by substitution, ie by keeping

the temp constant), it's still unclear how much light is reflected

off the termistor and not absorbed.

So again, there's good reason why commercial power meters are so

expensive, and that's similar to microwave power meters for similar

reasons. I believe it'd be hard to get an accuracy better than

say 20-30% or so, with any sort of home-build non-calibrated meter.

As a side remark, one needs at any rate a "transimpedance" (low

input resistance) amplifier so that one measures current and not

voltage (which runs non-linear). That's certainly not a big deal

with standard opamps, but needs to be done for sure.

Then there is the difficulty that the laser power tends to vary

during any calibration process, just due to Murphy's law. So one

needs to either have an ultrastable laser, or track the power with

another sensor and factor power variations out. With a digital

interface this can be done more or less automatically, ie by computer.

It was only this way that I was able to determine optical densities

and sensor sensitivities down to reproducibility of ca 5-10%.

So what's the upshot ? After all the attempts to carefully calibrate

wler Mon Mar 28, 2005 7:46 pm #34
Just came back from a trip and first thing (hobbywise. to check

was how well a green LED would perform as a power meter. I picked

a random green one and found at 10mW 532nm it gave off 20-24uA

depending on polarization (I used a waveplate to rotate the

polarization). So it was some 20-25% less than the popular value of

2.8uA/mW, and that's not at all bad.

It may be that the value of 2.8uA/mW (sometimes I saw 4.5 cited)

pertains to a particular brand, ie the one Radio Shack sells, and

variations between lots and brands are anyway expected.

The linearity up to 100mW was pretty good, it gave almost exactly

10 times the current at 10mW. Apparently only a tiny amount of

energy enters the small junction so that the diode is not driven

into saturation (real photo diodes do like 500uA/mW, which is two

hundred times more sensitive and correspondingly easier to overload).

So this may solve the attenuator problems indicated earlier (at

least to the order of 100mW which would be fine for most

holographers, I have no idea where the linearity would break down,

and what the damage thresholds is). A simple circuit to convert

current to voltage with proper amplification, with the possibility

for external calibration (so that eg 100mW would correspond to 1V

output) could yield a simple power meter at almost no cost. One

drawback is that the diode needs to be very carefully adjusted, up

to a few degrees, otherwise the reading is very much off.

As Bob H has predicted, I would chime in and be pro using the meter sensor parallel to the plate. It just makes perfect sense since this is how the light sensitive material is going to see the light. All the cosine and polarization attenuation is taken care of automatically.

Sometimes manufacturers don't help the matter by putting tubes in front of the cells or mounts that could cast shadows at steeper angles across the face of the detector so accuracy could be off. Or the mount is so thick that it doesn't feel like you're even close to the fringe field.

The probe with the Newport 840 meter fulfills these requirements; it has a one cm^2 area detector, low profile, and its connecting cord comes out the side so that the wand can be placed anywhere on the holo plate's surface. (Who would have ever thought Newport would ever get anything right?)

This technique has served me well for the last 25+ years. Just be consistent in whichever mode you want to operate in.

wler Wed Feb 15, 2006 7:47 am #46
> aghisays: I would also be interested in knowing how to calibrate a laser power meter. Michael and I co-own one like this:http://www.technika.com/Sper/s840011.htmthat is calibrated for HeNe.

I just checked the description and it said there is a curve included

for extrapolation to other wavelengths. This should be accurate

enough for most holography applications, I would think! Anything

that is more accurate than, say 20-30%, should suffice.

At any rate, calibrating or transferring a calibration takes some

effort if one seeks an accuracy better than this number. You need

to create a very clean, ie homogeneous, collimated and stable

illumination to start with. What I was doing is as follows: First,

clean the beam via a spatial filter, expanding it very wide. Use a

perfectly clean parabolic mirror to collimate a piece in the center

of it (where the intensity varies very little). Then at some

distance (like 2m) put an aperture (a hole) with an opening a little

bit larger than the detector size. This hole should be black, esp

on the inside, to avoid any specular reflections. And then just

place the various detectors right behind the aperture to compare

them.

I do this at a separate table, in order not to touch the spatial

filter table. Also, I create a side beam to monitor the intensity

of the laser at any given time - in this way I can factor out

intensity fluctuations, which can be serious, depending on the laser

(esp argon). In this way I get measurements that are reproducible

up to a few percent. And that's anyway within the accuracy of my

commcercial power meters (most professional meters have an accuracy

of typ +/- 5%).

Edit, PS: The above procedure applies to to illumination measurements

(ie power density, uW/cm^2) and not to power measurements. I mistook

what was asked for (as the thread name is light meter and not power

meter For power measurement with a photodiode, you need to cut

down the laser power to a few mW, by something like a neutral density

filter, but this brings in extra error and uncertainty.

I happen to know a source of precision Newport OD3 (0.1%) attenuators

883-SL, for like $15 apiece. I am receiving some of those shortly,

and I can give you the email of the person who has more. Those

would be perfect to measure higher powers with photodiodes (they

are used to extend the range of the Newport 818-SL photodiode power

sensors to 2W). Of course, extra calibration is necessary for making

precise measurements.
