While automation is nice, it is possible to:

...use a manually adjusted slit and a manual card shutter, but this means you are right there near the table for each exposure and you need a way to accurately time each exposure.

...use film negs or transparencies, but image registration could pose a problem. DLP is also good choice when automating everything.

Also due to the losses associated with LCD, etc. you might need a more powerful laser. Mine went from 7mW to 20mW to become usable(shorter exposures.)

I would also say you need a real love for this and lots of patience. I tried twice to do this manually. In the begining I was trying to do thirty 300 second exposures with a manual shutter, manually moving the slit. The whole thing counting settle times took over 3.5 hours...and I ended up with nothing but a back-ache! I think it is doable manually, but everything, including your own actions must be perfect.

Ed Wesly Mon Oct 31, 2005 8:07 pm #12
Although the mirrors are vibrating to give grey scale, they are stopped at the end of their travel when they throw a bright spot on the projection screen. So the fringe system generated by that pixel is frozen for that instant.

I know it works really well because I used a system at my former place of employment that had an SVGA (600 by 800) TI DMD projector chip. It was from a developer's kit, so all the unnecessary peripherals (light bulb interlocks etc.) were not there to begin with.

The layout was designed by Craig Newswanger. He had years of experience recording stereograms from embossed to meter square size, derived from movie film. He had worked out the geometry of the master to screen distance and alpha angle tip with these earlier systems.

This incarnation of his stereo system was set up at the CFC Northern Banknote (at that time) facility in Countryside, IL, during the summer of 1998. When I started working there in October of '98 the bugs were all worked out, and it proved to be a reliable performer, with some of the holographic stereograms that it has churned out that viewers of this forum may be familiar with are those on the Beanie Baby labels, the security labels on Intel chip packages, and the dropped in 3-D Microsoft Windows Flag on certain issues of software CD's. It is still up and running fine.

With the SVGA sized chip, 6" by 8" images had visible pixelation, but in this digital day and age you could probably get away with it. The switch to XGA resolution turned into a bit of a problem as that chip's developers kit included a board with the DMD but it cost $10k (that's right, more than 3 or 4 projectors!) plus that board is incapable of producing grey tones, so you need to buy another $6k board! Hats off to anyone who gets those DMD's fired up from a non-working projector.

A few other details: the slits were .2" wide (50 over a 10" width), with the slits moving and the plate stationary. The master was not parallel to the screen but angled toward the top of the image, as is typical of registered rainbow set ups. The blue slit at the top of the master was centered on the image.

The Reference Beam was collimated very well, and was incident upon the master along the normal. This made transferring the image easier.

Enough reference beam light was used to keep the exposure at 10" or so, (supplied by about 3/4 Watt 458 nm photons) with a development time of 5' in what is known in some places as Nick's #6, published in the '88 Lake Forest Symposium. The ratio was not tweaked for every different image. We had observed that it really wasn't necessary to play with it every time, since some images had lots of black background, so a lot of light would have been wasted trying to get down to a 4:1 ratio. This is because of something that I have christened "Bjelkhagen's Conundrum" an experiment which Hans and I often discussed but never got around to performing. Maybe if posted it on the forum someone might perform it.

Another stereogram system that I am intimately familiar with was one that I built with students at the School of the Art Institute. (Described in the 1991 LFC symposium)

Dean Randazzo was the driving force, modelling the School's after something he had seen while working at Lee Lacey's. Initial design incorporated a home 16 mm projector, since there were plenty of Bolexes to check out. We had a turntable for filming.

The first incarnation used a good old-fashioned typewriter carriage to move the slit, spaced two pica pecks on the space bar apart thanks to an electromagnet. The H1 was 8" by 10".

The drivers for all this was designed by the very capable Electronics Department Technician, Ed Bennet. This first incarnation used 555 timing chips etc mounted on a piece of plywood, looking like the guts of a pinball machine. There is a picture in an old L.A.S.E.R. News showing Nick Phillips "tweaking" the device.

Eduardo Kac, the bloated Holo-Poet, took notes during all this as his contribution to the project. However, much to the chagrin of all others concerned, he used this to perform one of the sleaziest bits of holographic skullduggery ever, like misrepresenting that this was his invention and not a collaboration, and getting himself a job teaching a computer holography class.

He said that he had some good news to tell us at lunch, and when he announced that he had this class that relied on the printer, I watched in horror as Dean stopped a forkful of food in mid-flight to his mouth, put it down and couldn't eat another bite he was so bummed out. After all, it was his inspiration and perspiration that got it built!

The next phase used a wider dot-matrix printer chassis to move a slit across the long side of a 30 by 40 sheet of film. Slits were 4mm wide, 87 of them across the master. All the electronics were mounted in a nice box.

Since we had no collimating optics that size, the reference beam made three passes of the 10' table for a length of 25'+, pseudo-collimated. The master was parallel to the screen, and the transfers looked great, although registered rainbows were out of the question.

This last phase of the device was moved from the basement of School of the Art Institute building on Columbus Drive to 112 South Michigan Avenue into some very cramped quarters. There is some other behind the scenes drama as to why that happened. Matt Schreiber, Grad Student, Kurt Lawson Holography Department Technician and Jesus Lopez, international-student-extraordinaire-at-large helped move two 4' by 10' Newport tables in a record-breaking cold winter. No volunteering to help in any way from Eduardo, even though he was still teaching Computer Holography class.

We got the 4' by 6' slab o' steel Beginning Holo Class Table ready for the first day of class, and the printer running the second week of Advanced Class. The transfer table was set up behind the mastering table, and it too used the long path length pseudo-collimated approach to rainbow, achromatic, and reflection transfers.

The printer table had a rugged reference beam path and plate holder alignment; all optics were screwed to the table, no mag bases. With the reference beam locked in place, beam stealers were positioned in the object beam so that not only could stereograms from film be made, but masters of diffusely reflecting objects, or back lit objects, or one step rainbow shadowgrams. Lots of good work was done with this system.

But alas and alack, when I left and Eduardo returned he ordered this system dismantled. It doesn't sound like it is up and running, and if it ever will be again.

Other observations: we had a deuce of a time finding a decent lens for the projection system. We used a 16 by 16" square groundglass screen, and we wanted to be able to change the size of the image, for portrait or landscape formats. We didn't want to move the projector back and forth to change the magnification because that would cause path length problems

We had a PO with a local camera store, and we kept shuttling at first zoom lenses to form the image, but even though we were trying high roller Canon and Nikon lenses (we had $ in the budget! Plus lenses were cheaper back then since they didn't have all the auto-focus devices!) there were always pesky internal bounces that showed up in the image. The lenses were AR-coated, but not for He-ne.

The solution we arrived upon used a Canon 50 mm 1.4 for landscape, blowing up the 16 mm format to almost 12 by 16, and their 35 mm for portrait, making it about 16" high to fill a 30 by 40 Agfa sheet film with a head! With the DMD machine at CFC the projection objective which was a pair of achromats in the best Rapid Rectilineartradition still could have problems with garbage light.

And there is a lot of garbage light coming out of the DMD's. The pixels are like 13 microns square with a 1 micron gap inbetween them, and that makes a dandy low frequency grating. You'll see all kinds of stuff flying all over the place reducing contrast of the image.

But at least they're better contrast than those LCD panels for overheads! My experience with them was in researching a hologram that displayed CAT scans. Dr. John Kaufman, no relation to the famous multi-color holographer, got one of these and we would expose, in a back lit reflection hologram mode, one of these units placed at varying depths behind the plate, each position at the depth of the original CAT scan. Kind of like the Voxel system, although before I had heard of them.

The holograms were more of the abstract rather than medical laboratory grade, as the screen's contrast ratio was 2:1; the off position was half as bright as the on. Probably there are better screens out there, but it is something to watch out for.

I hope someone can benefit from this dissertation! It seems like there is a sleeping rattlesnke den of stereographers out there!

BobH Tue Nov 01, 2005 3:27 pm #14
> So and old b/w vga LCD transmission panel for overhead projection is not worth persuing and I actually got lucky in it not functioning?

I used a Proxima VGA overhead projection LCD panel to make the "TV" hologram used on the cover of "The Holography Marketplace 5th Edition" ten years ago. This was a full parallax image, 3D in both directions, and animated in the vertical.

The object beam (IIRC) was:

a) diverged and collimated off a tilted parabolic mirror,

b) converged off another parabolic mirror,

c) focused more through an 8" diameter lens,

d) sent through the panel (6"x9" I think).

e) The image was then projected onto a diffuser at the size used (2x3"),

f) and the light finally went through a rectangular mask that was moved in front of the 12x16" master plate.

The only modification to the panel was to disconnect the fan inside it. The excessive scatter in the final photopolymer copies was the result of the subsequent steps I needed to take to deliver the master on schedule.

The Simian systems I worked with incorporated LCD panels harvested from NEC projectors. I wish I had one of those to do the "TV" project!
