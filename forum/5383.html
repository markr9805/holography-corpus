Wouldn't a vacuum platen plateholder be easier to use for creating H1 transmission holograms instead of index matching the film to glass?

Then you only have to index match "keepers" to put in the H2 setup.
MichaelH Fri Feb 17, 2006 4:57 pm #2
"easier" is very relative...

It assumes:

1) You have a vacuum platen.

2) It's reliable.

3) You either have a design that doesn't require vacuum motors be running during the exposure or that they're far enough away not to generate hologram wrecking vibrations.

About the only certainty without getting to specifics is that a vacuum platen is less messy.

geso Sat Feb 18, 2006 5:02 pm #5
This is something I have wanted to build for a while.

My plan is to have a vacuum pump evacute a largish holding tank, something like glass carboy. Pressure would be low enough, that there is no need to worry about implosion. The pump would only run to create a slight vacuum inside the tank. During exposure there is no need to worry about vibrations, since the pump isn't running.

To build the platen itself, I have bought some 1" thick sheets of delrin platic from a woodworking shop. I am going to use a router to mill a nice square hole into the plastic. There will be a 'lip' milled, which will hold an aluminum perf board. The aluminum perf board will support the film. The back of the platen will have a connection for the vacuum.

I still haven't figured out a few things, like how to make sure the film has a good seal against the aluminum. Also, the holes on the perf. are staggered a bit, I might have to fill them with epoxy. I have everything to give it a try, but its just too damn cold right now to get into my shop!
Ed Wesly Sat Feb 18, 2006 9:02 pm #6
I have seen and used two different types of vacuum devices for holding film. They both work, but they both have a similar problem, and that is the film sometimes picks up a texture.

For transmission holos only: a piece of metal (or plastic just as well) is drilled with a lot of tiny holes, looking kind of like a miniature Newport table. This sheet is attached to a frame where a plenum has been milled or routed out behind the perforated sheet. The film is sucked down to the perf board, but if the vacuum is too high, the film will deform into the holes. If making a thing like this, watch what happens to a piece of film as the vacuum is applied. You want just enough to hold the film without deforming it.

This device was at Lake Forest College, and TJ had a 4 by 5, an 8 by 10, and a semi-cylindrical version that might have been 10 or 12 inches high. I used the 4 by 5 many times to make stock laser transmission holos for Integraf (phone with magnifier, multi-channel sherub and skull, gratings, etc.) for their Teacher's Holo Pak.

Curiously the vacuum pump was continuously running, even during the exposures! The table was an 8' by 8' by 2" thick piece of ship hull steel on a lot of inner tubes! Not only a lot of mass, but its resonances were so low the pump couldn't match them. I would step on the vacuum hose to cut off the vacuum while changing film! The pump would sometimes be outside of the room, not for vibration's sake but to keep the vacuum pump oil fumes out of the lab

Another kind that I saw, and the first time I saw one was at the Lasersmith's lab circa 1982 or 3. A piece of glass was recessed in a metal frame. Surrounding the glass were some channels milled around the glass, where the vacuum was applied. The outer inch or so of the film was held by the vacuum, and the inner part was held against the glass, so the usable area of the film was cut down, like for instance on a 30 by 40 cm (approx. 12" by 16") the image area was down to 10 by 14 or so.

I built ofne of these for myself, but when the film was sucked down sometimes you could see an orange peel effect! Too much vacuum could be dangerous! When I built a thing like this I used a vacuum cleaner motor, and had a tee off the line with a valve to bleed the vacuum. It wasn't easy to find the right trade-off of stable holding versus warping the film.

If you are worried about motors running during exposure, there are hand-held vacuum pumps sold by some of the major suppliers of science education equipment. (I have seen the same pump in a food packaging kit, to make a tight shrink wrap, and even in penis enlargement ads!) It takes a bit of muscle to get things held down tight.

Another kind of vacuum device is to use running water! Hook a tee fitting to your faucet, let the water shoot straight down the top or cross part of the tee, in other words the tee is rotated 90 degrees from its letter orientation, and hook up the vacuum line to the ascender. It's not much, but it can work for a 4 by 5!
Martin Sun Feb 19, 2006 9:51 am #7
In his Photographic Handling (in Caulfield, Handbook of Optical Holography, Academic press 1979) Steven Benton mentions a vacuum-channel film holder:

> Rather more success has followed the use of vacuum-back holders, which draw a vacuum between the film and a flat and rigid back. Air flow can be distributed through a multiplicity of holes distributed behind the film, but the very slight dimpling over each hole is often apparent as a Bragg-angle mismatch during viewing. Instead, we use a single channel around the film periphery (...). Its success depends on the plate being very flat indeed (as provided by a single-pass milled finish) and on waiting long enough for all the air trapped behind the film to escape. Here too, every particle of trapped dust is likely to slow up as a dimple, so that the very highest quality exposures still are made on glass plates, except in the case of pulsed laser exposures with no rigidity requirements, although film flatness may remain a problem. (p354)
