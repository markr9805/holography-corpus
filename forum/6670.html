Joe Farina Mon Jan 21, 2008 9:33 pm #6
Rallison makes the following comment regarding regular DCG:

"The glass may be soda lime plate or float glass or most any other kind but it has to be thick enough to withstand the shrinking forces generated during exposure. This means it should be double strength or thicker (3 to 6 mm) for 8 X 10 shots, single strength (2 to 3 mm) for 4 X 5 and 5 X 7, and may be picture glass or as thin as 1 mm for 2 X 2 exposures."

On a side note, the "picture glass" mentioned above (1 mm) is thin glass previously used to cover framed pictures. This is not readily available anymore, and tends to be expensive. It has been replaced by slightly thicker glass which doesn't break as easily as the older, thinner stuff.

On a purely intuitive level, I doubt that plate bowing is your problem with MBDCG, but on the other hand, I always use double strength (3mm) even on 2.5 X 2.5 inch plates (for regular DCG). I also used 3mm glass with 4 X 5 plates with MBDCG without apparent bowing problems (5 minute exposures). There have been quite a few postings of highly successful MBDCG holograms on this forum, without apparent bowing, and I would tend to think they were done on ordinary double-strength glass (3mm or 1/8 inch thick).

Sergio Tue Jan 22, 2008 1:38 am #8
Tracing a parallel with a polymer coated onto glass plate, principally after development, not recording, a separated gelatine layer would shrink uniformly in all directions, but on glass plates the the adhesion of polymer on the surface provide a force that opposes any changes in the polymer's shape in the direction parallel to the glass.

The force places the polymer under tensile stress parallel to the plate. The force acts only on the interfacial plane and remains constant while the increasing polymer thickness require a remarkable force increase to maintain the polymer shape.

At some critical force, provided from gelatine development, the emulsion may delaminate, curve the plate or result in nonuniform shrinkage were the middle section of the polymer contracts while the edges remain fixed by the substrate, seems this is your case that may occur with glass thickness of d > 1mm,.

Perhaps a very hard gelatin or different bloom strength can provide a insight to models that can be adapted for a better results.

> Unlike with normal DCG, in MBDCG hardening of the light-struck fringes occurs during exposure.Would you care to elaborate on the rationale behind this statement?

Ok, perhaps I am not entirely knowledgable enough about classic DCG. But the way I understand it, when light strikes a classic DCG emulsion, Cr(6) is converted to Cr(5). The Cr(5) does not crosslink the gelatin. It simply adheres to the nearest gelatin molecule. The actual cross-linking takes place during a dark reaction, or in development in a strong reducing agent such as Sodium metabisulfate, where Cr(5) is donated two electrons and converted to Cr(3).

In MBDCG this is different. A dark reaction is not noticable, and development in SM does not give any benefit (I tried this). So, a logical conclusion is that the cross-linking takes place during exposure. I believe to understand from Jeff's paper that in MBDCG, light strikes the MB, which then goes to a higher energy state. The MB in the highe energy state is able to accept an electron from the electron donor (TMG). When the MB has the extra electron, it is colorless and wants to give it away to someting. That something is the Cr(6) molecule, and upon the next excitation the Cr(5) molecule. When the MB has given away it's electron, it becomes blue again and is ready again for action. (It was discussed in previous subjects that alkaline chromates, thus also MBDCG, do not dark-react.)

No. I'm just of the opinion that the dark reaction of MBDCG hasn't been adequately investigated. In conventional DCG, the diffraction efficiency continues to be enhanced (by the dark reaction) after very long time periods, even for a month or more after the exposure is stopped. When you say that the dark reaction plays "no role," I think that is a hasty conclusion. If you check the Kubota paper on MBDCG you will find the following comment:

"One method of increasing the sensitivity is to develop the exposed plate after a lapse of time....As is shown in Fig. 1, the required exposure for maximum diffraction efficiency is less for the hologram developed with a time lapse after exposure than for the one developed immediately. This means that the sensitivity of the plate can be increased by having an interval between exposure and development. The exposed plate is stored in a light tight box until development at room temperature (20 - 23C). The sensitivity becomes highest when the plate is developed several days after exposure, and then it reduces with time."

So even without taking any special measures to preserve the leuco state of the MB, he was able to use the dark reaction to considerable advantage with MBDCG. So I'm wondering if the leuco state in preserved as well, perhaps the performance would be even better.

From Jeff's papers on his TMG-DCG system I learnt to pay particular attention to one key feature: there are no dichromates involved here (so far the acronym “DCG” is misleading). There only is chromate, which is far less reactive. So no wonder the dark reaction becomes slow (not to mention the low quantity of potassium chromate present in this system). I guess even in the case where we mixed a coating solution of potassium dichromate, TMG and MB, we would end up having potassium chromate only. The highly alkaline TMG turns the dichromate into chromate.

Somewhere Rallison mentioned that adding a small amount of TMG to regular DCG improved the shelf life of the DCG layers also. Maybe this is simply due to the presence of chromate.
