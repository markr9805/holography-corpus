Let me also add something I learned recently and the hard way. One

thing I was concerned with was properties of beam splitters and

alike. Particularly troublesome are metallic coated ones, such as

those wonderful neutral density wheels. I had been wondering since

a while why my settings were not stable and pretty jumpy when turning

the wheel.

Turns out that metallic coated splitters/neutral density filters

have a ridiculously low damage threshold. To the effect that when

acting with 100mW at small beam diameter, you instantly burn little

holes into the coating, making the surface very uneven over time.

I did some research into metallic neutral density filters (for

building a power meter), and found that the damage threshold is

typically like 50mW-500mW/cm^2. So with a 100mW laser at beam

diameter of 1mm^2, you have 10W/cm^2 which can be 20-200 times over the limit.

So the message is that, if you have a powerful laser like a 315M,

don't use metallic beam splitters but splitter cubes, or dielectric

splitters. To have the beam ratio variable, the best thing is to

use a polarizing cube and waveplates. But that's another story.

Dinesh Fri Mar 11, 2005 2:47 pm #6
I think you have a point about thickness. There's also the heat dissipation in the material. It should be possible to do a rough calculation. If the beam radius is r and the thickness of the coating is d, then the mass of the metal being heated is:

m = pi(r^2)d(rho)

rho = density of metal.. If the beam power is W (per unit area), then energy /sec delivered to the volume, w_d:

w_d = pir^2d(rho)Wpir^2 = (pi^2)(r^4)Wd(rho)

If the energy needed for spluttering (let's call it the bp of the metal) is E, then time it takes to splutter t,

t = Ew_d = ms(delta theta) + m*L

where delta (theta) is, of course the temp differece between room temp and bp and L is the latent heat of evaporation. Knowing these figures, it should be possible to calculate the thickness of the metal in the different coatings, or at least, give an upper figure. Of course, I don't take dispersion into account. Strictly speaking you'd need a "loss factor" for heat dispersion into the surrounding material, which would be a function of the thermal conductivity of the metal.
