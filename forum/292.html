Jeff,

glad you mentioned that the fact that the laser can move if it's before the beamsplitter, provided it moves only by a very small amount. I don't think it's just for the picky. The books and the "experts" all say, categorically, that any motion before the beamsplitter is OK. Mitch had this problem too, that any motion before the beamsplitter was OK. I demonstrated to her, mathematically, that the allowable range of motion before the beamsplitter was greater by several orders of magnitude than the allowable range of motion after the beamsplitter - millimeters, rather than microns - but that there was still a range beyond which you could not move it. I don't think she believed me, but she did ask Benton, who confirmed the calculation and confirmed that you could move the laser, but only by a millimetre or so. Today, I hear repeatedly that you can put the laser on a rickety old wooden table and thump the table during a shot and it'll turn out OK, because any motion before the beamsplitter is OK. Another one of those myths that 's been handed down, but without the assumptions under which it was true.

By the way, concerning motion before the beamsplitter, the fringes are much more sensitive to angular motion than to linear motion. The reason is that if you rotate the laser by some small angle theta, then the beam through the splitter, the transmitted beam, rotates by theta, but the beam reflecting off the splitter, the reflected beam, rotates by 2*theta. Lateral motion of the laser causes both the transmitted beam and the reflected beam to move by the same amount. So, if the laser in the other room is fed into your studio via a transfer mirror, then the transfer mirror needs to be stable to within 1/2 a degree or so. Remember that a rotation of the transfer mirror causes a rotation of twice in the beam going into the studio, then twice that again on the reflected beam off the splitter. So a rotation of half a degree in the transfer mirror can cause a rotation of 2 degrees in the reflected beam off the splitter.

Dinesh Fri Aug 05, 2011 7:51 am #4
> holorefugee: but, can you show me the math? Or at least lead me down the path.

Say you have two beams, one going through a beamsplitter and one reflecting from it. The setup is adjusted so as to have the reflected beam hit the plate at some angle phi and the other beam at some angle theta. Then:

nu = lambda/(sin(theta) + sin(phi))

If the beam hitting the beamsplitter were rotated by a small angle, then the beams hitting the plate would also change by some small angle, delta(theta) and delta(phi),

Now :

nu' = lambda/(sin(theta+delta theta) + sin(phi + delta phi))

The denominator,

sin(theta + delta theta) + sin(phi + delta phi) = sin(theta)cos(delta theta) + cos(theta)sin(delta theta) + sin(phi)cos(delta(phi) + cos(phi)sin(delta phi) ~ sin(theta) + sin(phi) [for small delta(theta) and delta(phi)]

And so,

nu' ~ nu for small delta(theta) and delta(phi)

Thus the spatial frequency has not altered, so long as the change in angle is less than about 5 degrees. If the laser is at a distance d from the beamsplitter, then the variation in motion, delta(x), that results in a variation in 5 degrees is:

delta(x) = 2dtan(2.5)

Since the laser is usually a meter or so away from the beamsplitter:

delta(x) = 2tan(2.5) ~ 0.09m

> holorefugee: I am not sure why moving a laser inline with the beam for a mm would have any effect for example

If you move the beam inline, then both delta(theta) and delta(phi) would be 0 and there would be no effect. If you moved the beam laterally (without rotation), then again both delta(theta) and delta(phi) would be 0 and there'd be no alteration of the frequency.

If you want to do this far more rigourously, you'd have to calculate the phase variation in the reference beam and the object beam due to a small rotation of the laser. For this, you'd have to calculate the path variations p_r and p_o, then the phase variation would be k times the path variation.I suspect that if you do calculate the phase differences, then a small motion inline delta(x) would cause a phase change of kdelta(x_r) in the reference path and a phase change of kdelta(x_o) in the object path. The overall change would then be:

phi' = k*(delta(x_r) - (delta(x_o))

Dinesh Tue Aug 09, 2011 8:40 am #7
> holorefugee: A back of the coaster guess has always led me to the idea that moving the laser inline would switch bright and drark fringes lowering the contrast of the image.

Assuming that by "inline" you mean that the laser moved along the line of the beam, then a shift of pi (the switch between dark and light fringes) would result from the laser moving by pi/k, or lambda/2. Depending on the geometry, there may also be a shift in the object beam. In a single beam Denisyuk, there would also be a phase shift from the object beam of pi/k also. Since there is no shift of the object phase relative to the plate (the plate-object distance has not changed), the fringe integrity relative to the plate would seem to be maintained. If you had a beamsplitter with a bunch of mirrors, then each mirror would "see" a phase shift dependent on the path difference between the mirrors, which would depend on the angles of reflection (assuming no motion or rotation of the mirrors) and the plate would also see a phase shift dependent on the reflection angles. However, the phase shift at the beamsplitter would also translate along all the mirrors in the object path, creating a phase shift of the object light also. Since the phase gain at the beamsplitter corresponds to a phase loss after the splitter - a loss of say half lambda at the splitter results in a gain of half lambda after it - the two phase shifts would probably only cause a slight overall shift of the fringe structure.

Image contrast in a hologram would depend on the level of noise and the efficiency. If any shift in the fringes occurred during the exposure, there'd be a loss in modulaton and so a lower efficiency. If noise, ie low frequency grating superposed on the main fringe set, were present then unwanted spurious light would diffract into the image areas and cause a loss in contrast. So, if the fringes did shift by pi slowly enough (or fast enough) that a shift of pi occurred in a significant proportion of the exposure time, then yes there'd be a loss in contrast.
