OK, after spending a month on the bathroom project ([http://holographyforumorg.ntitemp.com/p ... php?t=3757](http://holographyforumorg.ntitemp.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=3757)), I'm getting back to the table...

I have also acquired some big Hene lasers - big 'ole Spectra Physics jobs. Problem is the lasers are bigger than my table or the air baffles. So it's time to get the laser off the table. I've seen many folks advocating this for various reasons.

My question is, how do folks deal with the setup? With the laser off the table on a non-isolated mount, the beam comes onto the inner-tube isolated table and eventually gets to a spatial filter. During setup, the table moves around a lot, in response to adjusting optics. Due to the shifting weight, the static position of the table changes too. This means that the adjustment of the spatial filter constantly needs to be retweaked - a real PITA.

Do folks really get the laser off the table and if so how do they deal with this problem? Or when they say the laser is off the table, is it still mounted TO the table (a la Saxby)?

Yes. It is a problem when shifting stuff around on the table. But it frees up the table space and once you get it tweeked in it should be good. If you find your spatial filter is off from day to day or week to week without moving mounts then that tells you your table has creap or if inner tubes is leaking are. As my tables are usually 4 x 8 foot putting my ion laser on the table takes up a lot of space.

I guess you have to weigh the pros and cons but having the laser off the table WILL WORK. Even for multi split beam work. Make sure your laser is on solid ground (concrete slab on first floor best) and your table does not creep.

BobH Tue May 17, 2005 5:11 am #7
I've used lots of lasers mounted off the table and don't miss the hassle associated with it. The hassle I remember was minimized by:

using a very heavy table.

 using minimal air pressure in the inner tubes.

fixing air leaks as soon as they were detected.

 always using a periscope assembly to get the beam onto the table, and through two irises as it goes the length of the table. This allows fast and convenient adjustments to get the beam on a defined path.

* using the largest pinholes I could so beam wander in the spatial filters was a bit less critical.

The most important thing I think is to design the layout of the room so that it's very easy to monitor beam alignment and adjust it. Also, I'd be careful about putting the laser head or power supply under the table because of their pumping heat into the table top. I always mounted the laser up near the system's beam height and provided a seperate flow of air around it as well.
JohnFP Tue May 17, 2005 9:25 am #8
Bob, that also reminds me of anther point. I don't direct the beam right out of the laser to my table. I let it hit a transfer mirror that is in front of the laser off the table then direct the beam from that mirror to the table. That way if something does go out I can usually tweak the mirror off the table and everything falls into place. This is especially useful for multiple beam set ups.
Tony Sat May 21, 2005 12:35 am #9
Thought I'd post an update on my efforts to get the laser off the table. The short answer is it works, of course. It is very finicky to align the beam and the spatial filter, and very sensitive to any displacement of the table after alignment.

Given that my setup is a bit unusual, I did find a way to make it much more robust. I moved the spatial filter off the table too! My setups have the beam passing through the spatial filter first, followed by a variable density beam splitter. I moved the spatial filter off the table so that it is kept in mechanical alignment with the beam. The beam splitter (of course) is still on the table just a short distance away. This way, any small table movements do not result in gross movement/extinction of the beam. Made a test hologram of this setup tonight - it worked!
