Well, I am not sure how much you want to spend and how much research and time you want to put into a meter, but Jeff Blyth turned me on to this one and I simply love it. It has the capability to being set to almost any wavelength. It can measure the beam strength out of the laser up to 1 watt and can measure the expanded beam strength at the plate down to .5uW (not milli but micro). It has a small profile and is hand held, thusly can be placed almost anywhere. For $335 US it will be a meter you will use for many years and can grow into if you decide to go with a different wavelenght or up your power.

Cristiano_Perrucci Fri Feb 02, 2007 7:50 am #9
> ErichRose: I am looking to save some money and build my own laser power meter. Has anyone built the one described in J. Iovine's book? Its all Radio Shack parts and appears fairly straight forward.Or are there other designs you might suggest?

Here's my home made power meter i'm currently using for optical measurements. It's a standard DC coupled TIA followed by a variable gain amplifier in order to linearize the photodiode responsivity for each wavelegth. Mine is tuned for 488,532,633,670nm

The circuit has been tested up to 70mW @488nm with very good results.Over this level of power I think non-linearity effects of the photodiode might affect the measure itself.

For the first prototype I used a 0.5cm^2 silicon fotodiode, multiplying the readout by two, one can get the density power per cm^2. A Excel sheet calculates energy required thus exposure time, doing the dirty job for me.

Unfortunally the diagram was written in Italian for the Italian Laser & Holography ML.

Joe Farina Fri Feb 02, 2007 3:28 pm #13
Unfortunately, those books don't seem to be available at the stores, either. That's a shame, because they are excellent (I have many of them). Radio Shack seems to be moving away from the needs of the electronics enthusiast. They seem to moving (more and more) towards "consumer products" in the pejorative sense.

Anyway, for an exposure meter, I recommend the "age-old" analog voltmeter (with a cadmium sulfide photocell, i.e., a photoresistor). I think the cadmium sulfide cells are still available from Radio Shack (!) The only problem is that it's hard to find an analog voltmeter with a lot of resistance ranges (for varying light levels). Having the needle (analog as opposed to digital) is really a necessity for this kind of work in my opinion. The one I use has a large needle and 6 resistance ranges:

[http://www.tequipment.net/ProtekA450.asp](http://www.tequipment.net/ProtekA450.asp)

As for measuring laser power, in my opinion there is no alterantive: a reliable, calibrated one is needed. I use the LaserCheck as mentioned above.
