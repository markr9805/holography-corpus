Jean Dufrasne’s bringing up the processing method in my 1991 Applied Optics paper

(in the “WHITE CRACKLY...thread) requires me to emphasise an important photo- chemical difference between the 2 systems. Because it involves what John FP is currently discussing on 2 threads I need to introduce here a third thread to make this important clarification I’m afraid.

I have just made a calculation that the weight of chromate used in my system is only 0.42 % of the weight of gelatin , and in terms of the amount of chromium involved in that system compared with the chromium equivalent in the ammonium dichromate used conventionally, my system uses only:

0.3% of the dichromate equivalent with respect to the weight of solid gelatin.

So that is less than a tenth the amount of dichromate that John uses I gather.

The reason for this huge difference is that the methylene blue (MB) dye-initiated system for use with red lasers does not involve any absorption of light by dichromate ions . The dye does the absorbing and produces a very reactive species (“leuco-MB”)

that instantly reacts with chromate to produce CrIII gelatin cross-linker. A good deal of moisture is essential in the gelatin for this system to work .

In the system that John has been using , blue light needs to be absorbed by the dichromate directly and it does not get immediately transformed to chromium III but goes through intermediates beforehand. In earlier papers in the 80’s evidence showed that increased diffraction efficiency came from leaving the exposed plate for a period of time before processing it. [No doubt this was to allow the transformation

(Cr6>Cr5>Cr4>Cr3) time to fully take place in the exposed antinodes].

John however has probably speeded that stage up hugely by using “FIX” . Fix = thiosulfate which is able to react rapidly with light struck dichromate-gelatin . (As to how it affects the unexposed dichromate I cannot answer.). On top of this, John’s system uses hardener (probably alum) and that gives a hardening bias to the gelatin which requires higher temperatures to be used in the process which as Jean hints creates much unhealthy alcohol vapor and is a fire risk .

To be honest I did not originally use less than a tenth of the dichromate equivalent and cool alcohol solutions for environmental reasons. I did it because it worked so well! In fact if you up the amount of chromate substantially it did not work at all well.

It is not only Jean who can vouch for the system see also the email in

the MB-DCG system at:

[http://cabd0.tripod.com/holograms/id4.html](http://cabd0.tripod.com/holograms/id4.html)

> JohnFP: .........................Jeff, what is in the fixer that is needed? Any ideas? This is really strange.

This is a really helpful experiment you've done John.

You comment :-

".... I thought the fixer was just to remove the AmDi and really was not needed. Especially looking at Slavich's processing and Andy's no fix but thermal hardening process. Those seem to indicate hardening is more important then fixing. ......"

There is no fixing involved.

Thermally the hardening takes place much more slowly. You know how slowly unexposed DCG plates harden up at room temp. It is hugely better at 100C.

The metabisulfite/sulfite part of the fixer is the key ingredient and it is the thiosulfate (hypo) bulk we could do without .

The metabisulfite/sulfite does 2 jobs rapidly.

1) It finishes off the intermediate steps started by the exposure turning dichromate ions into intermediate chromium compounds finishing up as chromium +++ ions which do the hardening job in the light-struck fringes. The chromium compounds must have been locked in place by the original exposure and are not free to go into solution.

The other way we can do this is thermally with more time of course.

2) The other job metabisulfite/sulfite does is to turn all the unexposed dichromate around to be an effective hardener. Now this is an important point . While the primary reaction of reducing the products from light exposure is spatially locked up, the unexposed dichromate is not and should'nt be. Which is why you recommended (if I remember rightly) vigorous agitation initially in your fix/hardener . The dichromate ions are so soluble that they get swept into solution faster than they can be reduced to chromium+++. So you finish up with a bath of Cr+++ hardener. Other aspects of this is that you may get a less satisfactory result if you cannot do your normal agitation because then the dichromate wont get out of the unexposed zones fast enough before being reduced so the dark fringes will get almost as hardened up as the light struck fringes. In your effort to sectionalize your plate into 5 sections you inevitably have not been able to give as much agitation as normal or am I wrong?

Yesterday I did 2 zone tests by dipping half the exposed plate into 5% metabisulfite alone for a minute and then carefully rinsed all the metabisulfite off without letting it get on the dry section , then I put the whole plate in water to remove all dichromate. Now I was using a dye initiated system so my dichromate level was roughly a tenth of yours, but even so in the final processed plate there was sufficient general hardening in the metabisulfite/sulfite treated half to produce a very big difference and absence of milkiness.

The untreated water half was dazzlingly bright but it was buried in too much milk. There needed to be some hardening bias there and a careful prevention of water temperature going above about 20C.

So with so much dichromate around in your formulation for getting green sensitivity , you should be able to have more than enough of the bias hardening needed, so you should not need to use the aluminum sulfate hardener as well , provided you have enough metabisulfite/sulfite around to deal with the dichromate. Maybe that's the best solution, because the test object could be reproduced easily by different holographers. For example, say the test object is a flat piece of aluminum, sprayed with a specific brandof flat white.

Of course this will depolarize the light (if an opaque coating of paint is applied to it).

If a reflective, diffusing, polarization-maintaining surface is needed, then the aluminum can be abraded on the surface. I use abrasive etching in a sandblasting cabinet with 600 grit silicon carbide. A similar effect can be had by simply using sandpaper on it. The rougher the grit (and the more forcefully the sandpapering, or blasting, is done), the more polarization will be lost. There are an infinite number of polarization-preserving levels when the surface of the aluminum is abraded in this way. I have used a "light touch" with 600 grit silicon carbide in a blasting cabinet, and a great deal of the polarization is preserved, but not all.

In anodizing, the surface of the metal is chemically etched to produce a similar result.

There are also other methods of doing the same thing by applying translucent coatings to a reflective surface.

A mirror of course would maintain all the polarization. I have not been able to find a surface treatment which is reflective, essentially diffuse, and which keeps all of the polarization intact.
