Tom B. Sun Apr 01, 2007 3:13 am #2
Saxby, in "Practical Holography" 3rd ed. p. 256 has a very short entry on true color transmission holos. It's possible in theory and has been done (the example he cites was made by our very own Kaveh Bazargan) but there are 3 difficulties listed: 1) must be lit by light with same wavelengths used to record 2) loss of fringe contast due to very similar overlapping fringe patterns and 3) crosstalk between fringes from different colors leading to spurious images. The only way to reduce 2 & 3 is, as you guessed, using drastically different reference angles which leads to the further problem of aligning the 3 lasers in exactly their original positions for reconstruction.

I wonder if narrow bandpass filtered white light or LED sources would be OK for reconstruction so as not to tie up expensive lasers for the display? Still not cheap, though.
Sergio Sun Apr 01, 2007 12:52 pm #3
Pennington and Lin demonstrated the first multicolour transmission hologram using two wavelengths, 488 and 633nm. The reference-beam angles were of 90°. The beams are mixed in-line.

Inside the holographic emulsion, the Bragg angle is calculated ~28°. It does not vary very much so the using of "drastically different reference angles" here would not so useful for cross modulation control!.

The Bragg deviation DeltaLambda (DL) is related directly with emulsion thickness L, using a 12um 649F emulsion the computed DL = 21nm. The two colours at 633 - 488nm = 144nm are well defined and separated and no cross talk will appear on 12um emulsion, also the 633-532nm = 101 may not present problems for this setup. With thicker emulsions, presently available only to DCG or photopolymers is possible recording multi-colour H1 masters with a "white-light laser beam" with no problem.

> Ed Wesly: US Patent # 3,894,787, awarded July 15, 1975, to none other than Emmett Leith and Juris Upatnieks shows a reference beam from below, the right side, and another one from right and above for a transmission hologram set up.The funny thing is that they don't prescribe any laser lambdas, but label the 3 beams as red, blue and yellow!

I did this in the lab once, just for fun. You have to be very careful about all optics, alignment, etc. I did it with 3 wavelengths (476, 514 and 633). It takes even more patience than making a single color hologram! It took a week to set up the table, but then I made several color holograms in quick succession, with a single exposure for all. Here is the table layout:

![Image](http://www.bazargan.org/color-transmission.jpg)

At the top is a Spectra Physics Argon laser, giving out 476. To the right is a Lexel Argon, giving 514. middle top, almost invisible is a tube HeNe for 633. For object illumination, all beams go to bottom left mirror, and white light comes out of the microscope objective. To do away with the spatial filtering of the three beams together, I just put a diffusor , and tweaked the beams to get a uniform white illumination.

The three beams come in at three different angles for reference. I separated blue and green to come from opposite sides, to minimize risk of cross talk. The blue and red come from the same side, but the angles are such that they reinforce the Bragg selection, so it is less likely to give cross talk. (There was no cross talk).

Recording materials are Agfa 8E56 and 8E75, sandwiched emulsion to emulsion.

I remember Benton was visiting me when this was still up. His comment was: Well, someone had to do it!

Here are some resultant images:

This is a color chart. Not a standard one, just one cut out of a book or magazine. Original on the left, hologram on the right.

![Image](http://www.bazargan.org/Color-chart-lowres.jpg)

Here are the results of the various combinations of wavelengths. The faces are pink, but were yellow in the original. This is a good example of choosing the wrong green. 528 from Argon would have been better, but power was too low. Funny anecdote is that if I had not drawn attention to the originally yellow faces, it might have been hailed as a great reproduction of colors. But going to pains to point this out, and the reasons for it, I am known as the guy who can't make yellow!!

![Image](http://www.bazargan.org/lego-color-lowres.jpg)

Here are some stamps from the Axis of Evil. Orignals on the left.

![Image](http://www.bazargan.org/stamps-lowres.jpg)

Now notice that the yellows are much better here. This shows that the printed colors have a different spectral reflectivity, and just happen to look better with those wavelengths.

From K.S. Pennington and L.H. Lin, "Multicolor Wavefront Reconstruction," Appl. Phys. Lett. 7, 56 (1965).

They did not separate the beams due the Kodak 649F 15um emulsion thickness providing phase separation, Kaveh did it with his outstanding setup demonstration on how to use a thin (7um) Agfa film for multicolour holography. Well done.

They computed from Kogelnik theory to figure out the deviation from Bragg wavelength (that wavelength band that will not interfere with other superimposed holograms on reconstruction)

Ksi=-(DL/Ln)(tanT0)(2pi.n0/Ln)T(sinT0) (Transmission)

Where Ksi is a parameter that corresponds to ~3

DL = Deviation from Bragg wavelength, the bandwidth for each reply laser wavelength.

Ln = Illuminating wavelength.

T0 = Bragg angle into the emulsion ~28° for this setup

pi = 3,1416...

n0 = refractive index of recording medium (gelatin)

T = Emulsion thickness

With different thickness values, you can compute the minimum value for no cross talk with your transmission hologram, with all colour reference beams coming from the same angle.

Also is important correct the each light energy value based on our visualized luminous efficiency of monochromatic light with a unit called lumens, so we can compute the correct power relation between wavelengths, possible getting the correct yellow colour.
