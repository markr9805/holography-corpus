Probably the cheapest way to drive these would be to find a wall-wart (transformer) that was based on a switching design. More and more of them are switching designs these days. Switchers typically have both a voltage set point and a current limit. Some have a shutdown triggered by current. If one can be found with a current limit rather than shutdown then by modifying the current limit circuit you should be able to have it drive the LED at the correct current. This modification could be as simple as changing the current sense resistor.

> Joe: I do know that a diode bridge can change AC to DC, so I'm wondering if this is a possibility. What I want to do is change the wall-plug 120V AC to about 4V DC at 1 amp. For consumer electronics this is done with a transformer, so there are probably very good reasons why this is used, and also why the higher-current ones need a physically larger size.

Traditionally, most wall-warts (those little black plug-in power supply things) used a small transformer to reduce the voltage, a bridge rectifier consisting of 4 diodes to convert to DC and a capacitor and voltage regulator to filter the output. These become large and heavy when high-current outputs are needed, mostly due to the size of the transformer needed.

More modern supplies (those used for laptops for example) use switching converters which work differently. They directly rectify the 120VAC to produce high voltage DC, then reduce the voltage by switching it on and off very quickly at a variable pulse width into an inductor and average the voltage by filtering to produce smooth DC. These are much lighter and smaller than non-switching supplies because transformers and capacitors working at the high switching frequenciies can be made much smaller than they would have to be to work at 60 Hz line frequency. They are also much more efficient, generating less waste heat, which also reduces size since minimal cooling is needed. This is what's built in to the OptiLeds devices.

Ron Michael Sun Feb 18, 2007 8:22 pm #24
I haven't looked at the specs on these leds. However, to power leds or laser diodes you have to have a voltage just greater than the junction voltage. So for example if the junction voltage is 1.8volts and you use two in series 2*1.8=3.6 volts then a 4 or 5volt power supply would work best to reduce heat lost in the resistor limiter. In this case if you used a 5volt supply you have to drop 1.4volts at 1amp so you need approx 1.5 ohm 3watt resistor. You should overrate your resistor wattage for safety. I would recommend that you find the junction voltage needed and subtract that from the lowest supply voltage you can get that is capable of suppling your 1amp or 1.5amp need. Small SWPS (switching power supplies) offer good current in the low 3.3volt,5volt or 12volt and it's easy to find small cheap ones. For example if you wanted to power a gallery of pictures, you could string them in parallel or series depending on matching the swps capability.
