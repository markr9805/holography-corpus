Ron Michael Tue Jun 06, 2006 12:37 am #4
John,

It takes guts to admit mistakes, I worked on electronics for over 30 years. I am able to write this as I too can say I'm a lucky one also and have survived a few electrical mistakes. Some rules have saved my life. Luck also, but I don't want to depend on luck. As tempting as it may be to build some things, some things are better off not being built if you are not sure of what you are doing. I'd rather burn out 10 nichia blue laser diodes at once (yes I'd probably cry) than take a hit from leakage of a HV source. Making mistakes starts with a lack of knowledge, careless approach, indifference to safety, improper design, lack of electrical/chassis grounding or even having a chassis and barriers to protect you or lack of safety or proper test equipment etc. I'm constantly surprised by people who say they know what they are doing and then want to hook an oscilloscope direct to HV. [http://www.ecmweb.com/mag/electric_usin ... index.html](http://www.ecmweb.com/mag/electric_using_right_voltage/index.html) Even with the best intentions and precautions and training, mistakes still happen to us.

One rule is your life is not cheap, so don't be motivated to doing things on the cheap, anything doing worthwile that envolves hazards are best done prepared and properly equipped which generally means spending more that you originally estimated. With dangerous electricals, cheap is the path to shortcuts which leads you to the darkside "the lights out non-living". As a helicopter pilot, I never say "o, that $10,000 gear or shaft is too expensive, lets use that old worn or rusty one, its not worth it". I figure I have over 10,000 ways to kill myself and I hope my stupidity won't be one of them.

I say all this with the best intentions and sincerity, just please be careful and make education, safety and it's cost the first choice in any project .
