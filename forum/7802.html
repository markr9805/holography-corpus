Joe Farina Wed Apr 08, 2009 10:28 am #15
> Danny Bee: i think the room is way too noisy

There is no "one way" to do DCG. I think he should have a number of options available, before finding a method that works. There are too many variables involved to try and lock the process into a "single" method.

The light bulb method was described by Rallison as an alternative to using fixer. After exposing and dark reaction, expose the DCG to a 100W frosted light bulb (at about 6 inches from the emulsion) for a minute or two, maybe more, maybe less. Not sunlight, not a halogen bulb, not a fluorescent light, not a LED, not a laser, but a standard, old-fashioned, frosted, 100W light bulb. This will have a reducing effect on the DCG and is analogous to fixing. If the final hologram is milky, expose to the light bulb for a longer time period for the next one, to increase hardening/reduction.

It does sound as if the gelatin is very soft. My suggestion is to let the film age at room temperature for 24 hours (not in a sealed box so the moisture can escape also) to get it harder before you shoot. Then bracket exposure times and bracket post exposure light bulb hardening (Dutchelm05). By bracketing I mean cover all but 1/4 of the plate and shoot at a time, then cover that 1/4 and leave open another 1/4 while covering the rest. Do a longer exposure. Repeat for 4 times. You should have 4 strips of 4 differnt exposures. Then when you expose to the light bulb do the same thing but leave the 1/4 uncovered at 90 degrees to the strips of the exposures and repeat for the other 3 1/4's. This will give you 16 different exposure/post hardening combinations that are processed exactly the same.

Also, very important, as previously stated, you probably misread the water washing of AmDi out of the plate. Wash unil no yellow is visible, then 15 seconds, not 15 seconds. I doubt seriously the AmDi comes out in 15 seconds, especially if doing a post exposure hardening.

On a final note on safety, AmDi is very nasty stuff. And if you are not fixing it (reducing it to CrIII) then it will still be very nasty. So that water rinse you are using should be bottled and taken to a Hazardous waste facility. I do not recomment washing is down the drain.

Joe Farina Thu Sep 24, 2009 5:40 pm #42
The way I see it, the dark reaction is doing exactly the same thing as exposure to laser light. So yes, harder in the exposed areas, which is going to give better modulation to the exposed areas versus the unexposed areas, and quite possibly higher diffraction efficiency. Of course we have to consider overall or bias hardening which is different. Bias hardening would happen during the storage of unexposed DCG, and it would keep on happening after exposure as well. But the dark reaction, as I see it, should be clearly distinguished from this. The dark reaction is really just allowing more of the light-exposed Cr6 (which might be considered Cr5) to reduce itself to Cr3. The benefit of allowing the dark reaction to do its job (rather than exposure) is the fact that there is nothing to disrupt the fringe structure while this is happening (like table/object movement, drafts, temperature changes, etc.).

The bias hardening, or hardening due to ageing alone, could be considered slow. But the dark reaction of the exposed areas would be much faster.

So to make a long story short, I would expect the exact same effect for both increased exposure and increased dark reaction. The only caution would be that bias hardening is also going on at the same time (but that should be slow compared to what's going on with the dark reaction of the exposed areas).

As you know, Tony, to get a very clear picture of cause and effect is very difficult in DCG.

Ok, so let’s take a look at this dark reaction thingy. As per Jeff B’s research while perfecting the G307 formula, he found that there have been studies on the CrVI to CrIII. Read the Discussion part of that paper.

Those studies indicated that during exposure, the CrVI converts very rapidly to CrV as compared to the CrV converting to CrIII in the exposed region. So we have a very high population of CrV in the exposed region that is bound to the gelatin and a very high population of CrVI in the unexposed region that is not bound to the gelatin.

Dark Reaction

During dark reaction, hardly anything happens in the unexposed regions. Almost all the Cr is in the CrVI state. The CrVI just sits there for the most part. In the exposed regions which contain a high concentration of CrV and a small population of CrIII (needed for crosslinking), the CrV takes a long time to be reduced to CrIII solely from dark reaction. In fact studies show it can remain in the CrV for hours before finally being reduced to CrIII. Thus my statement earlier that I didn’t think the dark reaction thingy for 5 minutes actually helps anything at all.

Fixing

Now during fixing in the unexposed regions, the CrVI is being reduced to CrV but this CrV is not bound to the gelatin and thus gets washed out before being reduced to CrIII needed for crosslinking. In the exposed regions the CrV is bound to the gelatin and when the fixer reduces the CrV to CrIII it crosslinks the gelatin. So in comparing the two spots, unexposed and exposed, the CrVI gets reduced to CrIII much more slowly then the CrV to CrIII, as if it didn’t we would lose our differences in crosslinking.

Discussion

So, you can see what I base my decision in stating I do not think that Dark Reaction really does anything if only done for 5 minutes. But I will have to say to really improve the fringe contrast there may be a way to remove the CrVI completely before being put in the fixer as I am sure a very small percentage of CrVI in the unexposed areas does get reduced to CrIII and does cause a small amount of crosslinking in those areas.

Could is be as simple as washing the DCG in water prior to fixing? This would remove all Cr that is not bound, basically all the CrVI which is mainly in the unexposed areas. Then absolutely no crosslinking would happen in the unexposed regions. Meanwhile the water wash would not affect the bound CrV and CrIII in the exposed areas.

Is there something else going on with Dark Reaction that I am missing that does happen in 5 or 10 minutes?
