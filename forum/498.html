I thought I'd carry over Danny's call for new emulsions to a new topic, since it has nothing to do with the green sensitivity issue.

At any rate, Danny, the problem is that holography has stricter requirements than photography. Tne gamma for the film has to be above 2, and modern emulsions are about 3 to 4. Also, the developer has to be extrememly high contrast, ie also high gamma. The problem with the collodion, tintypes and the platinum and palladium developers seems to be that theye were intended to create a certain grey-scale image. This was obviously highly valued for black and white photographers, but this also means that the gamma of the films and developers must have been low, or else you cannot get the particular grey scale lighting effects that they were looking for. It may be possible to load the collodion with more silver and smaller silver - that is, increase density and reduce grain size - but you may have to watch for internal scattering within the collodion matrix. As the grain size is reduced, the scattering of laser light on these small grains increases quite a bit. Also, the process of making collodion is quite complex (which is why it apparently fell into disfavour) with a lot of chemicals that today may be expensive if at all avavilable. I have an old book ("Photographic Facts and Formulas" by E. J. Wall, 1924) which states a formula for red tones (apparently called "Red Chalk" or "Bartolozzi colours") as follows:

Uranium Nitrate..........................2gms

Thiosinamine.............................10gs

Water......................................1 l

I have a pretty good relationship with my chemical suppliers, but I think they might balk if I asked for Uranium Nitrate!

Having said that, new matrices for silver may well be a possibility for new polymers. After all, gelatin was considered pretty creative at the time. Wall's book has a chapter entitled " Gelatino Chloride or Printing Out Papers" so there must have been an intermediate period where they weren't quite sure of gelatin and wanted to hedge their bets by adding gelatin to the collodion.

So, my suggestion would be that you try silver with various, different polymer matrices, keeping the silver size and density as close to constant as possible (so the silver does not become a variable). Then find a way to objectively determine some sort of quality criterion; may be shoot a transmsission grating with exactly the same angles at exactly the same wavelength and measure the de. Then you make a table listing polymer matrix and the de of the resulting hologram next to it. Another thing you may have to watch out for is the density of the matrix. If this is too dense, it may hinder the passage of the developer and the developer action will fade as the developer penetrates into the matrix - this should be especially true of thick emulsions (so you may want to throw that into the results). You might submit this to Photonics West or the next ISDH (assuming that it's too late for this one).

Dinesh Sat Mar 17, 2012 9:09 am #5
> Martin: I guess you meant to say the opposite: the smaller the grains the lower the scattering.

Well yes and no. As the grain size decreases, the scattering goes from Mie scattering to Rayleigh scattering. Mie scattering is generally pretty equal across the spectrum and applies to large structures approaching 100's of nm. I assume that the grain size of photographic emulsions, especially these old emulsions, were in the 100s of nm. Rayleigh scattering applies to smaller, molecular sized objects in the 10's of nm, which is the grain size Danny would be aiming for to make a holographic emulsion. Rayleigh scattering is proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength, so the greens and blues are much more scattered then the reds. Since most of us seem to be working in green blue (presumably due to the cheaper green blue lasers such as the 315), the smaller the grain size, the greater is the Rayleigh scattering component and the more is green/blue scattered. However, as you say, as the grain size is further reduced, the scattering is also reduced and in the limit of zero grain size there's no scattering at all.
