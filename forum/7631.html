JohnFP Thu Dec 11, 2008 10:21 pm #3
I seem to see this in younger film, exposed and processed perfectly.

No one really asked and I used to ponder it quite often but have not lately. I will have to start thinking about it again. The key for a really really blinding bright DCG is to get the two index of refractions (exposed and unexposed areas) to be air and crosslinked gelatin, not two different areas of differenly crosslink gelatin (one with a relative lower index of refraction compared to the other area).

So lets talk this through. I seem to feel this happens when there is very contrasty fringes, exposure on the nut to get maximum crosslinking in the exposed areas and minimal in the unexposed area and processing to remove some uncrosslinkin gelatin and force the water out of the un-crosslinked sites in which then there is an air void left. So we have to think about how to do that. A soft gelatin (low bloom or young) is surely a key. Also, we would like amino chains small (short) enough that there is less likely one that is in the unexposed area has part of itself dangling into the exposed area then thusly would not be able to be washed away becuse it was attached at its end. Long chains would surely do this more frequently then short chains. (for anther topic; How do we arrange the amino molecuels such that the lay parallel to our fringe planes? Magnet? Cintrifically? How do we shorten them? Possibley lower bloom which gives us less crosslinked to start thus short "trains" of molecules?

The gel has to be soft enough so that after exposure and after fixing the unexposed area gives up its uncrosslinked gelatin and there is mosly only water in that area. Hummm, a young film with a very good exposure... to try to (after fixing) use up all free amino chains (crosslink them) in the exposed site and try not to latently crosslink any amino chains in the unexposed site with the fixer. (PS: this was one of the reasons I thought Jeff's idea of splitting the fixer into it separate usable parts would be greatly benificial, that is, hardening (crosslinking) and a recuction of the Cr.) We have to go back to the proofs and studies that show how CrVI reduced to CrV then to CRIII as stated in out paper and proved by other independent souces also.

With light CrVI goes to CrV very quickly but CrIII very slowly. So at the end of our exposure we have some residual CrVI, probably lots of CrV and minimal CrIII in the exposed areas. In the unexposed area we have lots of CrVI, minimal CrV and probably no or very very little CrIII. It is the CrIII that is used for crosslinking. Now we fix. The fixer must act much more quickly on reducting the CrV to CrIII then it does in reducing CrVI to CrV or we would reduce all the CrVI in the unexpose area and not get those differences of crosslinking. So in the exposed area we want to fix all the CrV to CrIII but not fix it to long as to continue the reducing of CrVI to CrV and ultimately CrIII in the unexposed area.

Now if we can use the Sodium Metabisulfite first to get all CrV to CrIII rapidly, then wash in Alum to harden the gelatin just enough so we can use a higher temperature alcohol process. Then We should have the criteria for the air/gel interface of fringes instead of the gel/gel interface with two different index of refractions.

We not only have to process the exposed areas to get maximum crosslinking but we have to process the unexposed areas to get minimal crosslinking and maximum material removal which then is replaced by air.

JohnFP Fri Dec 12, 2008 11:36 am #5
Yes Jean, I believe the warmer water bath helps to disolve out the uncrosslinked gelatin.

Gelatin is soluble at slightly lower then our body temperature, so having that water just about at 90-95F (32-35C) will insure that the uncrosslinked gelatin disolves out. But depending on the grade of the gelatin, this my be a little too high. We want to disolve out the uncrosslinked gelatin but we dont want to disolve the crosslinked areas.

JohnFP Fri Dec 12, 2008 4:50 pm #13
Well, normally I just use the water coming out of the Ion laser. It is warm, probably about 75 - 85F depending on the power that I run the laser at. If I want to use warmer water then my laser is putting out, I wash the hologram with the laser water for 3 or 4 minutes then put in a heated tray of water for 3 or 4 minutes.

AmDi when disolved in gelatin has the chromium in the CrVI state. Yes this will simply disolve out as can be seen by putting a mature plate in a tray of water. The water will turn yellow. This is not bonded to the gelatin in any way. YOu can also see this in the fixer. The CrVI is actually reduced all the way to CRIII and still precipitates out as that blue stuff. Obviously not bonded to the gelatin. Now when the CR is exposed to light and reduces to CrV, this IS bonded to the gelatin. And when the CrV is reduced to CRIII it bonds to two amino chains (not just one) to form additional crosslinking. This is what I understand from mostly Jeff's comment and deductions in the G307 paper in addition to first hand observations.

The less bonding (crosslinking) the more the material can be disolved and washed away.
