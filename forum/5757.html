cubfan Sun Aug 20, 2006 2:03 am #1
I have questions about the potential/limitations for use of holographic images integrated into an outdoor display - I would greatly appreciate insight or referral to people who have experience with this.

a. Is it practical to use holographic images outdoors? (I gather that some kind of directed light source is needed and that ambient sunlight may make a holograph hard to view). Are you aware of any permanent outdoor displays?

b. I can imagine a holographic image being wrapped around a column in a display -- is that feasible? Can holograms be mounted such that a person could walk around the display and still have the holographic effect?

c. Can holograms be printed on transparent substrates?
Tony Sun Aug 20, 2006 11:10 am #2
I'm not an expert, but I can answer the questions-

a. Sunlight is great for viewing holograms, on a clear day. However, if it's cloudy, viewing can be very poor.

b. You could wrap a hologram around a pole, but viewing would only be possible on the side illuminated directly by the sun. On the side in shadow, viewing would be poor to nil.

c. Yes, you can make holograms on transparent material. I'm not familiar with the usual choices for mass production though.
BobH Sun Aug 20, 2006 3:01 pm #3
I think sunlight is a terrible source for illuminating a permanently mounted pictorial hologram. It's way too broadband, the IR and UV in it kills many materials over time, it's in constant motion (which means a constantly distorted image), its intensity is constantly changing, it's an extended source, and it's off half the time. Sure, holograms look OK when you hold them at some weird angle to briefly look at them in the sun, but they really don't like it.

Holograms also like to be flat. It's really difficult to make them curved accurately for reconstruction on the same curved surface, especially in production. the cylindrical hologram recording techniques of Jeong and Cross are the exceptions of course, but they require the light source to be inside the cylinder.
MichaelH Sun Aug 20, 2006 3:05 pm #4
> cubfan: a. Is it practical to use holographic images outdoors? (I gather that some kind of directed light source is needed and that ambient sunlight may make a holograph hard to view). Are you aware of any permanent outdoor displays?

I don't have any links handy but there are a few permanent outdoor exhibits. The quality control requirements go up quite a lot if you intend to expose your holograms to sunlight for any length of time.

UV and heat both degrade silver-halide holograms. You can minimize the effects but the jury's still out regarding permanent solutions.

Other forms (DCG, SHSG) behave differently but still have their own problems.

Sunlight can make a hologram hard to view although that's ultimately dependent on the background used.

For example, the following hologram isn't very visible because the background is too bright (and the camera focused on the tree).

![Image](http://holography.dragonseye.com/images/present/july2003/oghamplate1.jpg)

Showing the hologram against a dark background would fix both problems.

> cubfan: b. I can imagine a holographic image being wrapped around a column in a display -- is that feasible? Can holograms be mounted such that a person could walk around the display and still have the holographic effect?

Cylindrical transmission holograms are quite good at this. It's difficult to make a bright reflection hologram in cylinder form although I imagine that purely digital holograms could accomplish this with ease.

> cubfan: c. Can holograms be printed on transparent substrates?

By their nature, holograms are made on transparent substrates. Reproductions can be made on opaque or transparent mediums.

BobH Sun Aug 20, 2006 5:47 pm #7
> For all practical purposes sunlight is a point source

No way! It's half a degree, which will certainly limit the depth of an image. Compared to a 2'x4' fluorescent fixture in an office ceiling, it's a point. Lighting designers and marketing people will call it a point source, but in a technical forum like this I think a more accurate definition is a good idea.

Also, the extent is constantly changing for the worse as haze and/or clouds move through the beam.

> doesn't destroy holograms if you take care to process them with that kind of viewing in mind

Theoretically, maybe, but can anyone cite an example of a pictorial hologram that's been on display, sucessfully, in the sun, for any significant length of time? Achieving and/or maintaining professional display quality? Dupont's material should be able to do it, and I suppose Zebra Imaging would be the ones to know.

> won't blind you if you make the efficiency of the hologram appropriate to the reconstruction light.

But the reconstruction light intensity is constantly changing. A hologram made dim to be properly balanced in maximum sunlight will be terribly dim and fuzzy when some haze or clouds get in the way.

I'm not saying that you can't use the sun to view a hologram, I'm saying that it's not a good idea to display a hologram with the sun as its source. Unless the whole installation is art, of course. 8)
walschuler Thu Aug 24, 2006 5:05 am #8
In general I find viewing holos in sunlight gives very good results, if as mentioned above there is a black backgrond for the holo, or it is a very efficient image.

The question of what will last depends on whether the processed emulsion will print out, mostly due to blue and UV exposure, and whether the emulsion will fail due to moisture, pollution, heat, or thermal cycling. I think most of us do not fix our images. I usually redevelop in ascorbic acid, which gives some protection against print-out, but I estimate that several days of exposure to direct sunlight will start to fade the image. If fixed, this should not be a problem, but conventional fix shrinks the emulsion and that of course shifts the color to the blue in reflection holograms. Transmission holos should be immune to that and thus fixable. Perhaps one could re-expand the emulsion with a bath in sorbitol. Based on some images I have that were done that way I suspect the sorbitol evaporates or combines with humidity and gradually (over say 6 years) leaves the emulsion, sending it part way back to its original green color from pink. This loss might be prevented if the image is sealed behind glass.

Sealing behind glass will of course filter UV and deep blue and should slow the printing-out considerably. For DCG sealing off is essential to prevent contact with humidity. DCG should not print out. Photopolymers should also be light stable, though embrittlement from heat might be possible over the long term. I have not seen any such images exposed to sunlight over a long time.

Joe Farina Thu Aug 24, 2006 11:56 am #11
From the standpoint of permanence, the most impressive holograms (HOEs actually) that I know about were described in Holography News, December 1996/January 1997 in an article called "Billund's Holo-Sculptural Landmark."

To quote from the article:

"The town of Billund in Denmark...now boasts a holographic public art sculpture, sited in front of the City Hall. The sculpture Sfirot (Sapphire) by artist Frithioff Johansen was commissioned by the city.

Sfirot is an 11m high steel construction with a 40 tonne granite rock resting on the top; on the rock's underside water drips from an anchor-chain into a ten-sided basin of polished black granite. The lower part of the rock is faceted, and 10 polished spherical hexagonal granite mirrors reflect light from ten holographic optical elements (HOEs) built in the basin wall underwater. The HOEs, produced by Ibsen Micro Structures, are recorded in photoresist and etched into the fused silica substrate."
