In about two weeks, I get to teach a class of 10-20 high school kids the basics of holography. The section will be taught over two consecutive class periods, with at least one demo (ideally, I'd get everyone in the darkroom and have them make their own using Saxby's sand-bucket-and-lab-stool technique).

Any suggestions? Since few, if any, will have had a proper physics class, I'm going to skip most of the theory and focus heavily on the application. That said, I'm not sure just how much I can expect to fit in -- if I try to cram "Practical Holography" down their throats, it will very quickly become naptime (or paper airplane folding class).
Stendec_Stan Tue Mar 01, 2005 10:16 pm #2
You're right, making it into a science lesson might not be the best way to go, I'd be aiming to make it more of a fun arty project, just show them what they need to know to make a hologram and let those who want to know more know where they can find the information.

Making holograms takes time, you need to think carefuly about whether or not you'll have enough time for everyone to make their own hologram, which would be the best case scenario, failing that you can split them into small groups.

White light reflection would be best, that way they can display their results in the class room, or better yet, take them home with them.

less time in the classroom means more time in the dark room so try to have photocopied diagrams/ instructions ready so you can hit the ground running. Get as much worked out before hand as you can.

Good luck Eric, I'm interested to learn how this goes for you.

Stan
BobH Tue Mar 01, 2005 10:36 pm #3
Don't try making any holograms! Talk about the technique of holography, and how it's unique amongst all the other 3-D techniques. Bring in as many samples of different types of real holograms, and explain the set-ups used to make them. Bring in a hologram making set-up (like you suggested), and go over the important things. Be able to demonstrate interference fringes with the set-up, and use them to show the stability needed to make a hologram.

Again, DO NOT try making one. You'll only fail and bore the socks off them while you struggle. Aim to make them able to recognize a real hologram when they see it.

Dinesh Tue Mar 01, 2005 11:39 pm #5
We've done this a number of times. The students were adults, mostly non-technical, doing it as part of a Coimmunity Outreach that the local college holds. These are our experiences. First a pre-class procedure: the day before the class I make a laser transmission H1 with PFG-01 and a HeNe. In fact I make two or three, just to be sure that it's repeatable.

The class is set over two days.

Day 1:

Joy talks on 3D vision in humans. Accomodation, vergence, disparity, that sort of thing. Then she talks about these techniques and their use in art from Renaissance Italy to modern day. She then gives an overview of why holography is a different form of 3D than any other; parallax, the use of all three visual cues etc.

I then give a brief presentation of the history of holography from Gabor's attempt at microscopes through Denisyuk, Leith-Upatniks, Benton, Fred et al with the San Fransisco group, Dan Schweitzer and the New York group and the world expansion of display at roughly late 70's, 80's. I then give a presentation of the properties of light: waves, (I'm going to have remember a piece of clothesline ), incoherent light vs coherent, coherence length. Then I go to interference, diffraction, reference beam, object beam etc. I stay away from the actual working of the laser, unlike Tony. I feel that the laser is an instrument like a lens or mirror. So I briefly go over why a laser is coherent and basic laser safety. All of this takes about an hour to an hour and a half. Then they go to the table and Joy sets up the table for a two beam laser transmission. As she sets up I talk it through: "She's now setting up the object beam. Note that we're lighting the object from both directions so we need a beamsplitter to split the object into two beams. Now she's setting up the reference. Note she's carefully measuring the angle that the reference hits the plate. This is important. Now we pinhole (<brief explanation of laser beam purity>)" etc etc. I then have them watch while I shoot one, develop it and place it back into the plateholder. I then have each one shoot an H1 and develop it by themselves. More talk as they do this: "Keep rocking the tray. Note how it goes dark slowly. Too fast and you've overexposed, too slow and you've underexposed. Note also how the dark spreads. If it spreads from the center outwards your beam is well adjusted, if it spreads from an edge, your beam is off. Take it out and look at the safelight through it. Now look at the safelight directly. This gives you an idea of how dark to go. Note how long it takes to bleach" etc etc. Oh yeah,a brief talk on chemical safety first. By now it's 5pm and free for all time.

Day 2:

Brief talk on reflection holograms. Reminder that last time the holograms were only visible in laser light. We're at roughly 1963. Now along comes Yuri Denisyuk (1963) and white light holograms. Brief talk on Bragg planes and why they allow white light holograms. Now we go to the table and, oh I forgot, they each have their own object, anyway, back to the table and Joy helps them set up their object as a single beam Denisyuk. I talk them through the importance of lighting not only to maximise brightness but from an aesthetic point of view. "Note that if you light it like that, the shadow of the object falls right across the most important part of your image. Also, make sure that the light reconstructing the image from above does not cause a sideways shadow - this is discomforting" etc etc. Then I shoot a Denisyuk after which each one sets up (with a little help from Joy), shoots a Denisyuk of their own object and develops it themselves. More talk. "Note how the plate needs to be much darker this time..." This takes about 3 hours. Then, we set up the H1 we did the previous time and set up for a split beam H1/H2 reflection. Again more talk as Joy sets up the table. "Note that this time the reference comes from the other side. Nots also the angle. We turn the plate over and <as Joy does this with a diffusion glass> we note that we can pass a plate right through the volume of the object. We choose the best image plane and place our H2 plate there." Then Joy varies the beamsplitter as I talk it through. "The beam ratio is very important. Nothing is more damaging to a hologram than a bad ratio." Then we measure the power at the plate, determine the exposure, set the shutter and I shoot one and develop it <by now they can pretty much develop it themselves without me>. Then they each shoot and develop one. By now it's almost time to go, so I get them together and ask if there are any questions, points they're unclear on.

Sometimes, they don't really care too much for the theory and want to get to the table quickly, so i cut the theory short. Sometimes they swallow the theory whole and we end up talking for longer than I intend. Sometimes they ask question well off subject and I try to go a little way into it, but discipline myself not to go too far. One student last time wanted to know how X-ray machines worked and why X-rays allowed you to see bones. Another was fascinated by firstly the developing action then went into a lot of question on redox reactions and silver grains and photographic development theory. One peron was very interested in phase realtions, which was to the point so I did go into it at length in that particular clkass. One person was pretty tight lipped throughout it all. Suddenly he saw his object come up in the Denisyuk and broke into this really wide grin!

Dinesh Wed Mar 02, 2005 2:18 pm #8
All my students were adults so we covered "what they really are" and "how to use them" in the theory section as well as the "chat session" during processing or settling times.

That said, I've never taught holography to high schoolers (I assume by that approx 15 years old). However, I have taught physics and maths to this age group, but in a voluntary, private teaching level. In this case, where they voluntarily come to learn, I found there was a genuine interest. Their minds pick up a lot faster that adults (obviously?) and they see where something is going far faster than adults. Also, they were incredibly curious. Funnily, I saw that when I explained even fairly complex mathematical concepts in the context of physical phenomena the girls picked it up faster while if I fixated on the methodolgy the boys picked it up faster. Maybe holography, being this famous mixture of art and science, can be taught in different ways to the different sexes - an intuitive picture for the girls and a methodology for the boys. I have a theory that girls think inductively and boys think deductively. While any proposition can be argued from a deductive or inductive viewpoint, their method of learning seems to be different.

I see the tragedy that in 10 years time, at 25, they're going to be more pre-occupied with picking up girls and getting laid then they are now.

Eric I would strongly recommended doing the upturned sheet of glass method rather than the sandbox/cattlitter one which I think is an awful system.

I have been able to make 18 passable Dennisyuk holos on 2.5” square BB640 plates plates in less than an hour and a half for 20 students (2 only had to go away disappointed.)

Some of the holos were exceptionally good even inspite of the rough and ready aspects.

I bought a sheet of 6mm thick glass about 1 metre square and had it supported on

the legs of a table resting on its side on top of a workbench in the same manner as shown in the diagram of a chair on Colin Davidson’s website at the bottom of his page:-

[http://cabd0.tripod.com/holograms/id5.html](http://cabd0.tripod.com/holograms/id5.html)

About 6 students could have their own suitably stable objects holographed at the same time. This was done by having the objects placed firmly ontop of the big clean glass sheet in 6 separated positions .

I was using at the time a 4 mw HeNe laser with an ordinary 1 cm focal length convex lens stuck on the end with sticking gum so I could safely wave it around by hand..

Each student could examine what their object looked like under laser light by viewing up from under the glass.

Once the arrangement was made OK by them .I then turned the lights out and under green safelight very carefully slid a small BB640 plate under each object (some plates were cut in 2 to economize for small objects such as jewellery) .

With each batch of students I allowed around 10 minutes for the objects to stabilize

on their plates, and gave a holochat meanwhile. (Most students agreed to go along with idea of resting the objects on the BB640 glass side rather than the emulsion side so that they could later watch replay colour change by condensing breath on the image.)

I then exposed each object on its glass plate individually from underneath the big glass by just holding the laser so that it had a spread beam diam of around 3cm. for a second or 2 on each object. Being careful of course not to touch the table at all during exposure. (The exposure angles of course varied a lot depending on where the objects were positioned. Flat coin type objects were situated to near perpendicular position if possible.)

I collected up the exposed plates put them in a box and set up the next batch of students with their objects.

At the end of this I had all the exposed plates put in a toast rack type frame (from Slavich) and processed the whole lot together in 3 plastic buckets.

Yes I had prepared beforehand by doing rehearsals to establish exposure and dev. time and yes I had pre-swollen and activated the plates in 7% TEA a day or two before. The best results came from objects that benefited from having an anodized aluminum pie dish put over them. (better than a depolarizing white pie dish).

A striking result came from a small white real flower on a stem which came out as a

fine 3 dimensional black “shadowgram” against the pie dish walls.

Using a laserpointer to do this you would I think have to be careful to not let its temperature change by only holding it by a small vice and not by hand .

The bar of light you get can allow a strip of say 3 objects to be done at once.

Anyway good luck Eric and may it generate at least a few new holonuts for the future.

Jeff

Actually if I had to do this for classes on a proper regular basis in less than an hour say I would arrange a big enough glass top table so that you can have all 20 objects down at one time and a “ filter shutter” on the end of the laser. A filter shutter is a bit of green celluloid or glass which only lets through not more than 10% of the laser light intensity. Then you can aim at each object and open the shutter completely to shoot one object at a time..

I defy anyone to think up a better way to allow all 20 students to go off happily clutching their own Denisyuk holograms.
