Jeffrey Weil Mon Apr 21, 2008 3:46 pm #4
I know this is opening up a can of worms because money is tight for everyone but here's my suggestion about your adapter. Learn how to machine metal!

I've been in holography for a long time and have built many labs. About 3 or 4 years ago I started to get milling machines and lathes and learn how to use them. Now, I have no idea how I ever lived as a holographer without them.

Even small things like the ability to make a slot for a screw will change your world. Here's a good one, the ability to make two parts separately and have they bolt right up to each other without problems!

A small lathe can be had for a few hundred, a small table top milling machine for about 5 hundred. Tooling for them will add a bunch but for a grand or two you could be totally set up as a machinist. At least for small things and that accounts for much of what a holographer needs.

I used to look at Newport stuff with awe. Now I can quickly see all the tooling marks and know exactly how they made it, I also know how easy it is to make this stuff now. Really, its not that hard. Dovetail slides, translation stages, your adapter, spatial filters. Think of all the money you could save. It will cost you time though.

That link to Thorlabs even has the mechanical drawings so you can make it yourself on the lathe.

And then theres home stuff too!!! I bought a new ceiling fan. My electrical box was very old and not the type that the fan connects too. This fan hung from the box itself. Instead of ripping open my ceiling, putting in a new box, repairing the ceiling and then painting it, I just made an adapter plate out of alum. Polished it up and it REALLY looks like it came with the fan. Perfect example.

There is so much you can do for your lab with this equipment. I just made a new plate holder with lots of registration stuff and clamps and etc..... How much would this cost if I didn't do it myself? Probably more than the machine cost that I made it on!

I don't know if any of you are interested in this but if you are just post questions here and I'll answer them.

Jeffrey Weil

I would add to pickup a used copy of "The Machinist's Handbook". You don't really care what edition. Mine is from the 40s and it has everything I need.

Also, listen to your machines. They will tell you what the cut is like. Also, examine the cut and the cuttings and you will learn how to sharpen tools. I was in a machine shop 20 years ago and there was a bin of aluminum/magnesium shavings that you could almost read newspaper through! The machinist was taking .0002" passes at the part! It was a Porche engine casing.
