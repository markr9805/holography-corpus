I received Gary's Gapoxio on Monday. The stuff comes as two blocks of putty. Initially, we thought of putting it in a mould. However, when we tried this, we found the epoxy was gooey and sticky and so didn't seperate from the mould too well. It kind of got drawn out as it was pulled from the mould. Joy didn't want to let it harden in the mould because the instructions said that the stuff would stick to the mould metal (aluminium?) and be impossible to seperate.

Next, she moulded it into a bear with her fingers. The actual moulding took about an hour, but over a period of about 3 to 4 hours, the figure started collapsing and shortening.(Joy now called it "slumpy bear" because the bear progressively started to slump. Reminded me of schooldays when the Latin master would whack you over the head with "Caesar's Commentary" and snap, "Don't slump, dear boy!" - but that's another story). Anyway, here's the final figure:

[http://www.triple-take.com/PicsEvents/p ... object.jpg](http://www.triple-take.com/PicsEvents/photos/photos/gallery/thebearobject.jpg)

We set it up in a two beam H1 geometry with two lightings. Initially, one lighting, on it's head, seemd bright but the other, on it's stomach, seemed dim. I thought it might have been an optic moving, so I changed the mirror three times and still the stomach was dark. Joy had deliberately made the large ears since she thought that that would be a godd test of motion. So, it was pretty surprising when the ears stayed still, but the stomach didn't. I tried only illuminating the stomach and still dark. Finally I had to conclude the stomach was moving because the stuff was still collapsing. That would explain why the ears apparently was steady - the ears, being thinner, had collapsed as much as they were going to, while the increased mass in the stomach meant a longer drying out period and more "motion modes". But...This didn't sit well. If this were true, subsequent shots of the stomach should have made them darker and darker as the stomach kept collapsing. This wasn't true, the stomach area was dark, but it was the same dark, not darker. Then it suddenly occured to me that we have an air etalon and also a local heatwave. So I measured the path length difference between the head lighting and the stomach lighting. Sure enough - a two foot path difference. We evened out the path difference and now - nothing! zilch! So, I'm sitting there, playing solitaire on the computer when I wonder whether we evened it out the wrong way! We measured the reference path and then the object paths and found that both object paths were two feet and six inches longer than the ref! So, once more unto the breach. This time, we got a good, all-round result. It wasn't particularly bright, I'd say good for H2's on silver but a bit weak for H2's on DCG. However, I can now report, in the words of Galileo, "Si non movere" - "It does not move".

Sounds like Joy didn't like it much. Did slouchy bear consist of the whole package that I sent? If so, yeah, that much will sag under it's own weight, it does take some getting used to. Now, if you want to add detailed musculature by laying in individual muscles with small amounts of putty, there shouldn't be a sagging problem. Pushing it into aluminum molds would be a definite no-no.

Slouchy bear ought to feel like a small stone idol by now. (yes, I like the "Slouchy Bear" thing )

I suppose I should take this opportunity to suggest that others report on their own preferred (or not-preferred) materials here, so as to have some useful information consolidated in a single thread?

Long ago I used plain old wet clay, the kind you put in a kiln, and once dry I sprayed it with silver paint and got pretty good results - lots of diffuse reflectivity without actual gloss. Unfortunately, thin sections are extremely delicate and I wouldn't call it a long-term material.
Joy P Mon Jul 25, 2005 8:44 pm #3
OK, so he's finally dragged me out of my nice little mucky room and got me to post...

Firstly, the gapoxio slumpy bear only took about 15 minutes to build, sort of like a snowman, and was fully hard (we presumed) in about an hour in our seasonal sauna-like weather. But the stuff was so soft in the beginning that he wouldn't stand up well, as you saw. Also, since I chose to use gloves, it felt sticky and was a bit hard to manipulate. However, I also freely admit that in my effort to conserve the precious stuff, I only cut off small bits as I needed them. And I didn't measure in any scientific way to assure equal proportions. I suspect that if I had more resin half than binding half, (or vice versa?) the result could be a bit thin.

In the end, he dried nice and hard, and I even drew him a few hairs with a file - it seems that it takes well to filing. My thought at the time was to recommend making a crude lump of a shape and then carve out the model afterward.

I wouldn't say I don't like it. It's nice that it forms together (joining pieces) better than regular clay, and the finished piece is lightweight - a definite boon if you're trying to make a large model hold still under long exposures. I just need to improve my technique, since I'm actually not much of a sculptor. I usually use ready-made models or molding techniques rather than go from scratch.

I have also used sculpey, both leaving it soft clay and baking it. It shoots fine either way (the soft way shows fingerprints easily) but it can be heavy.
