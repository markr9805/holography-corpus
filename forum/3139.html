Dinesh Sat Feb 19, 2005 5:11 pm #2
There may be many causes for a dim image. The most common are motion, bad ratio, incomplete development.

1) Beam Ratio

The beam ratio, the brightness of the reference beam as compared to the brightness of the object beam, is a very important ccharacteristic for the hologram. Generally, this ratio is 1:1 for a reflection hologram, which means that the object needs to be reflecting the same amount of light to the back of the plate as the reference beam hitting the front of the plate. Since the reference beam is what's causing the reflection from the object in the first place, this is obviously not completely possible. (There are tricks and work arounds). However, if you spray painted your object flat white and placed it as close to the plate as possible, you'll get pretty close to 1:1. However, bear in mind that the object needs to reflect back onto the plate. If the object is oriented such that the object is reflecting off the plate, the ratio will still be bad. There are two ways to check this. One is simply to see . Look at the object from the reference side and, without getting your head in the way of the reference beam, try to get as close to the reference as possible. If the brightest parts of the object is not at the plate your ratio is poor. Another way is to place a white card with a hole at the center. The hole needs to be small, but not too small. Now the reference comes through the hole and illuminates the object which reflects onto the back of the white card. Look on either side of the card and you should be able to judge the ratio.

2) Motion

If either your table or your object is not rigid you'll get a constant motion and the brightness drops. The easiest way is to put a Michaelson Interferometer on the table. Actually a still easier way is to mount a platform attached to the plateholder and mount your object onto it. This way any motion of the plateholder also moves the object and there is no relative motion. Make sure your object is rigid, not soft plastic or rubber for example, and rigidly mounted. After the object is mounted, tap it with your fingernail. If the sound is a solid "thwunk" you'll probably be OK, but if it "rings" or sounds a little like "twink" or "ping" (sorry, getting a little onomatopoeic here - not particularly scientific ) it may be moving. Also, be sure that the plate is rigidly held in the plate holder. Do the "tapping" test on it.

3) Development

For a reflection the plate needs to go about 80% dark in about 3 minutes. It's difficult to judge "80% dark" but the best way I've found is that the safelight almost disappears when seen through the developed plate. If this happens too soon (less that a minute or so) then you've overexposed, if it happes too late (more than 4 minutes) then it's underdone. Bleach until clear, this should take about 30 seconds to a minute, and then 30 seconds more.

A few general rules: Try to avoid shiny metallic objects. These tend to throw light coming in at reference away from the plate. Plaster or porcelain objects are probably the best to start with (a lot of people start with porcelain animals, hence we call ourselves "The Porcelain Cat Group"). Don't paint the object red just because you have a red laser. They may not be the same "red". Paint it flat white. Let the plate settle for a couple of minutes. If you have to be in the room to expose, then after loading the plate, keep very still for about 2 minutes before exposing. A shutter controlled from outside the room is best. Several forum members have made their own . In any case, let the plate settle for a couple of minutes between loading and shooting.

As for Slavich 03 being "better", it all depends..... The emulsion consists of very tiny grains of silver halide. The smaller the grain, the "cleaner" the hologram and the greater the level of detail - within limits. However, the down side is that it takes longer to expose and certain characteristics are more sensitive, like beam ratio and the level to which you let it go dark during development. In your case of making single beam Denisyuks, it makes little difference.

It's not so much where the laser is, but the relative motion between the plate and the object. If you're using plates, then it's probably a good idea not to let your object touch the plate, since the object is on the emulsion side. In general, it's still a good idea not to have your object touch the plate since any motion of the plate transfers to the object and may set the object vibrating with an independant vibration. The best geometry is to ensure that the plate and the object move together. One way is to have a platform jutting off the plate holder. Then any motion of the plateholder will also move the platform and both plate and object move together. Others have a cradle sort of system with the plate sitting on top. So, you have a platform laid down horizontally with 4 long screws coming off it. You then place you plate on top of the 4 screws and maybe find a way to clamp the corners of the plate from above. Now both the plate and the object are connected through the horizontal platform and the 4 screws. You now bring in your beam from the top down.

As for the glass/plastic, unless you expect to keep the developer for years, I can't see any difference. For what it's worth, I keep mine ina dark glass bottle.
