JohnFP Tue May 29, 2007 2:12 pm #3
Well, here is the reason I ask and I may be digging too deep. And it may also be that the mesh sizes available simply are not different enough to change any properties but let me try to explain myslef.

After the collagen has been broken down into partial triple helices and separate amino chains and dried. The gelatin is ground into mesh sizes as determined by the application (I was told this by Great Lakes Gelatin when I spoke to them a couple years back).

Now the collagen molecule is about 300nm long and lets say the amino chains separate all but a 50nm section near one end. Then of course there are other molecuels doing the same thing. As these strands cool, there is some bonding and reformation of the the individual amino acids into triple helices and just bonded amino chains. I envision, if it could be looked at, that you could see that one partial collagen molecule connected to another, to another and so one so that in essence a molecule 1mm away from another molecule is actually connected to it via many more molecules in between.

So, if the mesh size is large, lets say 25 microns, then about every 83 molecules across this chain is broken, not only broken but possibly destroyed by a mechanical force. Now lets take some extreems. What if the gelatin was crushed in a mortice and pestle to a very fine powder, lets say 1 micron.

Would this damage enough molecules such that when the gelatin was redisolved and then cooled that the properties would be different then that of gelatin of a much larger mesh size right from the collagen hydrolysis process?

It would be an interesting test to take some gelatin of known bloom and of a very large mesh by industry standards and grind one into a very very fine powder, then make two batches of emulsions and compare the differences.

Hey, just some food for thought!
