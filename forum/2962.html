Dinesh Sat Dec 18, 2004 9:01 pm #2
Woodgrain is not limited to film, but the effects are worse on film than on glass. Basically, it's interference from the front and back of the emulsion surface. The traditional way to get rid of woodgrain is to shoot at Brewsters angle (56 degrees), but most sources don't tell you that it's meaningless unless you have p polarisation. If you have mixed polarisation, there's no way to get rid of it completely.

Reflected light and incoming light forms a plane. If the beam is polarised parallel to this plane, it's p. If it's polarised at right angles to the plane, it's s. If it's polarised at some angle, it's mixed p and s. The reflectivity of light from any surface depends on the polarisation of the Light. If the light is polarised in p, then reflection off the surface gradually falls off until, at a particular angle - Brewster's angle - there is zero reflectivity. It then gradually increases. For glass this angle is 56 degrees. For any material of index n,

Brewsters angle = (inv tan)(n)

Light at s polarisation just reflects, there is no angle at which reflectivity falls to zero. The reflectivity of mixed, or unpolarised light will never vanish but may dim, depending on the proportion of p to s. You can remember this by "p passes, s skips"

So when light at p and at Brewsters angle hits the back surface of the glass, then there is no reflection and it cannot interfere with the front reflection, of which there is again none.

One way of finding the polarisation is to take a piece of plane glass and hit it with a raw, unexpanded beam (care!). Slowly revolve the glass in a horizontal plane so that the reflected beam pans across a wall. If the beam is horizontally polarised, the reflected spot will gradually die off. If it never dies off, try again in a vertical plane so that the beam pans across the ceiling. If it now dies off, the beam is vertically polarised and you need a half wave plate (or is it 1/4 plate, I can never keep that straight!) or an overhead reference beam. Whether or not the reference is vertical or horizontal is purely a personal preference. Some people swear by overhead references and some by horizontal setups.
Tom B. Sun Dec 19, 2004 5:21 am #3
If you have a pair of polarized sunglasses (or clip-ons) this will serve as a reference. And you can check your laser's polarization, too. They are oriented to reject glare from water and road surfaces, which I think (from the mnemonic P=passes. S = skips) is S polariized. So they pass P-polarized light when oriented horizontally as normally worn. The Brewster angle thing is that P-polarized light is almost 100% transmitted (no reflections) when it hits at a particular angle, about 56 degrees from normal (perpendicular) for glass. You have a few degrees leeway.

For an overhead reference shining down on the plate, the dimming from the sunglasses for P-polarized light should be at a minimum when they are held horizontally. The angle to the plate should be 56-57 degrees, relative to the plate normal. I made up a little 2.5 inch square card with a line at 56 degrees to the corner that I can rest up against the plate and shadow with my finger to see what angle the light is hitting at. For horizontal setups, rotate everything 90 degrees.

When I get totally confused about polarization, I play with a laser pointer (all of which are polarized AFAIK) and a piece of glass to remind myself how it works. Also my expanded beam is bright enough that I can see the plate reflections for non-Brewster angles easily (if I look for them)
