[quote:07703ff680="mrpaslow"]I'm envsioning a computer-controlled, almost-real-time holocamera for making ultra-high-res stereograms. I think some other people might already be doing this.

An automatic holo-camera has been built by several people over the years. One problem is that it requires automatic exposures of hundreds of images all exactly the same. If any exposures aren't right, the particular frame gets exposed differently from all the other frames and the image will "jump". Motion problems aside, if the laser you use is unstable, such as a DPSS, then it's going to be a real problem to ensure reliability for all the frames. At say 15 frames/sec, 1 minutes of video requires 60x15=900 frames and so requires 900 exposures all exactly the same. Having said that, I believe Colin and Larry are working on a system to stabilise these lasers. Since it's their work, I'll let them describe it.

[quote:07703ff680="mrpaslow"]And I want to make sure of I understand a couple of things:

1) With great coherence length, doesn't that mean that I don't have to be concerned with beam-path-length matching?[/quote:07703ff680]

Yes, true. There is a cost. The more off-path-length you are the more the image decays, but if you're within about 20% of the coherence length of the laser you'll probably not notice it. The reason is that when the two beams meet, you get interference fringes. The hologram is a photograph of those fringes. Hence the better the contrast of the fringes, the better is the photograph of them and the brighter the hologram. However, the contrast of a set of fringes is a function of the coherence length. When the beam paths are exactly the same, the fringe contrast is at it's highest. As the beam paths vary, the fringe contrast drops until, at the limit of the coherence length, there is no contrast at all. The rate at which the contrast drops depends on the coherence length. Hence, with a laser with a coherence length of, say, 2 meters, or approx 6 feet, the fringe contrast drops to zero within 6 feet. Thus the drop in contrast over 12 inches or so is negligible. As the path difference approaches 4 feet, there'll be a notice-able drop in fringe contrast.

[quote:07703ff680="mrpaslow"]2) If I had a YAG pulse laser, couldn't I make a high-power pulse green laser by running the output through a frequency-doubling crystal?

What am I missing? [/quote:07703ff680]

Yes, that's what a YAG does. YAGs lase at 1060 odd (IR) and this is passed through a frequency doubling crystal to end up at 532. You can pass it through another frequency doubling crystal and get down 266, but you'll probably not see much
