Good day.

Couldn't resist to send photos of my first silver-halide hologram.

Shot yesterday, on homemade plates.

Special thanks to Jeff Blythe - for the recipe for DIY plates. Thanks Jeff !

X-eyes viewing format

![Image](http://www.unipress.waw.pl/~sarzyn/stereo04.jpg)

![Image](http://www.unipress.waw.pl/~sarzyn/stereo04.gif)

Shot with 5mW laser module, 1' exposure time (I guess it's overexposed), Denisyuk scheme.

Developed with DIY Neutol, bleached with rehalogenating bleach.

About the plates - I used photographic-grade gelatin, KOH/isopropyl alcohol glass cleaning, no silane, courtain-coating method (after developing and drying gelatine layer is 18um thick- "little" too thick I guess) , 1 min. hardening in 5% formaldehyde, 5h curing at 60 C deg., 4 min. Ag loading and 4 min. KBr/dye bath.

No TEA, no ascorbate bath (yet)

Yes, you are right. But don't forget these two things:

1st. you're talking about prices from 20 years ago, when (at least in Spain) you could buy a music record for the equivalent to $6, whereas now you pay at least three times that price for a CD which is supposedly easier and cheaper to produce.

2nd. (and more important) Kodak and Agfa were the main manufacturers of holographic film and plates at that time, although for them this was like a 'prestige' product, not making any profit (if not loosing money) from it. They make the big profit with the whole range of photographic product they marketed, and they were huge companies. The few companies producing holographic material nowadays (and I know one quite well ) are very small, and their main business is producing holographic plates and/or film. We must make at least some profit; otherwise we won't be able to survive as private companies. We do not have a big company backing us.

Lasers are cheaper, because the demand is very large. In the late 80's there was neither laser diodes nor DPSS lasers, the main market being He-Ne lasers used for supermarket scanners. Only universities, research centres and a few security companies were using green and blue lasers. Now everybody is got at least one CD player/ burner and a DVD recorder at home with a high power laser diode inside.

So it is no wonder that the world is upside down compared to what it was in 1987 regarding holography (and it is not the only thing that has change since then )

Tony Thu Feb 01, 2007 10:37 pm #16
I have to post an opposing opinion on plates vs film for beginners-

If you can take advantage of other's experience, and not have to reinvent the wheel, the lower cost of film means you can make more exposures on limited funds, and therefore learn more of the 'other' lessons.

As a beginner, you do not have to index match. Just clean the glass plates used to sandwich the film well with Windex.

The biggest trick to mounting film between glass, is to preven the glass from bowing outward. I know it sounds minimal, but if clamps are placed outside of the film area, the glass will bend outward, allowing the film to move in the center. So either place the clamps so that they overlap the film, or stick a layer of cellophane tape along the outside edges of the glass to provide a spacing between the plates that is about the same as the film thickness.

Honestly, the one thing that helped me get up the 'learning curve' the most was buying a 50m roll of 75mm wide 8E75 on eBay for $50. Cutting it into 5 inch strips, it lasted over two years of test (and learning) shots.

Experience is the great teacher. Experience is limited by time or money. If limited by money, buy film. If limited by time, buy plates.
