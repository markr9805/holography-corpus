Joe Farina Sun Sep 24, 2006 12:45 pm #1
This turned out to be a lot simpler than I thought, thanks to a tip from Colin. All that's really needed is a huge quantity of plastic drinking straws. To make a laminar flow station, the following method can be used. I've built two already, and they work really well.

Decide on how big the enclosure needs to be, based on the available space in your lab, the size of plates you want to coat, etc. My cabinets measure 2 feet wide by 4 feet long by 15 inches high. While you consider this, obtain the hepa filter(s) that will be placed toward the back end of the cabinet. These filters do not need to completely fill the space measuring 2 feet by 15 inches (or whatever the space is) but it should be reasonably close. Multiple filters can be taped together.

Also, decide on the kind of fan that will be placed at the very back, which pushes air through the hepa filters toward the front. Again, the fan size does not need to be a perfect match, but the closer the better. I used a cheap fan that has two small fans in one unit, which is designed for use in a window (it cost $14).

To build the cabinet itself, I used the stuff called "melamine" which is particle-board coated with a hard white plastic on both surfaces (the thickness is 3/4 inch). (I don't know if they have this same kind of material in Europe, but plywood could be used if it was thoroughly sealed with varnish.)

The final component needed is the straws. Luckily, I live near a restaurant-supply store which had all the straws I needed (not wrapped with paper, because it would be extremely tedious, to say the least, to have to unwrap thousands of straws). I'm sure with a little searching, there would be an internet source that would sell them (the company I bought them from is called Gordon Food Service). Each of my cabinets required about $60 worth of straws.

Start stuffing those straws in the interior of the box, towards the back. You want a wall of straws. When the wall is almost complete, start wedging more straws in near the interior walls of the enclosure, so that the whole wall of straws is tightly wedged in, so that it won't slide around. Make the wall as tight as possible, this may take a couple of hours. Then from the back, add the hepa filters just in back of the straws, and tape them in place along the edges (inside the enclosure). Then put the fan in the very back (just barely inside the box), and tape it along the edges, so that air pushed by the fan doesn't leak out backwards.

That's it. The air is pushed by the fan into the filters, then through the straws (which act like a honeycomb to make the air flow straight out the other end, with no irregular swirls or eddies that could draw dusty air back in). Maybe another honeycomb material other than straws could be used.

It works extremely well, is cheap, and can be customized to your lab size.
