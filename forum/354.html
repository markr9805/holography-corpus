Ed Wesly Thu Oct 13, 2011 4:11 pm #7
It was good to have attached the image so I could see where your confusion is coming from.

Certainly you can see that if you have a square 1 linear centimeter on a side, then it’s area is 1 square centimeter. But a square that is a half a centimeter, .5 cm, on a side, does give a surface area of .5 cm X .5 cm = .25 cm^2. Notice the linear dimensions are in plain cm’s, and the area is in cm^2. And it is obvious from your drawing that there are 4 quarter centimeter boxes in the whole square centimeter.

But the sensor is not a square but a circle, but before we get to that part, let’s look at how long each side would be for a square whose surface area is .5 cm^2. In other words, since side times side equals area, what number times itself yields .5?

Off the top of your head, you can see that since .7 X .7 = .49 the sides of a square that has a surface area of one-half square centimeter is around that number. The actual number is a little bit more that .7 to make up the difference to get from .49 to .5.

The real dimension of a side of a square that is precisely .5 cm^2 in area is the square root of two over two. Kind of hard to post this in symbols, but write down radical two over plain 2 and you have the number as precise as it can be.

But how can it be? Write down another radical two over two next to the first one with a times sign between them. Then do the math; in the numerator you have the square root of two times the square root of two, which equals 2. (That’s the definition of the square root of two: the number that times itself equals two.)

In the denominator we have 2 X 2 with no radical, which = 4. So the fraction at this point is 2/4, reducing to ½ which = .5. If you do this on your calculator, you will see that if you take the square root of two, then divide by 2, you will get .7 and some change. If you take that number and multiply it by itself, you will get exactly .5.

If you add another square to your drawing, with its sides being .7 etc. as close as you can, you will see a square larger than the .25 cm^2. The rest of the stuff, the L-shaped leftover which can be converted into two rectangles, does have the surface area of the other half of a full square centimeter or .5 cm^2. (This exercise is left to the reader.)

Another tact to take to illustrate this conundrum is to convert the square centimeter to square millimeters. Since there are 10 mm in 1 cm, the one centimeter square has 10 X 10 or 100 mm^2. So .5 cm = 5 mm, so that a square 5 mm on a side contains 25 square millimeters, which is 25/100 or ¼ of a square cm.

Converting the square centimeter to a circle which has the same area is bit more daunting. Here goes: 1 cm^2 = pi times radius squared. Divide 1 by pi. This equals the radius squared. Take the square root of this number, and this is the radius of a circle whose area is a square centimeter, which turns out to be .56…, or 5.6 mm. Your 8 mm detector has a radius of 4, smaller than the 5.6 mentioned above. How much smaller? Dividing 4 into 5.6 yields 1.4, which coincidentally turns out to be the beginning digits of the square root of two.

So does this begin to make everything as clear as mud? Your 8 mm diameter does have a surface area of about a half a square centimeter. An approximately 11.2 millimeter diameter detector does have a surface area of one full centimeter squared. So your fudge factor is indeed 2 to bring your detector up to the per square centimeter standard of the industry.

The square root of two rears its head in the photographic f/stop series. Each numbered stop differs from one on either side of it by a factor of the square root of two, but usually rounded off to just one decimal place. This is because the f/stops relate the size of the exit pupil of the lens to its focal length, but each major click stop opening is either twice or half the area, to let in twice or half the amount of light, so the radii of the circles of the pupils change not by 2, which would make the area either four times as big or ¼ as small but by the square root of 2, since that number will end up being multiplied by itself. Viz. 1 X 1.4 = 1.4, 1.4 X 1.4 =2, 2 X 1.4 = 2.8, 2.8 X 1.4 = 4, 4 X 1.4 = 5.6, 5.6 X 1.4 = 8, and so on. Something I teach every semester, so I was familiar with this area problem.
