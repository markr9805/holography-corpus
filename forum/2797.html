Ok, talked to Jimbo. Got an explanation of the Gravity Well in the Planetarium. I wasn't quite right in my description of the effect and construction, so I was to some extent wrong, but not entirely. It's even weirder than I thought. I don't think Dinesh would have approved of this idea either, but this guy outranks him.

Rather than try to paraphrase, take a look at the picture again:

[http://www.9000shops.com/omniscan-jimbo.jpg](http://www.9000shops.com/omniscan-jimbo.jpg)

Then read what Jimbo said:

>As pertains to the OmniScan: The planetarium projector is actually

>incidental to the 3D scan projection. It only projects the starfield on

>the dome. The 3D grid representing the Swartzchild radius, gravity field

>and event horizon of the black hole is done using a laser (one watt mixed

>gas He/Neon) concealed in the bottom of the cabinet of the Spitz

>planetarium projector. Four beam splitters coupled to fiberoptics carry

>the beams up top to the defusers beaming through a 360 degree holo image

>mounted on a turntable allowing it to rotate. Think of it as an endless

>loop filmstrip with the beginning tacked to the end. To compensate for

>the shape of the dome and the audience perspective the loop is not cut at

>right angles before being tacked together like you'd do in a Quicktime

>panorama, for instance. Instead, the ends are cut in such a way as to

>make the top circumference smaller than the bottom one. Sort of a

>modified teepee shape, if that makes any sense. If you look carefully at

>the picture you can see the holo filmstrip right at the top of the base

>that the Spitz is sitting on, although the quality of the picture is such

>that you'd be hard put to recognize it unless you were told. It's the

>illuminated thing going around it. Your friend is partially correct.

>Although not technically what we would consider a true 3D mid air image,

>the liberal use of particulate matter and the effect of the coherent

>nature of the laser light reflecting to the eye off all those millions of

>tiny specs of matter and the stereo nature of our vision fools the brain

>into supplying the missing information that would be necessary to achieve

>the true effect. The planetarium director had the original idea to do

>something "a little different" and we sort of took it to the extreme as

>an exercise. We do conventional laser shows in their planetarium on the

>weekend nights. We just wanted to see if we could do it. Turned out to

>be quite a lot of work; it's not nice to fool Mother Nature :^)

>

>On a related note: A Japanese man recently successfully demonstrated an

>actual Princess Leia type mid air 3D projected moving image using 32

>cameras and vid projectors and rotating mirrors. Apparently it is not

>impossible. How about THAT, Mr. Fung!!!

>

>You'd better start building quickly if you're going to beat that guy to

>the patent office!

>

>There you go. More than any sane person should want to know about a

>stupid sixty-second effect in a Saturday afternoon planetarium show, eh?

>

>BTW, my personal opinion is that any physicist worth his salt should know

>better than to claim that ANYTHING is impossible. That's tantamount to

>issuing an invitation to be proved a fool. Of course I can only claim a

>master degree in the field. Perhaps your friend is a Fud (excuse me,

>Ph.D.) in which case he's entitled to be stilted and opinionated. As

>we're unlikely to ever meet in person, you can tell him I said so and

>what'ya gonna do about it, eh! ;^)

<B>My questions:</B>

>First I was confused,

>but after squeezing my brain a little I think I'm slightly less confused.

>So is this kind of like a projection variation on the old multiplex

>thing? With sixteen reconstruction beams? And, on the dome or mostly

>reflecting off the particulates? Yeah, I'm still a little confused.

<B>Jimbo's reply:</B>

>Yeah, that's the basic concept. Mostly on the dome but we found that the

>particles really made the difference in tricking the brain.



Ok, so I had to look at the picture again. See the projector base? See the bright spots around it's edge? Those would be the sixteen illumination points, and it looks like the little strip of transparent stuff is the "holo-filmstrip".

Uh, can you say "extreme"? I said* these guys were high end, this makes most of us look like a kid with a flashlight. I still don't really understand how it can even work, but there it is, straight from the Physicist who's team did it. And I still wish I could have been there to see it.

It makes me feel a little silly sitting here trying to come up with design concepts for a display the size of a small coffee can....
