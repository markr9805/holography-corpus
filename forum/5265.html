O.K., this is all purely speculative, but here goes.

There are methods used in the paint industry to measure film hardness and thickness. I don't know what they're called, but I can describe them. (I haven't tried them myself.)

A way to measure thickness is by using a scratch test using a special "card." This is a piece of metal with very short "notches" in one end. Each notch has a different depth. In the commecially-available cards (which are quite cheap), the depth of each notch is sometimes labeled in microns, but usually mils (as I recall) and these depths have a pretty good correspondence (at the end of the smallest notches) to the (dry) thicknesses of gelatin on holographic plates. In the paint industry, these cards are pushed forcefully against the paint surface to produce a series of scratches or gouges. Based on the appearance of these gouges, the thickness of the coating can be easily determined.

Maybe one of these could be made at home using a small sheet of steel (or brass?). The edge of a small file could be used to make a series of notches on one end. It would be necessary to have some reference for the depth of each file cut, maybe those cheap "feeler gauges" used to measure spark plug gaps could be used. Then the card could be scratched across the edge of the plate, and the type of scratches produced should tell the story. This method is a bit destructive, but I think it could be done (along an edge of the plate) without much trouble. For DCG, the edges should be cleared of gelatin anyway.

To measure the hardness of films, a "steel ball" method is used in the paint industry. A steel ball is simply dropped on the film from a fixed distance. The indentation on the film tells the story. A large indentation might mean softness, a small indentation might mean hardness (?) A magnifying glass or cheap microscope could be used if necessary. (I haven't tried this on gelatin, so I don't know what the result would be.)
Hans Wed Dec 14, 2005 3:52 pm #2
If you have DCG that is only sensitive for blue light, could you not use a red laser with known wavelength to bounce light of the coated side? You should then get at least two reflections: gelatine, glass, other side of glass. The knowns are the angle of the glass to the test laser and the distance to the projection screen. It should then be fairly easy to calculate the thickness by measuring the distance between the fringes created by the first two reflections.

If you had a knife with a cutting angle of an exactly known angle, I think you could look at the cut through the microscope, measure what you see and again calculate the thickness.

Is hardness measured by the spring constant of a material or the resistance to crack/break under a certain pressure?

If it's the former, would it not be possible to make a kind off precise mini jack stand? On top of this jack stand one could place a very precise electronic weighing scale. On top of the scale rests one side of a lever. On the other side of the lever is the emulsion side of the hologram. If one could now increase the height of the mini-jack by a known small distance and read the difference in measured weight of the scale. And then calculate the moment related to the differences in arm length of the lever. It should be easy to calculate the difference in pressure on the gel if the surface area of the lever side resting on the gel is a known factor. Hmm, perhaps the tolorerances of all the components of this setup would be too strict to be fabricated at home?

Oops I did not read your first post about hardness. How about resting a plate on top of the sensitive chemistry weighing scale and making a rigid metal mount around the scale to which a micrometer is attached. This way you don't need any special springs and levers. You could then depress the gelatin from the top with your micrometer and read the difference in measured weight on your scale. Calculations are simpler that way too and there is a lot less to calibrate with this setup.

Hans Wed Dec 14, 2005 5:46 pm #5
I just thought of another way to measure the hardness. This one should be by far the easiest if you are not interested in ABSOLUTE hardness in measurements like Newtons and milimeters.

If you clamp an coated plate onto something rigid on one side, and then displace the other side (thus bend the whole plate) with a fixed distance, with a micrometer from the coated side, and then count number of fringes that get displaced from reflected laser light you could measure a RELATIVE hardness that you can compare with other successfull plates. Of-course the glass needs to be of the same type for different plates.

Here's an example of the "thickness tester" that I was talking about:

[http://www.thomassci.com/catalog/product/2846](http://www.thomassci.com/catalog/product/2846)

Also, I would like to note some corrections to my earlier post. The thickness gauge in the above link is called a "wet film" gauge, but I think it could be used (?) on a dry gelatin film in a "scratch" manner. This is because a dry gelatin emulsion is much softer than dry paint coatings.

As for a homemade one, using feeler gauges as a reference for the depth of the file-cut may not work, since feeler gauges are probably (?) too thick. Some other thin material would probably need to be used for reference.
