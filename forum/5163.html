Concerning the proximity layout, I am not sure. I know we discussed it a while back and I used to call them contact copies, but since I separate the master from the copy, I think I was corrected in that it is not a contact copy.

This photo shows my plateholder holding a piece of glass where the film would normally go in a single beam reflection hologram. When a film plate is put in the plateholder the emulsion faces the object. This allows the emulsion to fact the copy in the proximity set up. The light is coming in from the right above which you cannot see. The object would normally be placed just to the left of the plate and as close to the plate as possible. The final reflection has overhead lighting and can be used as a proximity master with the overhead lighting.

Then in the contact copy the object is removed, the plate in the plateholder is removed and the top bar of the plateholder, which is adjustable is raised all the way up or take off. To the left of the plateholder is the proximity copy set up. It is very simple. I just lay the processed single beam reflection hologram down on a back piece of paper. It is on the bottom. It is flipped over (illuimated from bottom now not top) such that the pseudo image stands above the plate. I then use three screws as stands to hold the unexposed film. The three being used in this photo raises the plate about 1/2 an inch. This give a image planed hologram with 1/2 an inch of the object in front of the plate. Near the bottom of the plate holder you can see three screws that are about 1 inch long. They are used for more image planing with the object about 1 inch in front of the plate. I used the latter for the train. After the plate is exposed in this set up with bottom lighting which is actually a hologram of the pseudo real image, it is processed and flipped around for viewing, which puts the replay light back to coming in from the top and the virtual image is reconstructed.

I've done some reflection transfers with Ultimate/BB-640. My first efforts used the more traditional H1/H2 arrangement, with the object far enough away from the (4x5) plate that it was not shadowed by the plate (about 6-8 inches) and light could be controlled independently from the reference. Then the normal flip and rotation and H2 transfer to a plate about 6" away. Results were bright, but as John mentioned, the field of view is not very wide when the plates are so far apart unless the master is much bigger than the H2.

Later I tried a transfer with the plates much closer together (about 1/2"), hoping that even though the object/reference ratio could not be adjusted there might be some brightness gain and a bit closer image. As it turned out, I didn't see any noticeable increase in brightness and the contrast seemed lower (blacks were not as black) which I guessed was likely due to scattered light and reflections of object light bouncing between the pair of plates in the setup. The image was OK, just not as clean as I am used to. I think that using a a thick glass spacer plate between H1/H2 and index matching fluid on both sides might have helped.
Joe Farina Wed Nov 09, 2005 1:06 pm #13
John, if you want to use a DCG master that is stable under ambient conditions (without sealing it under glass) there are a couple of alternatives you may want to consider.

First, the Chinese have done work to stabilize dye-sensitized DCG by using ninhydrin. For example, see "Methylene blue dichromated-gelatin holograms: antihumidity method for taking off strongly adsorbing humidity groups" in Applied Optics, Volume 37, Number 2, pages 326-8 (1998). I beleive there are a number of papers by the Chinese on this subject, but I haven't followed them because I still favor sealing DCG/SHSG holograms under glass for "final" protection. However, if you just need to use the master for a limited period of time to make transfers, it would be a different story.

Second, I've made reference on this forum in the past to a method described by Jeff Blyth, which involves keeping the hologram in a very dry state for a long period of time. I will quote from an e-mail of his that was forwarded to me in 1999, regarding his dye-sensitized DCG system:

"....The whole point is that to be stable these DCG holograms must be as dry as you can possibly make them. It is no use warming them at 60C if they are almost in a SAUNA or steam room....You have to have the lowest possible humidity in your oven....Oneway I have done it successfully is to buy say 1kg of silicagel crystals (preferably the larger size crystals which are also the self indicating ones which are blue when they are dry and pink when damp. To work properly these crystals should be extremely dry so that they are more violet colored than blue. To get the crystals as dry as that it is best to use a vacuum oven but if that is not possible then heat the crystals in a very hot oven at about 250C - 300C for an hour or more....It is practical to spread these silica gel crystals over an iron or aluminium tray and after heating don't give them time to reabsorb moisture from the air, put the tray in your 60C warmer together with your holograms and leave them all together for 24 hours or more. The amounts of water locked into the gelatin fringes (as cracks) are held there very tenaciously and you have to allow it plenty of time to be driven out slowly....I have found that keeping any type of DCG hologram in an extremely dry state for a few days makes them highly resistant to ever disappearing under all ordinary conditions. They can keep for years without sealing them up unless you put liquid water on them...."

Joe Farina Fri Dec 02, 2005 1:01 pm #24
It has been a dream of mine to find a method of sealing DCG or SHSG holograms so that they are totally impervious to moisture.

It is a complex problem, however.

A long time ago, Jeff Blyth mentioned that the Pilkington firm had managed to seal DCG in holograms in glass, and I took this to mean that the actual glass edges had been heated and fused.

There is a very serious technical problem with this, however. When a part of a piece of glass is heated to melting temperature (and another part of the same piece remains well below that temperature) and then the whole thing cools back down, very severe internal stresses are created in the glass. These stresses can easily be observed by viewing the glass through a pair of crossed polarizers. I consider these stresses to be totally unacceptable, as the glass can easily shatter when subjected to sudden temperature changes. These stresses will be created in ordinary glass or borosilicate glass.

Now, if the hologram could be made on a sheet of fused quartz, then another piece of fused quartz placed on top, and the edges fused with a torch, then it might be a different story, because I'm under the impression that quartz will not have such troublesome stresses when it cools (but I don't know for sure). But of course, fused quartz is not readily available in large sheets, and when it is, the cost would be prohibitive.

I did try it with borosilicate sheets, however. I can verify that the stresses can be very severe. Not only did I observe them through the polariscope, but the stuff shattered as it cooled. (It's funny, though, that just after it shattered, it caused an unusual event which caught my attention. Taking a clue from this, I did another experiment which led to my finding another method for sealing DCG/SHSG holograms, which has held up extremely well under accelerated tests. But I wouldn't recommend it for outdoor applications.) When I asked Jeff Blyth for additional information about the Pilkington method, he didn't respond. And I haven't found even the slightest reference to it anywhere, despite considerable searching on my part.

Joe Farina Fri Dec 02, 2005 3:25 pm #28
Yes, Pyrex is a borosilicate glass. I used Borofloat (which is a Schott product) because it was available in thin sheets, and I thought it would have better surface quality since it's made with the float process. Pyrex and Borofloat have pretty much identical properties.

The thinner the borosilicate glass is, the better it would be for this purpose. Say for example you have a sheet that is only 1mm thick and heat one spot with a torch. When you remove the flame, the heated spot will cool down very quickly. The stresses won't have much of chance to build up. The problem arises when the heated area remains hot for a prolonged period of time, allowing stresses to form. Although borosilicate glass has lower expansion characteristics compared to ordinary glass (as Colin metnioned), those stresses are still unacceptable in my opinion.

It's a completely different situation when it can be put into an annealing oven. The temperature of the whole piece can be gently raised to the annealing temperature, which is below the softening temperature. Then the stresses will be slowly relieved with the careful procedure that Colin suggested.

I think Aclar can be hot sealed. You might equally look out for other fluorinated plastics like FEP, ETFE, PFA, PFFE, PVF. By the way we (Polygrama) are equally looking for high-quality films for our photopolymers: preferably non birefringent materials with high oxygen-barrier protection.

I understand Aclar etc. are difficult to coat. Like most plastics they require special pretreatment.

In the case of DCG/SHSG sealing you don't need oxygen protection. Water vapor is the thing to watch for. By the way, water vapor permeability is also something to consider when using optical adhesives. Silicone materials e.g. do very poorly in that respect.

PVDC (“Saran”) would be an interesting material which provides almost as high protection as fluorinated plastics.

As you know, I used to record the old Ilford films in a water tank to replay them wet. The Saran-sealed films kept wet very well. But optical quality of that wrapping film became an issue then.

I believe – though I was unable to locate that source – Saran can be dissolved in some nasty aggressive solvent (?).
