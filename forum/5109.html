MichaelH Thu Oct 13, 2005 7:17 am #2
Since you ask, I'm going to assume you're using a CW laser of relatively low power and say that even without chemical treatment it's possible to make a hologram of a living plant as long as you keep the exposures as short as humanly possible.

One trick used by Lon Moore was to keep his lab cold and use hot developer which allowed him to expose for a shorter period and speed up the development. Even with this trick I suspect that you'll have a hard time with anything less than a 20mW laser if your subject is large. I heartily recommend you try though.

There may be chemicals which will slow down the metabolic (is that the correct term when used for a plant?) processes in a plant but that knowledge is well out of my realm.

BobH Thu Oct 13, 2005 2:32 pm #8
If you paint the plants and successfully make a hologram, you'll get a hologram of painted plants! Painted plants will look just like .... painted plants. Painting everything to be holographed flat white makes for very boring and bland images.

With your laser and technique, I'd recommend placing the plants in front of a very diffuse and reflective background, and concentrating on the shape of the 3-D void that will be recorded because of the plant's movement. Peter Miller did something similar with leaves that resulted in some stunning images. Jim Feroe made "shadowgrams" of density patterns in a water tank that were also wonderful.

Cristiano,

I agree with Bob, you are asking too much of a DCG system that is more than a 100 times less sensitive than silver halide. I will be posting more on this problem later with a diagram.

A holographic shadowgram image is not going to be the uninteresting 2 dimensional silhouette you may be imagining. If arranged right you should have an attractive intricate 3 dimensional black flower .

The simplest way to make a successful shadowgram is to go to a kitchenware shop and buy an anodized aluminum pie or cake dish. This should have about 50-100% greater diameter than the extent of the flower which hopefully is going to be small but intricate. You usually have a number of dishes to choose from in a shop so this is then helpful because you should first checkout the dish in the shop before purchasing by placing it upside down on a completely flat clean glass surface. Try to get one that does’nt have any tendency to rock when you place your fingers on top. So that when you arrange your flower in this dish you are then going to rest your recording plate across the top free from the risk of micro rocks. In your studio lab it is best to carefully stick 3 or 4 completely unwrinkled bits of insulation tape equidistant around the rim of the dish and since the rim is circular you need to have the tape strip width small to get it unwrinkled. These thin bits of tape act as microvibration dampers. It can be OK to just use the natural cushioning effect of the gelatin film itself as the dampener. But the presence of the flower and the gelatin in what is a confined enclosed space in the dish might cause a creep problem in the gelatin layer due to lack of humidity equilibrium which will take a bad long time to stabilize. So it may be best to shoot with the emulsion facing out.

The visible hologram is of course all of the anodized aluminium dish surface and not the flower. Anodized aluminium is the supreme reflector for this job because it does not depolarize the light or produce strong distracting specular reflections. Were you to use silver halide recording ..

(which I think you should initially) then parts of the flower might actually image themselves because of the much much shorter exposure time particularly if you use a TEA treated plate.

Jeff
