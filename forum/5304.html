Friday, December 30, 2005

BY JO COLLINS MATHIS

News Staff Reporter

Funeral services were held Thursday for Emmett Leith, a professor of electrical engineering and computer science at the University of Michigan who was widely known for his work with lasers and holographs. Leith died Dec. 23 at St. Joseph Mercy Hospital after falling ill the previous day at his home in Canton. He was 78.

Leith, one of the early innovators of holography in the 1960s, had been honored at a retirement party several weeks ago, although his official date of retirement was not until Saturday. He had worked at U-M for 52 years.

Leith is survived by June, his wife of 49 years, and their two daughters, Pam Wilder of San Jose, Calif., and Kim Leith of Baltimore, as well as three grandchildren.

Ed Wesly Tue Jan 03, 2006 1:02 pm #3
There couldn't have been a nicer guy to be the father of holography! Although Gabor got the Nobel Prize in 1971 for it, he left it on the shelf (the last paper of his I have in my collection was published in 1952, and the last paper on Gabor style holography in 1956) and it was really due to the flurry of activity in Ann Arbor that made the Prize Committee take notice of the new imaging process. And have Gabor come out of semi-retirement and move to America.

The first time I heard Dr. Leith present a paper was at the first International Symposium on Display Holography at Lake Forest College in 1982, where he spoke of the #1 problem of display holography from its inception, the "Search for the Hologenic Object." He showed he was not living in an Ivory Tower but knew what was going on in the street.

He could certainly come up with some ingenious schemes. He had a system for making diffraction gratings using white light! The idea was to have a first grating act as a sort of dispersing beamsplitter, sending two spectra out of it, then reuniting them at the holoplateholder. There were advantages to the modulation of the fringes in this type of set up, and I thought it must have been hard to align a system like this.

When I asked Emmett if this could be done by anyone in a lab, he said "Sure, I could do it with my eyes closed!" And with perfect comedic timing as if he were thinking twice about it, he followed with, "Well, maybe just one closed!"
