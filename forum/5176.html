Let's say the fringes are perfect. That is, a layer of crosslinked gelatin, a layer of uncrosslinked, a layer of crosslinked etc... In the water wash, the unhardened layer would dissolve out and the hardened areas would collapse onto one another. But this does not happen. The uncrosslinked area, which is washed out, retains its relative area in that space and we get a void, basically air. So we have to assume there is some kind of support holding the upper layer of hardened fringe a distance above the hardened fringe right below it. Columns in a parking garage if you will. Mostly air but supports there none the less.

Replacing the air space with water will result in only a difference of index of refraction, as stated, and the hologram will simply get dimmer due to less difference in index of refraction. This is true, as the hologram starts turning violet it does get considerably dimmer. The bluer to violet, the dimmer. But what would cause the hardened layers to get closer together. Do the supports that hold the two layers apart start to fail? If the supports hold the layers the same distance apart, the air is replaced by water and the hardened areas swell, the distance between the centers of the hardened areas still remains the same. You just get a thicker hardened fringe and a thinner unhardened area still the same distance apart.

So, I have to believe the key is in the supports that keep the fringes apart. With that being said, looking at those supports answers a lot of questions and also brings up many more.

Let's make a hologram and get those fringes again. We then put it in hardener, why, to make the supports. I imagine in the uncrosslinked area if there were no supports we would get no hologram. The hardener works by creating slight hardening, not only in the crosslinked but the uncrosslinked areas, thus creating the supports. We then rinse the hologram. In the crosslinked (by light) fringe, hardly no gel is lost at all. In the uncrosslinked (by light) fringe most of the gel is lost except for the small amount (supports) hardened by the post exposure hardening. This makes sense when someone long ago posted they harden with light exposure instead of what I use, Kodak fixer with hardener. Then when we dry the hologram in the alcohols, the alcohol replaces the water gradually giving the still somewhat soft supports in the unexposed areas, support so they don't collapse. Finally we are in the 100% alcohol and most of the water has been taken out of the exposed fringes and the unexposed fringes but has been replaced by alcohol. Then when we dry the hologram with air. It needs to be done in such a way that we dry the supports before they can collapse.

By allowing the hologram to sit in humidity when it is not fully dried, thus the supports are not completely hard, the humidity that gets to the supports causes then to collapse, shifting to the blue.

By reprocessing, the supports collapse thus decreasing the distance between fringes and shortening the replay wavelength.

The greenies are where the supports have either collapsed or have been broken because they were taken past their yield point.

Narrow band is processing in such a way that the supports all maintain the same support distance.

Broadband is processing in such a way that the supports are stretched to their max in one layer and hardened there and the supports in lower layers are allowed to collapse slightly and hardened there.

The supports are also where the spring analogy comes heavily into play, not in the constructive fringe but in the destructive fringe.

All this time I was thinking about hardened layers and unhardened layers and gave no thought to what actually supports the layers and keeps them apart. And it seems this is the key to processing DCG. Now every time I process I will have to keep those parking garage column supports in my mind.

To answer this point John I need to bring in the matter of photopolymer history and photoresists.

Years ago before Dupont and Polaroid developed their special photopolymers , it was a well known fact that photopolymer formulations were only able to record transmission gratings and not reflection gratings. Generally these involved the polymerization of a monomer under UV and the unexposed areas got washed away or simply drained away. Although recording transmission gratings (with fringes running perpendicularly to the substrate)worked to some extent , there were problems caused by the polymerization causing contraction during the exposure step and this had a limiting effect on getting high spatial frequencies because there was a self damaging effect going on on the light hardened bright fringes as they contracted and caused micromovement. A greater problem could often be that the process worked by a free-radical mechanism generated by the UV and these radicals travelled into the dark zones or nodes and caused polymerization unless their mean free path lengths were strongly curtailed by inhibitors.

Now let’s define the dark fringes as NODES (for NO Disturbance zone) and the light struck fringes as antinodes. The universally used photoresist used to make the nickel plated master shims for embossed holograms work the opposite way round by a pre-formed polymer coating becoming only soluble in the antinode regions after exposure. This solubilization process under UV does not cause contraction and volume change and does not cause free radical problems, so it does allow the highest spatial frequencies to be recorded as transmission gratings.

Now if you try to make reflection gratings with photoresist material , it simply does not work , the soft soluble zones simply disappear and don’t allow the reflection fringe structure to form.

Nor does it work with simple liquid monomer to solid polymer systems . It is like trying to make sandwiches with a filling of something runny like water.

So first Polaroid and then Dupont developed photopolymer systems where the soft nodes would not dissolve out , ( otherwise it would be like turning a pile of sandwiches back into a cut loaf).

So unlike transmission fringes standing up perpendicularly where you want to actually get rid of either the nodes or antinodes to give a surface-relief grating , in reflection gratings the nodes running parallel with the plate must be maintained but in a soft form to get as much contrast as possible with the hardened antinodes.

So the post exposure hardening step is necessary in you DCG process to stop the nodes running away. As a matter of fact if you use 250-300 bloom gelatin you can get away with not having a hardening step provided you keep the water and alcohol baths cold.

On the matter of your finding gelatin in the beaker after the water evaporated I still maintain that that is not the essential gelatin removed to form the voids, it is the lower molecular weight stuff in there as an impurity that has hit its melting point at your processing temperatures, leaving the much greater proportion of your higher melting point gelatin intact.

This is why I made a particular point in my previous post in this thread about the use of the purer expensive Sigma Aldrich 300 bloom gelatin . You can make very good DCG’s out of it without any

significant gelatin finishing up in the swelling water.

I guess you could prove this by removing the lower melting point from your gelatin by just making a film of it on clean glass then remove the film and leave it stirring all night in a large beaker of cold water at around 25-28 C so that the lower molecular weight “impurities” will elute out. Then you use your washed gelatin film for your normal process, I would perhaps bet you a crate of beer (subject to refereed conditions!) you will then be able to make good DCGs with no significant gelatin in the post exposure swelling water and possibly no “greenies” either.

"One Demonstration is worth 100,000 words"

John, It would be great for both of us and all DCGers if you could do the following experiment.

1. Coat down your usual gelatin DCG formulation on glass sheet but coat it much thicker so that as soon as it has gelled, you can immediately view the thickness of the coating edge on, cutting away any tapering or edge effects so that under a yellow light with the aid of one of your microscope objectives (x 10, or x 20 objectives should be fine) you can then make a rough measurement of your coating thickness. At this stage with the gel in its maximum swollen state even a ruler with half mm markings (500 microns) could be useful.

let us call the thickness of this swollen state........... t(0)

2. Now dry your coating as you do normally before exposure, allowing extra time for the extra thickness of course.

Could you meanwhile find a piece of film of known thickness say 20 micron that you could now place next to your dried coating to help give you an approximate thickness in its contracted state.

Let this thickness be ...............................................t(1)

3) Now cut the sheet in two , these 2 pieces are now going to be made to represent the gelatin state in the centre of a Light fringe (antinode) and Dark fringe (node) respectively. Lets call them sheets L and D respectively.

4) Now give sheet L a thorough zapping with your laser and try not to form any holographic fringes at all! A good idea might be to put WET black paper on the back of the glass to cut out the air interface and create movement. You need to get sheet L into the same state as would be found in the center of your light fringe. We know that UV exposure alone causes contraction (I remember that old DCG pioneer Richard Rallison told me that occasionally his glass cracked during exposure ) but this contraction is too small for you to measure but for theory sake we will call it ...............t(1').

5)Now process your sheets L and D together with your hardener/fix stuff and the water swelling step. Then measure the thickness of

the swollen films. The difference should be considerable

Let's call thickness of sheet L ..............................t(2)

Let's call thickness of sheet D ............................ t(3)

The difference values of t(1),t(2) and t(3) answer a lot of what you are asking.

6) Finally do your desiccation step in the alcohols allowing that it is going to be much longer than usual but we could do with a final value for the thoroughly

dried thickness for the discussion,

Let's call this thickness......................................... t(4) (I would be surprised if the value of t4 differed between L and D)

Discussion

It is useful to compare the value of t(0) and t(3) against t(1) to see just what effect your hardener solution is having without any involvement of light hardening.

I imagine that t(0) will be something like 5 times greater than t(1) whereas maybe your hardening solution will cause t(3) to be only 3 times greater than t(1) .---This could be a terribly wrong guesstimate.

Now to deal with my suggestion of a stretched strand model in the fully processed DCG hologram.

If t(3) is say 3 times greater than t(1) but t(2) is only slightly greater than t(1) then that means that the dark fringe gelatin wants to contract to a third of its swollen volume but cannot do so because it is bonded on each side by hardened gelatin in a highly complex fringe network and this hardened gelatin

represented by t(2) hardly wants to contract at all. So that leaves the soft t(3) gelatin no option but to stretch across the adjacent hard fringes and be in a strained condition which finally gets rigidified by being completely desiccated in the final alcohol bath. The swollen soft gelatin fights with alcohol over its water content. Isopropyl alcohol is particularly effective because it has a high affinity for water but gelatin has very low affinity for that alcohol . Therefore the water attracts the alcohol into the soft layer and the alcohol forms pockets around the strands and these pockets become air filled voids as the alcohol is driven out by your warm air blower.

I am hoping you will be able to actually get some sort of rough measurements to replace conjectures such as "the dark fringe gelatin contracting to a third of its swollen volume".

The answer I make here may sound fuzzy but I do think it centers around the truth.

When the dichromate ion gets activated by light to oxidise something it goes through some intermediate steps before

it finishes up as the stable Cr+++ ion which hardens the gelatin .In that previous long thread on the SHDCG that Joe started some months ago, this was brought up

and Martin refers to a paper on the intermediate steps between going from Dichromate which is “CrV1” (i.e. 6 electrons missing from the raw element Chromium) through to

CrV, CrIV till finally the very stable CrIII.

So when you hit the relatively dry dichromate-gelatin film with your laser beam , it does’nt just jump straight to the CrIII state crosslinking the gelatin. It forms partial bonds to the gelatin I

believe which serve to hold it in place while you swell the gelatin up with water. It then goes onto react and finish up with the CrIII. But this takes TIME but I do not know how much time.

Meanwhile all the gelatin swells up regardless of whether it is part of a node or antinode. The situation is made extra complicated by the fact that you use FIX (thiosulfate) as well as hardener and this will react quite rapidly to speed up the reduction of dichromate and its intermediates. At the end of the day your system works impressively well if it’s a good day!.
