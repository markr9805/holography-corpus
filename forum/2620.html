First, this post is to start a thread for comments on Jeff Blythe's article 'The Impossible Holographic Object', recently added to 'The Holographer' at:

[http://www.holographer.org/articles/hg00017/hg00017.php](http://www.holographer.org/articles/hg00017/hg00017.php)

I'd recommend you read this short piece before proceeding.

In this article, Jeff writes about his (unsuccessful) attempts to holograph a corner cube (CC).

The article seems to contradict the commonly held belief that 'a hologram of an object completely reconstructs the wavefronts reflected from the object'; that theoretically it is impossible to tell a hologram from the original object.

According to the description, the hologram was made in a simple Denisyuk setup. So I'd assume Jeff was using a diverging beam. The thing about the typical illumination beam is that it appears to all originate from a single point in space. Even if the beam is perfectly collimated, it still appears to come from a single point, just infinitely far away. So I'd argue that when illuminated from THAT point, his hologram of a corner cube should work - but we can't do that easily, as it's hard to get an eye and a light source to occupy the same point.

My suggestion is that because the CC was not illuminated from other points in space, it's hologram can't 'know' what to do with light that originates from those other points.

Sounds like a great project for a PhD thesis (maybe in Mathematics)!

Tony Tue Jul 13, 2004 2:01 pm #8
Here's an interesting thought experiment:

If you make a Denisyuk hologram with a perfectly collimated beam (flat wavefronts) of a perfectly flat mirror, and the mirror is perfectly parallel to the film plane, I argue that there will be no way to detect, from the finished hologram, how far from the film the mirror originally was. In the finished hologram, the Bragg planes will all be perfectly parallel to the film, spaced 1/2 wavelength apart. This will act as a dielectric mirror at the film plane. There would be no difference in these planes as you change the distance to the mirror.

Now, if you repeated the experiment, this time with a diverging beam, there would now be information contained in the hologram about the distance from the mirror to the film, because of the additional amount of divergence in the beam as it traveled from film plane to the mirror and back again.

So this experiment suggests further that the illumination function is important in providing information about the object in the finished hologram.
Dinesh Tue Jul 13, 2004 2:10 pm #9
It seems to me that when you shoot the CC as a single beam Denisyuk, the reference beam hits the CC and retro-reflects from one angle only. The subsequent Bragg planes are then formed at lambda/2 with the grating vector pointing at the bisection of the two beams for any given direction. Thus, for the corner cube, you have three sets of Bragg planes, each set orthogonal to all the other sets. In effect, a coordinate system of Bragg planes. At the "correct" Bragg angle, taking into account any swelling or distortion of the emulsion, the Bragg planes replay the object wavfront as a reflection of the reconstruction wavefront, including wavefront distortion due to aberrations from reference mismatch. In other words, if the exact same reference were used to reconstruct, the three independant Bragg planes would diffract into three independant analogues of the reconstruction. If the reconstruction were not matched, ie the reconstruction wavefront were a diverging "Jeff's eye" wavefront, then the Bragg planes would create three independant converging wavefronts which would reconstruct "Jeff's eye" but with negative magnification and in one direction only. Any other direction would be off-Bragg and would not reconstruct. The color shift is probably due to the angular increase in Bragg angle, the |K-k| vector in Kogelnik, hence the "Ventian Blind Effect"

Obviously, to completely reproduce the CC effect, you'd need to be able to diffract the reconstruction wavefront independant of the reconstruction vector. This would mean a transmission hologram capable of replaying in reflection mode. Of course such a scheme would give a lot of dispersion.

This then brings several interesting points. Clearly, Bragg selectivity is enhanced by emulsion thickness, so what if you did this in DCG (stop panting, John!)? By varying the emulsion thickness, in effect by reducing it to a point where both surface variation and the mod of the grating vector were approximately equal, wouldn't you then be able to get omni-directional diffraction with minimum dispersion?

Jeff/Tony:

I agree that the interference lines from a 40X objective reflecting from mirror would be a Fresnel Zone plate - sort of. The iso-phase lines from a point source at a distance Z_0 from the origin is:

phi = (1/(pilambdaZ_0))f(sqr rt(r))

for a plate at the origin.

At the mirror, the reflection would cause a virtual source appearing to come from a 40X objective at the same distance (Z_0) behind the plate, creating the same set of iso-phase lines. Since these iso-phase lines are circles with the nth radius at sqr rt(n) away, you'd get a Zone plate. However, if the holographic plate is at some distance, d away from the mirror, then the isophase circles from the 40X to the hologram is now at (Z_0-d) and the virtual 40X is now at (Z_0 +d) and so each set of iso-phase circes would shift. I'm not sure, without actually calculating it, that you'd get s zone plate anymore. By which I mean the iso-phase circles would not be at root(n) away from the center. Unless, of course, the mirror position co-incided with the hologram position. You would get a series of circles, just not a Frasnel Zone Plate
