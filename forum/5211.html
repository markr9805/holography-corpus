Joe Farina Sat Nov 26, 2005 7:14 pm #5
Thanks, Colin, for pointing out those references, I had forgotten about them. I saw that 1968 paper in Applied Physics Letters which I don't have, so I will look for it.

Most of the papers I've collected on DCG over the years are in a single binder, arranged in chronological order. It's interesting to go through them once in a while.

The next two I have are:

"Preparation of Dichromated Gelatin Films for Holography" by Brandes/Francois/Shankoff," Applied Optics, November 1969

and

"The Mechanism of Hologram Formation in Dichromated Gelatin," by Curran/Shankoff, Applied Optics, July 1970

The former gives lots of practical details, and here is one interesting quote:

"....The criteria were the fastest holographic speed commensurate with minimal gelatin precipitation or whitening during development for a gelatin with good commercial quality control and availability. Thus pigskin vs. calfskin gelatin, acid-processed vs. base-processed, low pH vs. high pH, and low bloom strength vs. high bloom strength were all considered in the selection. It was established that the only parameter which exerts a gross holographic effect is bloom strength. Commonly available gelatin ranges from 75-250 in bloom strength with quality control first being assured at 100-125 bloom. Baker USP gelatin at 125 bloom, readily available, with good quality control assurances, is the best of several tested for holography with respect to photographic speed and optical quality."

The latter paper includes photomicrographs of the DCG structure in a reflection grating.

Ed Wesly Tue Nov 29, 2005 5:19 pm #15
Although I have been collecting all these DCG papers, I am trying to figure out what was the inspiration for Shankoff to attempt using this system as a holographic recording material.

The hardening of organics by bichromates (going a little 19th century) under the influence of light was discovered by Mungo Ponton in 1840, and by 1855 the gum bichromate printing process was worked out. Light hardened gum arabic doped with dichromate, rendering it insoluble in water to make the shadows in the print, while the unhardened gum washed out in the highlights. The gum was colored with pigment to render tones.

I have read about unpigmented bichromated layers being used in other configurations as part of an ink printing process, the Woodburytype, and other miscellaneous 'types.

Closer to home, I have an "R. W. Wood Replica Grating", (ca. WWI?) and although in tatters, it looks like a dichromated layer. I have also heard of, but don't have any citations, of a cat named Quincke (sic?) who was replicating ruled gratings with DCG, all this before holography.

So does anyone have any more insights into the impetus of using this stuff for recording holos?
Martin Wed Nov 30, 2005 6:06 am #16
> Ed Wesly: Although I have been collecting all these DCG papers, I am trying to figure out what was the inspiration for Shankoff to attempt using this system as a holographic recording material.

So am I. Not meant to belittling Shankoff but I also suspect there's a missing link between 19th century dichromated systems and modern DCG. Of course there's been Lippmann photography – Lippmann himself already used dichromated colloids. From the documents I've seen, I understand he had a different approach to enhance index modulation though(basically, forming silver halide grains in the crosslinked layer).

So Shankoff's novelty seems to be in the introduction of the solvent dehydrating step. However, there's a scarcely known tradition of making transmission gratings and interference filters based on Lippmann photography. I believe it started with Ives around 1910.

> The hardening of organics by bichromates (going a little 19th century) under the influence of light was discovered by Mungo Ponton in 1840, and by 1855 the gum bichromate printing process was worked out. Light hardened gum arabic doped with dichromate, rendering it insoluble in water to make the shadows in the print, while the unhardened gum washed out in the highlights. The gum was colored with pigment to render tones.

Interestingly, there's a paper by Poitevin around 1850, in which he refers to a French guy, who was said to have experimented with light-sensitive dichromate systems as early as 1820 or so.

> I have read about unpigmented bichromated layers being used in other configurations as part of an ink printing process, the Woodburytype, and other miscellaneous 'types.

If you read French, Gallica ([http://gallica.bnf.fr/](http://gallica.bnf.fr/)) is a huge resource for early photography/lithography.
