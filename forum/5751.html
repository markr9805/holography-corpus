Ed Wesly Fri Aug 11, 2006 2:02 pm #4
The key to the success for this multi-wavelength stuff is to really know your materials by characterizing their dynamic range by exposure tests with development time (and temperature) held constant, for each separate wavelength. And also standardize on your replay source, since the sun with its higher color temperature has a higher proportion of green compared to red. Since most holos are displayed with incandescent lighting, standardize on that.

The trick which I have explained here previously is to make test strips by varying the exposure time. A more detailed explanation is in my CWC2 article posted on this forum.

Find what is the threshold, where there barely is an image, and saturation, where the image is bright but buried under the scatter noise. Since you give figures of 1000 and 1200 uj/cm^2, I would shoot single wavelength expo's on one plate using the cardboard quadrant device of 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 energy doses. And if that is not far enough try some more at either end, always retaining one of the last exposures; i.e. if all the above were over exposed, reshoot at 32, 64, 125 and 250.

Once you have a feel for what the plates (I'm assuming you are using Slavich PFG-03C, but this is the routine for anything!) are capable of, then try mixing. Pick a decently bright green exposure, not the most blown out, but a half of that as a background. Then put an exposure test series of red on top of that. You might see a green image in the yellow zone at the lowest red expo, then yellow, then orange when the red overpowers.

After you get a good working proportion of green to red, you can tinker by jacking up both exposures by the same proportionate amount to gain brightness.

This balancing act is tricky, and I wouldn't trust anybody's exposure dose numbers as the correct one. You have to work out your own numbers through trial and error. Keep good notes on well-regimented experiments. I know, I've been there! I once made at LFC test expo's with all the laser wavelengths there of my standard test object on Agfa, Ilford, Bulgarian and DuPont materials. You need this basic database before getting fancy.

Hans Sat Aug 12, 2006 6:41 am #7
Perhaps the green facets of your cube fluoresce in your laser light. Only a fraction of the original wavelength would be reflected towards your plate, causing the green facets to be dark in the hologram. The rest of the reflection would appear very bright visually, but useless to make fringes for the hologram.
JohnFP Mon Aug 14, 2006 11:44 am #8
alackofcolor, you describe the green as being dull. Is is possible that the green has quite a bit of red (well actually white wich will reflect the red laser light) such that in white light you are seeing red and green in the green spot but in the laser, you are filtering all but just the green.

While I was researching the Spectral Sensitivity of Paints, I found that a lot of colors we see as very pure and bright are that way because they have white in them. This white reflects all colors. So I think what is happening is the green on the cube has red or white in it in a small proportion. When using red and green you are giving it much to much red, thus in white light it looks dull green.

In your case, add more green exposure and cut back on the red. Ed suggestion would be the best to follow and if I understand him correctly make a grid of exposures. Do some horizontal slits (1 or 2 cm) of red with different exposures and some vertical slits of green with different exposures all on the same plate. Use a nice pure white object (a white painted flat surface).

Tom B. Tue Aug 15, 2006 5:56 am #12
I recall noticing a sort of rough organic "orange peel" texture to the images on some PFG-03M plates that behaved like this. I attributed this to some variation in the emulsion shrinkage when processed so that different areas had slightly different center reconstruction wavelengths. Slightly tilting or rotating the plate changes the Bragg angle to favor different reconstruction wavelengths which would explain your observation.

Other plates from the same batch were OK - I have no idea what caused it. Maybe if the plates were old (or just poorly made) the air side of the emulsion could form a tough skin which would swell differently from the bottom, causing wrinkling or irregular stretching of the emulsion during processing?
