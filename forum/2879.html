By "media" I'm assuming you mean the material and not the medium. That is, film as opposed to glass rather than silver halide film as opposed to DCG or polymer or something.

The obvious advantage is that you can't cut yourself with it while in the dark. It's easily cut up and distributed with just a pair of scissors, you don't need glass cutting skills. It's also much cheaper than glass. A lot of hobbyists use film for this reason.

The disadvantage to using film is that it has to be firmly mounted. You have to sandwich the film between two glass plates or onto a charged plate or some mounting system so that it doesn't move. As you may have learnt, the holographic medium cannot move by more than half a fringe during the exposure. If the exposure is, let's say, 10 seconds and you're shooting fringes of about a micron, it can't move by more than half a micron (0.0005mm or 0.000002 in) during the 10 second exposure. Also, film sometimes curls, so you need to develop it while keeping it flat in the tray or use a lot of developing chemicals.

If you mean silver halide as opposed to any other medium, the main reason is that it has very low sensitivity. You don't need a very powerful laser. The shorter exposures also mean that you have a lot of leeway in your table stability. The shorter the exposure, the less stable your table can afford to be. Red sensitive silver halide is easily and relatively cheaply available, you can use a relatively cheap HeNe laser. Most other materials are green and/or blue sensitive and need more powerful and more expensive lasers. The disadvantage of silver halide is that it has to be wet processed in some chemicals that you wouldn't want on your food. Also, you have to know which chemicals and mix them in the right proportions. There's no bottle of "Holography Developer #3" on the market yet. At least, I'm assuming that Integraf does not just sell it's developer without the kit. Talking of which, Inegraf (link at top of page) sells a completely self-contained holographic kit for students.
