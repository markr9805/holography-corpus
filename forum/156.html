Dinesh Wed Feb 09, 2011 12:37 pm #4
It's pretty good as it stands. If you use spatial filter assemblies, you may want to put down somewhere what objective you used with what pinhole. This gives you an idea of the beam divergence for a particular shot. One thing I was curious about: You have "reference power" and the "ratio". How do you get the ratio? Usually, it's not easy to get the object power because most meters have a small aperture and the object beam is usually pretty scattered. So, pointing the meter at the object light give far too low a value.

One other thing I find useful is a diagram of a layout, or a photograph. In this way, if you do have to repeat the same geometry, you know pretty exactly where the various optics were placed. This makes it very easy to reset everything. We have a camera mounted over the lab and connected to the office network. When we finish with a project, we snap a picture of the table from any one of three computers and write all the distances involved (spatial filter to object etc) and relevant angles on the pic.

Dinesh - I absolutely agree in having both photographs and layouts of table set-ups - it hugely simplifies the task of equalising object and reference beam lengths

My sand table is about 6 foot X 5 foot and I have marked the top of the box in felt tip pen D (depth) 1 - 10 at 6" intervals W (width) 1 - 12 also at 6" intervals - the same markings are on each opposite side of the box.

On a piece of card covered with thick clear polythene to a scale of 1" = 6" I have drawn the outline of the table with table markings shown - the Planner.

I have made cardboard 6 : 1 scale models of each of the larger components used on the table eg plateholders, spatial filter, collimating lenses.

On this covered card I can position and stick with BluTac each component I plan to use - lenses become small pieces of BluTac and with a piece if string measure my beam lengths and position the cardboard cut-outs to suit my design.

Once designed the actual optical components can be correctly placed in the sand from the indicated position on the Planner.

It's not perfect but it's pretty good photo attached

Dinesh Mon Feb 21, 2011 1:11 pm #13
All of that sounds pretty good. Must have taken a lot of work!

At one time I started writing a piece of software which would allow you to "create a table", ie the software would ask you for the table dimensions. Then, the software would draw the table on the monitor, put a grid on it and draw a long rectangle to represent the laser and ask have for the beamsplitter position somewhere on the table, where it would place a marker, and the plate position, where it'd put another marker. The coord positions of the BS and the plate would be noted with respect to a coord origin at one corner of the table. The software would then place a first transfer mirror and a blue line from first transfer mirror to beamsplitter (I think it was blue, can't remember the exact colours as this was all written in 1990). At this point, you'd place markers at the optics positions, ie mirrors and lenses. Back then (pre-mouse days), you'd use the arrow keys to position a marker and hit one of the F's - if memory serves it was F2 for ref and F3 for object beams (F1 was always reserved for 'help'). Each time you pressed the appropriate F number, it would place a marker and a running total of the distance from the beamsplitter to the optic just positioned would update, giving you a running total of the beam path. Once you'd got to the plate, the software would give a total distance for the ref, or obj, beam depending on the F number. It would then change colour, replace itself at the BS and start a fresh count, as you went around the "table" placing markers for the other beam. As you placed your markers this time around, there would be a runnung total of the distance, and a running total of the difference between the present position of the marker and the total distance of the other beam. Finally, when you got to the plate with the second beam, there'd be two beams on the "table", a total distance of each beam and a difference figure. If this difference distance was about a third of the coherence length of your laser then you were OK. A third because the fringe visibility against path difference is a sort of gaussian and I was trying to get within a sigma of the coherence-visibilty plot plot. At this point, you could ask the software for the coords of the optics, relative to a corner of the table, and place the appropriate optics in place. With this software you could "plot" an entire table in a matter of about 10 minutes, get the coords from the software, plonk down the optics and be assured that all paths were correct. You'd be ready to go within 20 - 30 minutes no matter how complex your setup.

This part of the software had all been written down (I have it somewhere, but it's all written in C for DOS. I've never converted it to a windows format). the next phase was a) to have multiple object beams and b) to have addressable coords on the table itself, so you could place all the optics on one edge of the table, connect the softtware to the table, design the table and hit "enter". The software would then move the optics to it's appropriate positions automatically. However to do this, I'd have to learn LISP, which never happened.
