> Dutchelm05: The reason why I'm so curious about this is that the only way to tell you have done everything right it seems is when the final hologram is processed.

I would have to agree, Tony. Of course it would be better if there were signposts along the way, but in that respect, I don't think we are very fortunate when it comes to DCG. But you are definitely on the right track with your testing processes. The scientific literature can do as much harm as good, maybe more. The important thing is to trust your own observations, and try to get as good a handle on the variables as possible. Once that is done, I would approach the problem empirically, and not worry about the scientific literature, or even science for that matter. By empirically, I mean just test, observe, make notes, etc. In other words, I would suggest practicing the "craft" of DCG, and borrow those elements of "science" that serve the craft. If it doesn't serve the craft then it's not only useless, but downright harmful.

I can't remember very clearly, but it seems that McGrew was able to note the differences in exposed/hardened DCG versus unexposed/soft simply by running his finger across the emulsion while it was wet. The soft areas would swell to a large extent, while the hard (exposed) areas would resist swelling. I think this was stated in the literature, if not by McGrew, then by someone else. So it was simply a "tactile" observation: the ridges, bumps, or whatever could be felt by running a finger across them.

The "blue" DCG I had mentioned before is rather faint and difficult to notice. It actually seems purple for some reason, and it seems like a very light dyed stain. It should be slightly visible in the hologram I sent you, but it's not present in either of the two you sent me. I have no explanation for it. By the way, the Santa hologram you sent is just as bright (if not brighter) compared to my DCG holograms, for areas of the object that are the same distance from the emulsion. DCG can be tremendously bright for object areas that are nearly touching the emulsion (take for example the ubiquitous coin holograms) but brightness and clarity (for a simple Denisyuk hologram, that is) will rapidly fall off as the object gets deeper.

Well, I wouldn't say NO predictor.

Visual inspection of the coating is a great benifit.
