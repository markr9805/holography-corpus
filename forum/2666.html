Dinesh Sat Aug 14, 2004 7:07 pm #8
[quote:3da62c6255]Dinesh ol' buddy, sometimes I think you type faster than you think! [/quote:3da62c6255]

Jeez, thanks Bob! Nobody else thinks I actually think, they think I just make this stuff up on the fly!

When you want an extremely narow bandwidth and you want the reflectivity plot in a photospectrometer to literally go "straight up" and come "straight down", as opposed to oscillating a little on the rise and on the fall, it becomes extremely important to have the statistical distribution of the fringes spacing to be as small as possible. Basically, the mean deviation of the fringe spacing broadens the bandwidth and so contributes to a loss in photopic and (especially!) scotopic efficiencies. In order to minimse the fringe distribution, you need to be very careful about reflections off the three (or more) interfaces - air/glass, glass/emulsion, and emulsion/air - plus whatever index matching fluid you're using. Even if you use index matching, unless you use some sort of "graded" index matching fluid, the actual index of the material needs to be an average of the two indices you're trying to match., which also has minute interface reflections. In the project I was involved in, I needed and OD of over 4 with a photopic of over 80% and a scotopic of over 60% - pretty extreme specs! In this situation, I needed to match and calculate extremely small variations in the rms value of the surface, so that I could take into effect reflections back from the glass causing very small grating vector distributions away from the desired grating vector. The difference in n and the effect of the n on reflections in s and p between the various interfaces and plot pretty good reflectivity curves for s and p for the various materials. As John initially asked: [quote:3da62c6255] Has anyone studied the polarization effects of the glass they are using for emulsion coating[/quote:3da62c6255] I mentioned that for a particular HOE spec I onvce needed, I did have to study the "polarisation effect", in that I needed to know exactly what the reflectivity of the interfaces was under different polarisations. Remember, also, that no beam of light is exactly p or exactly s, but a statistical mixture. I also studied the effect of the statistical ratio of s/p on the reflectivity.

I'm happy to state that all this care, along with a few tricks that, if no one minds I won't mention (still haven't seen any papers on the technique I used back then), I managed an OD of 4.5 with a bandwidth of 25nm along the entire reflectivity plot (straight up and straight down - no "bounce", no FWHM. The entire curve was "full width"!) with a phtopic of 85% and a scotopic of about 60% odd. Never could improve on the scotopic. This was in 1988, maybe such results are routine now, but back then, if I say so myself, it was pretty good!

Of course, for display, I don't take anywhere near this kind of care. I just shine a light on the cat/ gecko/skull and zap it. Half the time I have no idea what the polarisation of my beam even is. Actually, that's not quite true. A kind soul has given me a half wave plate on "permanent loan" and now I know that my polarisation is "sorta" vertical. I use s because of the greater efficency and the fact that you don't need to worry about woodgrain in pure phase materials.

Dinesh Mon Aug 16, 2004 1:02 pm #10
In fact that's pretty much what I did. The factors that affect local variations in fringe density, spacing and linearity are mainly the angle between the beams, the ratio and the beam divergence (which gives the "spread" of the fringes about the mean). This is in turn affected by reflectivity, polarisation and thckness which need to be known very, very accurately, which means knowing the exact refractive index of the glass and the exact variation of the glass surface (throws the plane of incidence off). These effects are very, very small for most display work and you can pretty much ignore them for off-the-shelf glass. Even if you wanted to know this kind of data, off-the-shelf glass is never measured to this level of precision anyway. You need to get research grade glass, which can be fairly expensive. If tou look at off-the-shelf glass with a polariser you probably won't see much diffference. Remenber that a polariser has an error of a few degrees and a "blue filter". The HN figure of a polariser is an indication of the filtration loss in percentage, mostly in blue. So when you get extinction, you're blocking off the reds, greens and oranges to within a few degrees. The variations in reflectivity caused by polarisation in off-the-shelf glasses are much smaller than the accuracy of a polariser.

For technical work, these small difference have to be taken into account, depending on the specs. In these case, you need to basically use statistical methods. Research grade glass has data on rms height variation- root mean square variation of height above an "average" - as well as refractive index to about 6 figures with error (sometimes). Of course, it's not all in the glass. You need to make sure that the beam angle and plane of incidence are very exact. If you use lenses and mirrors, then the lenses and mirrors also have to be research grade. Mirrors, for example, have the rms in terms of lambda. You'll see specs such as "rms < lambda/10".

BobH Mon Aug 16, 2004 3:58 pm #11
> Sometimes when I look at a plate of glass, specifically a large window pane I see blotches of dark spots. I am usually looking through another pane or the reflection of one pane in another.

I've not seen this effect in window pane (except maybe in the early '70s ), so I can't comment on its contribution to variations in color of a DCG recording. I can say that I've never seen polarization effects in any of the glass I've used in laser light. If you're getting color variations in your images, I'd suggest looking elsewhere. Small variations in total power at the recording plane, for example. It's pretty difficult to measure it accurately (even with a proper detector) when shooting Denisyuk holograms.

Variations in coating thickness, clamping stress in the plateholder causing weird subtle fringes, and contaminated chemicals will all have much greater consequences than polarization effects in the glass (in my opinion). I'm not a glass chemist. With the amount of time spent on making DCG holograms, I personally wouldn't skimp on the glass I used.
