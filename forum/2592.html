From Sam's Laser Faq, HeNe section

[http://www.repairfaq.org/sam/laserhen.htm#henhlt](http://www.repairfaq.org/sam/laserhen.htm#henhlt)

quote:

"(From: Lynn Strickland ([stricks760@earthlink.net](mailto:stricks760@earthlink.net)).)

Typical failure mechanism in a HeNe is cathode sputtering -- seldom gas leakage in the newer (like since 1983) tubes. Shelf life is stated to be about 10 years, but it's not uncommon at all to see HeNe lasers built in the early 1980's that still meet full spec.

Interesting lifetime note - it used to be that you left a HeNe 'on' at all times to prolong life. Since hard-sealing, you should turn it off while not in use. If it's a 20,000 hour tube, and you only turn it on for a few hundred hours a year, it will last a heck of a long time. Not uncommon at all for the HeNe to outlive several power supplies. The larger diameter tubes tend to last longer, but it also depends on fill pressure and operating current (higher fill-pressure tubes last longer). The typical 5 mW red HeNe will commonly live to 40k to 50k operating hours.

As for cathode sputtering, the tube has an aluminum cathode that is 'pickled' during the production process to add a layer of oxidation about 200 microns thick. The oxidation layer prevents aluminum from being bombarded away from the cathode during plasma discharge. As the tube ages, the oxide layer is depleted until aluminum is exposed. Sputtered aluminum can stick to the mirror, causing power decline, or to the inside of the glass envelope, causing the discharge to arc internally. This arcing, if allowed to continue for a period of time, will also cook the power supply. A tube with no oxidation layer on the cathode will die in about 200 hours of use. OR, once the oxidation layer is depleted, the tube will die in about 200 hours. This is why a HeNe life curve is usually pretty flat, then quickly degrading to nothing over about a 200 hour period.

"

BobH says:

I'm a little confused. How can He leak out of a tube which is at a very much lower pressure than the atmosphere around it?

This is a semi-permeable membrane phenomenon which does'nt follow ordinary gas pressure laws. So in theory over time the very low pressure inside the laser tube goes down still further as the major constituent He passes by random processes through the glass and cant be replaced unless there is a very Helium rich atmosphere outside that randomly causes He atoms to wander back inside the tube.

It is seen more familiarly in osmotic pressure demos where small salt ions and water molecules pass through a semipermeable membrane in a solution but large molecules cant get through the membrane and this creates a pressure difference.

Besides not knowing of any such mechanism, I did some back-of-an-envelope calculations for velocity of He in both cases. Using

(1/2)m(v^2) = (3/2)kT

where k = Boltzmann's constant

T = temperature

m = mass

If you assume that the plasma in the tube is about 60C, reasonable I think since the water for cooling Argons does not boil or steam and I've got to think that 10mW HeNe doesn't run as hot as a 5W Argon, and the mass of a He molecule is 41.6((10^(-27)) kg (mass of a proton), then

velocity of He with laser off ~ 1375 m/s

velocity of He with laser on ~ 1477 m/s

Ratio,

vel of He (laser off)/vel of He (laser on) ~ 1375/1477 ~ 0.93

It would appear that the velocity of the He is pretty much the same. I also did some calculations on expansion due to the laser being on and hot (again, using 60C).
