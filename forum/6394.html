MichaelH Tue Aug 14, 2007 8:53 am #7
Ahhh. I believe this is normal.

Several years ago Lon Moore recounted to several of us that he increased the brightness of his H2's by stopping down the H1 using a horizontal slit.

I've been doing this same thing since then with verifiable results. I typically use a cylinder lens to create the "slit" with the added benefit that I waste very little reconstruction light when making my H2's and so shorten my exposures.

By limiting vertical parallax to no more than you really need (most people don't jump up and down when looking at a hologram) you decrease the crosstalk and increase the final brightness.

Try putting a 5" high horizontal slit over your H1 and see if the final H2 is as bright as the original. I'll bet you a hologram that it will be.
Colin Kaminski Tue Aug 14, 2007 2:14 pm #8
The master Lon was showing us was for a handheld hologram. For this only a small vertical parallax is needed. However on my holograms that are wall hung I find getting the geometry right so viewers of different heights quite challenging and I often use a larger slit than I can for a hand held hologram.

JohnFP Thu Aug 16, 2007 5:30 pm #14
It sounds to me like there is shift in the fringe angle for the different parts of the H1.

A simple test would be to take your lenses out of the reference beam. Then shine the raw laser at different parts (ie. center and the far four sides) and note the brightness of the real image on a white card. Maintain reference geometry by placeing a adjustable mirror at the point where the lens or spatial filter is so you can move the beam around the plate with the adjustable mirror while keeping the original reference angles the same for all part of the beam.

If you want the center of your hologram the brightest. Find the angle where the raw beam through the middle creates the brightest real image on the card and use that angle no matter what the image brightness on the white card tells you when using the entire expanded beam.

Just my thoughts....
JohnFP Thu Aug 16, 2007 5:34 pm #15
Also, just for Ha-Ha's....

Illuminate your hologram with the entire reference beam and look at the real image floating in space through a polarizer and rotate the polarizer and see if anything weird is happening. You could flip the plate around and look at the virtual also the same way.
