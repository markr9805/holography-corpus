JohnFP Thu Mar 13, 2008 11:11 am #2
It looks to me like you do not have a vibration isolation system in place. Without this you can pretty much forget try to do split beam work. Remember, the set for waves that act as a reference beam and the set of waves that act as an object beam have to remain perfectly still for the duration of the exposure. This is especially critical for reflection holograms.

I would suggest placing your "table" on four small auto inner tubes but in looking at the mass of your table (low) and seeing it is made of wood I don't think this will help as much as my next suggestion.

First let me explain where the two beams, reference and object, come from in a single beam reflection hologram. Read this. Also note that in this short write up, the plate is leaning against the object. This is most important for you because you do not have a vibration isolation table and it is imperative that the object and plate do not move with respect to one another. [http://www.holograms3d.com/MakingHologr ... logram.htm](http://www.holograms3d.com/MakingHolograms/what_is_a_hologram.htm)

In the above example, since the plate is tilted back (leaning against the object) and the laser is perpendicular to the plate, the plate is really getting as reference beam that seems to be coming from the bottom. When you view this hologram you need to have the light coming from behind you and below your eyes or flip the plate over and view the hologram upside down with the more conventional overhead lighting.

If you wish to take it one step further and make a hologram with overhead lighting with the object being right side up, the following configuration allows this while still maintaining that stable contact between the object and the plate allowing you to still perform this without the isolation table.

[http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustration ... Single.jpg](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/SImpleSingle.jpg)

This shows how you should rest your plate right on the object. Choosing an object that supports the plate wholly works much better then using the supports in the illustration. I have found many small objects that can be placed on their backs and they are very stable (they do not roll or want to move) and they can also support a plate without the plate rocking or sliding. The only trick to this geometry is you need to mount your laser or a transfer mirror overhead so the beam can be coming down to the plate. Notice the black tape on the leading edge (the edge facing the beam) of the plate. This is to insure the plate does not get light into the edge of the plate where it will reflect back on forth inside the glass to give undesirable refractions.

Here is an easy way to mount your laser and lens and possibly a transfer mirror to accommodate the above geometry. Again you can do this on your existing table with no inner tubes or vibration isolation system in place.

![OverheadLightObjectOnBack.JPG](https://holographyforum.org/forum/download/file.php?id=6078)
Tom B. Fri Mar 14, 2008 6:10 am #3
I agree with John that the reflection setup shown is unlikely to be stable enough. If you are doing this in a school, there is the additional problem that there will be a LOT of vibration due to traffic, people, and air conditioning/heating machinery etc. Try setting up a Michaelson interferometer (you already have the needed beamsplitter and mirrors) to see how bad the vibration situation is. See for example [http://www.holowiki.com/index.php/Interferometry](http://www.holowiki.com/index.php/Interferometry)

A single beam reflection setup as described by John is much less sensitive to vibration, but even then results will be poor if you have too much - if so, you will have to find a quieter location or put some additional effort into vibration isolation.

Also, it wasn't clear from your pictures what you are using to control the exposure. Turning the laser on and off will not do, as the laser takes some time (possibly several minutes or more) to stabilize after being turned on. Instead, people usually let the laser warm up for a reasonable time with a card in front of it to block the light, and then gently and smoothly lift and replace the card to make the exposure.

JohnFP Mon Mar 17, 2008 3:00 pm #12
It sounds as if your object moved thus resulting in no reflection hologram.

From the rainbow colors you mention it also sounds like you did in fact make a hologram of your lense holder but it is a transmission because both the reference beam and the ojbect lighting beam are both hitting the same side of the plate. Viewing one beam at a time (card the other one off) and with a white card in the plate holder, make sure only the reference beam is hitting the plate. Use blockers strategically placed to insure this.

From the shadow description, it sounds like there is light coming from the laser or reflecting off something upstread of the lense holder, thus making a shadowgram. Again, check each beam individually and block off everything but light that you want where. Blockers are easy to make, Just thumb tack a piece of black foam board standing up to a piece of wood. Then place these all around the beams. You will see what you block on the blocker. Again, do one beam at a time and have a white card in the plateholder.

Also, make sure you tape off the leading edge of the plate so light does not enter the edge of the plate resulting in a series of rainbow bands.

Ok FryX, I hope you don't mind me marking up you photo.

I drew in which paths I thought were happening.

For the object beam, as you can see by the blocker number 1, that if you dont' block this light the left side of the beam will hit the plate.

For the reference beam, make sure you use a card with a small aperture after the mirror and before the lense. Also use two cards right next to the lens as close as you can get it. This insure reflections off the mount does not hit the plate.

Two MAJOR flaws with your set-up that I see if my lines are correct is that

1.your reference beam is hitting the plate completely on axis. This is not good. You want it to hit the plate at and angle Brewster is best (56 degrees off the normal).

2. you reference beam is twice the length of your object beam. If your laser, which it looks like, only has a coherence length of a few inches, then the object beam is useless and you are basically shooting a SINGLE beam reflection hologram using only the reference beam for light interference. Either get rid of the object beam completly and just shoot a single beam or alter the reference beam and object beam paths to match. Of course if you have a feet of coherence lenght you may be ok with path lengths. I can only tell relatively that the reference is twice as long as the object.

JohnFP Thu Mar 27, 2008 6:06 pm #21
Hey FryX good to hear back from you.

I am going to assume that the plate is the thin line on an angle at the end of the expanded beam which goes from left to right in your illustration. Assuming that, your geometry look good for a reflection hologram.

If the distance from the film to your object and back to the film is longer then the coherence length of the laser, the light that goes through the plate and hits the object and bounces back to the plate will not help in making the hologram. This alone without the other "helper" object lighting would be a Single Beam Reflection hologram. But even if the object is to far away for your coherence length, you should still be able to use the "helper" light to get the beam paths equal. You have to start measuring from the beam splitter down the reference beam path to the plate. A non stretchy string works great. Then you have to insure the "helper" object lighting is exactly the same length (or actually within your coherence length). This needs to be measured from beam splitter, down the path to the object then from the object to the film. I would get them within a 1/4 inch or so.

As a first test I would drop out the bottom object lighting (or just block it out) and take the object and place it as close to the film as you can (almost touching) and try to make that hologram. You can then insure that your processing is correct, then go back to the additional object lighting if you wish. Stability is a must for reflection with a free standing object. My suggestion has always been to make a hologram with the plate lying right on the object for a first attempt. That way there is minimal leeway for movement. This illustration uses film clamped between two glass plates which could be a holographic plate alone and the illustrion uses supports for the object but I would suggest using an object that lays steady on its back and a plates will lay steady on the front.

[http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustration ... Single.jpg](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/SImpleSingle.jpg)

For this configuration, you can either mount your laser overhead and point it down or point it up to an overhead mirror and then stear it down to the plate. This is just a suggestion to elliminate movement as your problem in which you can then concentration on processing. It's hard to find a problem when more then one exist. You don't know if what your changing is good or bad.

The brown rings that you see in processing probably match up exactly with the Newton rings you see from the specs on your lens. Place a white card where the film would go and look a the the laser light expanded through the lense hitting the plate. Do you see the same rings? While a little detractive from a final hologram, you should still be able to get a hologram despite these rings.

Questions/suggestions:

Do you bleach until the plate is clear?

Are you using plates or flexible film?

Do not dry the hologram at all with hair dryer, let it dry naturally after squeegying the water off.

FryX, not sure which instructions you have but here is the instructions Integraf has on their web site. Let's go over a few points, ok?

[http://www.holokits.com/jd-4_holography_developer.htm](http://www.holokits.com/jd-4_holography_developer.htm)

I. There are three parts to this processing (2 seperate developer parts and 1 bleach part).

II. Here is your tray order (Part A and B of the developer get mixed in the first tray and the bleach goes in the third tray.)

> Developer >> Rinse >> Bleach >> Rinse >> Wetting SolutionA&B

III. Here is the processing paramaters, which you should keep the same and adjust exposure time to get the good hologram. In step three below is states to bleach until clear then another 10 seconds. Also, use the longer rinse times (3 min), not the short ones.

> 1. Develop:Quickly submerge the plate into the developer so that all parts get wet evenly. Slush it around for about 10 seconds. The hologram should turn almost black.2. Rinse:Rinse the developed hologram with agitation for at least 20 seconds. For best results and longer lasting holograms, rinse up to 3 minutes to make help ensure that all of Part A has been rinsed off.3. Bleach:Place the rinsed hologram into the bleaching solution; agitate it until the plate is completely clear (this may take up to 1 minute); bleach for another 10 seconds.4. Rinse again:Rinse the bleached hologram with agitation for at least 20 seconds (up to 3 minutes).5. Finish in wetting solution:Optionally, place the finished hologram in this solution for about 20 seconds. Then, remove the hologram to dry. For best results, avoid streaks or runs as you remove the hologram from the solution.

IV. Finally, it states it is best to let it dry naturally, which is what I suggest. Blow drying introduces additional problems.

> A good way to dry the hologram is to stand it against a vertical surface with the bottom edge resting on a hand-towel or tissue paper. Best results are obtained when it dries naturally in clean, dust-free air. However, if time is limited, the hologram can be quick-dried by holding it vertically and blowing warm air across it with a hair dryer.
