Tony Thu Oct 21, 2004 1:58 pm #4
Having a septic system, I've been concerned about chemical waste disposal from holography day one (about 1.5 yrs ago). For the most part, I capture the waste, including rinse water from each step, in 1 gallon jugs. Fortunately my town has a Household Hazardous Waste day twice a year. They haven't asked any questions when I tell them it's photo processing chemicals. I also attach a sheet describing each to each jug.

My biggest concern is the bleach, which contains KDiCr. I do my bleaching in a plastic tray (Stouffer's Lasagna - excellent for 4x5s). After I'm done, I do three rinses, each rinse goes into the waste bottle, before finally washing the tray in running water which goes down the drain.

I don't mix the chemicals - I have separate waste bottles for D-19, JD-2 developer, Stop Bath, and Bleach.

I do allow a few drops of Photoflow to go down the drain.

I also try to get the most I can out of each batch of chemicals. I use Kodak Indicator Stop Bath until it turns purple. I had my first Liter of bleach last over 1 year - and finally mixed up a new batch because I had lost half the volume to the tray rinse.

Poisoning through skin contact is but one possibility here – and of course there's always the risk of dermatosis.

The main risk though may stem from dichromate dust particles.

Cr(VI) can enter the body when people breathe air, eat food, or drink water containing it. Cr(VI) is also found in house dust and soil, which can be ingested or inhaled. Of the various forms of chromium, Cr(VI) is the most toxic.Certain Cr(VI) compounds have been found to be carcinogenic in humans, but the evidence to date indicates that the carcinogenicity is site-specific--limited to the lung and sinonasal cavity--and dependent on high exposures, such as might be encountered in an industrial setting. Cr(VI) can cause a wide range of other health effects. Inhaling relatively high concentrations of some forms of Cr(VI) can cause a runny nose, sneezing, itching, nosebleeds, ulcers, and holes in the nasal septum. Short-term high-level inhalational exposure can cause adverse effects at the contact site, including ulcers, irritation of the nasal mucosa, and holes in the nasal septum. Ingestion of very high doses of Cr(VI) can cause kidney and liver damage, nausea, irritation of the gastrointestinal tract, stomach ulcers, convulsions, and death. Dermal exposures may cause skin ulcers or allergic reactions (Cr[VI] is one of the most highly allergenic metals, second only to nickel). And studies of mice fed high doses of Cr(VI) have shown reproductive effects including reduced litter size and decreased fetal weight.([http://www.mindfully.org/Pesticide/Hexa ... azards.htm](http://www.mindfully.org/Pesticide/Hexavalent-Chromium-Health-Hazards.htm))

In a holographic environment the Cr(VI) quantities involved may be small. I fear though our well isolated holo labs tend to preserve dichromate dust – once released - for quite a while. So each time we enter the lab we may get our “daily” dosis of Cr(VI).

> Martin: The health aspect may be one reason to not rely entirely on dichromate technology...

Dear friend Martin, I have to completely disagree with that.

Remember the days when we all used dichromate bleaches? Wow.

Anyway, I have a small jar of dichromate, and I use only 3 grams per ~220ml of DCG emulsion. That volume of emulsion can go an incredibly long way. The dichromate I purchased from Photographer's Formulary consists of nice, large crystals. So there's not an excessive amount of fine particles that can get dispersed in the air. I treat this small jar like it was almost radioactive, very slowly lowering a plastic spoon to gather up a few grams, then very gently laying the crystals down on a piece of weighing paper on my scale. Then, slowly and gently, I add or subtract from it to get 3 grams, and let it slide into a beaker.

As you mentioned, the inhalation of dust is the real hazard, according to what I've read also. As for Jeff's MBDCG, that should win an award for low chromate content: for about 100ml of emulsion, all that's needed is about 1ml of a 5% solution of potassium chromate.

A posting I put in the Tips and Tricks section back in July 2005 is still on the front page (at the bottom). It would seem even after 2.5 years it was not spotted as nobody in this thread mentioned it as a convenient practical step .

[http://www.holographyforum.org/phpBB2/v ... =12&t=3861](http://www.holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?f=12&t=3861)

Dave's answer using sodium metabisulfite to convert the the dreaded CrVI (dichromate) to much much less harmful CrIII is the best way to tackle the problem immediately.

(CrIII is even a vital "trace element" in mammals) . Bill's idea with EDTA will work very well tying up CrIII but I am afraid it will not work directly on

CrVI as this stuff does not behave like a heavy metal salt . However an interesting aside to this is that I remember over 30 years ago an Italian firm

marketing a form of dichromate for sensitizing gelatin on silk-screen stencils for the Graphic Arts technology folk . It was called I think "Sensiroto"

and consisted of a chemical combination of dichromate with EDTA and was claimed to increase the effective shelf life of the unexposed gelatin by a very useful amount. So it had the same colour as dichromate and was still CrVI but lasted longer in the gelatin. I always thought that the photosensitivity was inevitably compromised but it did work I found almost as well as the straight stuff. (In those days I used it to make dry transfer lettering and was not into holography).

Having written the above I realize suddenly that it rather begs an explanation as to how that Italian stuff was working and I have not given it any proper

thought over the past 30 years . The only answer I have come up with could be of relevant practical interest to all DCGersif it is correct . So I think the answer is as follows.

The less pure the dichromate salt the shorter the shelf life of unexposed DCG. (Eg. sodium dichromate discussed on the other thread here:-

[http://www.holographyforum.org/phpBB2/v ... a&start=15](http://www.holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?f=2&t=4992&st=0&sk=t&sd=a&start=15) )

So trace impurities of other transition metals ---Cu,Fe, Ni, as well as CrIII in the dichromate act as dark reaction catalysts cutting the useful shelf life down even in a refrigerator. So that means that adding the powerful chelating agent EDTA to ones dichromate sensitizing solution

will cause these impurities to have their electrons tied up and unable to catalyse the dark reaction. So my guess is that adding just a bit of EDTA to

ones dichromate solution [say 5% based on solid dichromate]could possibly double the shelf life. But in the case of potassium dichromate the impurity level is inclined to be lower

than the other dichromates but nevertheless the major impurity in the unexposed DCG could be traces of CrIII and probably CrV and these will be

causing an increasingly autocatalytic effect causing their own production to increase with each incremental step causing the dark oxidation and hardening of the gelatin.

So it could be that Bill Alschuler's suggestion has led to a really useful tip . Let's try it! The effect on photosensitivity should be virtually nil. By the way the EDTA in this plan can be either the free acid or the sodium

salts but must not be the Ferric EDTA of course since the iron has it all tied and complexed up already.

Jeff
