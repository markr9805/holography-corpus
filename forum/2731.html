edelbrp Thu Sep 23, 2004 5:31 pm #7
> kc: BTW edelbrp, do you know how to do the subtraction with photoshop ?

Sure, in a nutshell:

- Open your images in Photoshop

- Drag one image over the other using the tool which looks like a solid black pointer. This will put the image in a new layer in the document.

- Use the arrow keys to 'nudge' the image if nessesary to align it.

- View the layers pallete and select the top layer

- Change the pulldown in the layers pallete from 'Normal' (the default) to 'Difference'. You should immediately see a composite subtracted result between the top layer and the bottom.

You can also continue to move more photos into the same document this way and hide/show different layers by clicking on the eyeball next to the layer in the layers pallete.

Post some more images if you have something interesting to show!

- Phil

I think this is a different animal. In ESPI, you're recording the speckles (a much more macroscopic point of view). In holograms, you're recording the very small inteference pattern. To 'play back' from the inteference pattern, you need light which has the same wavelength that was used to produce it.[1] Speckles are much coarser and can be recorded by your camera.

BTW-AFAIK, there are no digital mediums that are fine enough to capture inteference patterns in the visible-light spectrum. Even if there were, there would be so much data to store, that it would take a massive harddrive array to record. And, I don't know how it could be reasonably played back... That's why we have to use 'holographic' film, which is really just photographic film with a very high resolving power (like 5000 lines per millimeter!).

-Phil

I think I agree with edelbrp that this is a different animal. I say I think because I'm not familiar with ESPI. However, a few points:

In standard holographic interferometry, you do need to see it in laser light, not necessarily with the same wavelength but it makes life easier if you do use the same wavelength. In this case, you make a laser transmission hologram of the object in the usual way - light reflecting from object and reference light at some angle to the plate - then you distort the object and make another exposure on the same plate. The interference of the two object beams each slightly displaced in phase creates a "beat frequency" which shows up as lines across the image. this is still, however, a laser viewable hologram. The object(s) are still several inches behind the plate and so it (they) cannot be seen in white light without a great deal of dispersion. If you see it in any other than the recording wavelength there will be a magnification of the image and so the apparent beat frequency will be shifted. There may also be other aberrations. This is also true to a lesser extent in shearograms. They're still made with a large enough angle between reference and object to create a high average spatial frequency and the objects are still an inch or so away from the plate. The angle is not as high as a traditional holographic interferometry so you could probably see one with a LED. The fact that you can record the ESPI on ASA 400 film implies to me that this may not be true for ESPI because the spatial frequencies are low enough to be seen in white light without dispersion. I believe ASA 400 corresponds to about 200 l/mm. If you did want to view in laser light, you'd view the negative by transmitting laser light through it.

I must admit I'm very curious about this ESPI. In shearography, it most certainly is a holographic technique, but with a very low average spatial frequency. Low enough, in fact, that it could be captured on "standard film". I

used holographic film (8E75) but could easily have used PanX. However, the point is that the fringe density is

in the order of a few hundred lines/mm, but the interference density is low enough to be easily captured by a

ccd. In fact, that's how shearograms and even holographic interferograms are analysed. But you can't record

the interference fringes unless you can first record the holographic fringes. In other words, you need high

(-ish) film resolution to capture the data but can analyse the data at a very much lower resolution. You can see

that from the following equations:

[http://images.techonline.com/images/com ... ation1.gif](http://images.techonline.com/images/community/content/old/features/OSEE/B80/equation1.gif)
