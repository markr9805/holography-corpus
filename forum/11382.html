Joe Farina Thu Oct 17, 2024 10:22 am #2
I've never used an X-Cube, so I don't know. I've only used the dichroic beam combiners from Edmund.

Regarding which beam to transmit and which to reflect, it depends on the power of your lasers, the difference in transmission vs. reflectance efficiency of the beam combiner, and the spectral sensitivity of the recording material. For example, if you need to conserve red light as much as possible, and your beam combiner has more efficiency in reflection mode (vs. transmission) then of course you want to reflect the red. In the long run, it shouldn't make much difference, and if your lasers are approximately equal in power, I wouldn't worry about it (when using Bayfol). Just combine the beams and shoot the holograms. It's much more important to know the actual power output of the lasers. Later on, if you're using other lasers, and there is an obvious power mismatch between the red and green, then it might be worthwhile to look into the beam-combiner efficiency issue. The most important thing is what your emulsion "sees" in terms of laser power. By the way, for measurements under 40mW, this looks like an inexpensive option:

Din Fri Oct 18, 2024 8:42 am #4
> Alan Sailerwrote:↑Thu Oct 17, 2024 11:32 am: I did some quick tests with a white flash light and the appearance of the split colors changes when I polarize the light.Cheers.

It would. Reflectivity is polarisation and angle dependent, so if you're reflecting, polarisation is an issue. For transmission, it's not because you transmit on-axis, most of the time, and so the light hits the lens perpendicularly. At perpendicular incidence, reflectivity is at 4% for a glass at all polarisations, and so you'll lose 4% at the interface. For reflection, the polarisation state and angle determine the reflectivity - the proportion of incoming light that's reflected. There are curves that you can read off for pure polarisations states 'p' and 's' (below),. 'P' polarisation refers to polarisation that's parallel to the plane of incidence, thus for a horizontal geometry (all the beams in one plane), 'p' polarisation would be horizontal. 'S' polarisation refers to polarisation that's perpendicular to the plane of incidence, and so would be vertical in a horizontal geometry. Of course, if the polarisation were some combination of 's' and 'p', the reflectivity would change. Also, if the beam changes direction from a horizontal geometry to a vertical one, eg is sent upwards, the polarisation is altered. In the end, what's important is that the polarisation of both beams are parallel at the film plane; for this reason, some people use a polarising beam splitter cube and a half-wave plate.

The colour appears to change because the colour rendition depends on the colour mix at the film plane. If you lose a certain amount of light at some angle for a specific colour because of reflection , and not lose as much in transmission for another colour, the colour mix changes, hence the appearance changes. If you (or anyone else) is interested, I can upload an article I wrote on colour mixing.

Din Fri Oct 18, 2024 11:53 am #7
One other point, thinking further, as to Joe's method using dichroics, the dichroics have to be at an angle to the incoming, transmitted beam, in order to line up the reflected beam. So, the transmitted beam is not hitting the dichroic at normal incidence (perpendicularly). This means there will be a back-reflection of the transmitted beam, and so be polarisation-dependant based on the angle of the incoming beam to the dichroic. Assuming you use 'p' polarisation ( most holographers do, or should do, for different reasons), it's important to have the incoming beam hit the (transmitting) dichroic at Brewster's angle to limit the back-reflected beam.

I built x-cube light engines for a few years and even assembled one out of custom dichroic mirrors. The problem you may face is that for those harvested from projectors, there are often wavelength dependent wave plates attached (or special coatings) that can mess with the polarization of the individual colors.

Ed Wesly's pages are full of accurate information and practical solutions for home holography.
