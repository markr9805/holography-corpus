So, you're telling me that you have swirling dust in the air. You zap the dust, reflection? transmission? Let's assume that somehow the dust particles are suspended and stable. This negates the necessity of a pulse laser. Whether or not a real pulse can do a real hologram of swirling dust is now a secondary condition.

Let's say reflection. Then you're presumably talking about a single beam Denisyuk? So the beam goes through the plate, hitsdust particles in the air, and then reflects enough object beam back onto the plate to create a hologram of these very small dust particles. You now look through the plate and see dust particles.IS this a reasonable description? OK, so the obvious question is: will the dust particles reflect enough of the beam to create a strong enough object beam.It seems to me that this depends on the number and density of the particles. It seems to follow that one dust particle would certainly reflect whatever the (how can I say"capture cross section of a dust particle"?) Oh Ok, the area of the beam covered by the dust particle. Now a dust particle is about 10 microns or less in diameter. I hope you give me this since we are talking specifically about dust. This is about 20 lambda or less . So you have a structure of width 20 lambda or less interrupting a beam. I expect this will diffract. So now the return object beam has a superimposed diffraction pattern around it. Since speckle is also a diffractive/interference effect, the diffractive pattern from the dust will compete with the speckle. Which will be larger? The average size of speckle is also on the order of a few microns, so this question hinges on the distance from the plate to the dust particle. Certainly I see this as a limiting factor. The dust will be an effective point particle with a dimension small enough to be an effective aperture. Since I'm assuming that we're talking a real dust particle, how much light will the dust particle radiate back? It's certainly possible to calculate the diffraction pattern and the reflectivity of a perfect sphere, but dust is not a perfect sphere. So we have an inhomogenous surface. If the structure itself is on the order of 10 or so microns, I think we can safely assume that the surface features will be of the order of about a micron, or roughly 2 lambda. So now we have surface features of 2 lambda each acting as a source of new waves a la Fresnel. These new waves will also mutually interfere and diffract. What will now be the net outgoing wavefront? And, what will be it's intensity? Considering these extremely small features, I think I can argue that there will be significant absorption and loss due to mutual diffractive effects from these surface features. So, I doubt you'll get more than a few percent of light back. Thus, taking the inverse square law into consideration, even in a perfect world, you have a ratio of probably close to 100:1. I would argue that the fringe modulation at 100:1 would be negligible. Of course, you could say that the dust particle is extremely close to the plateand so increase this modulation, but noweher near the values you'd expect to get a hologram from. These are general statements evaluating the possibility of taking a hologram of a staionary dust particle. If the dust particle were now moving, the modulation would further decrease because it's velocity would cause it to move more than one of it's diameters/sec. Thus there's a constant drop in object beam intensity depending on the rate at which the particle is receeding in the z direction. Since exposure is dependant on the respective intensities of object and reference and since reference is constant while object is decreasing, there is now a race condition. Now add a few more dust particles. Each of these will again be point scatterers positioned randomly in space. I can do the calculation (but I know you'll scream if I do ) but it seems evident that there will be a rapidly fluctuating set of low frequency gratings. Since these gratings are rapidly fluctuating, the average illumination on the plate will be uniform. I haven't even considered what will happen if the dust particles are revolving around an axis within it's structure, but the phase changes involved in this kind of motion at the plate is very interesting. I won't go into details because it involves a theorem! There are a number of other problems as well but these are the major ones I can see. Other possible problems will occur if the dust is charged (as it might well be through friction and passage through the air) and so there's a mutual repellant action oif dust particles. You now have to calculate the phase changes caused by this "dust dance"as well as more complicated motions such as rotation of two (or more) particles around a common axis between the particles. There's also the electrostaic attraction of the dust to the plate itself. As the plate is so much larger than the particle, the particle will have a high acceleration. This acceleration will depend on whether or not the particles trajectory takes it to the center of the plate or the edge. There's also the effect of a particle going around the edge of the plate to the backside. This will cause a point scatterer behind the plate interfering with one in front.

Note: no theorems, no mathematics, a few technical terms ("phase", "diffraction", "interference" etc) which I hope you don't scream too loudly at, since it's impossible to discuss holography without them.

Actually it didn't. Michelson/Morley did not "disprove" the hypothesis. They set out to measure the earth's velocity wrt the ether, which they took for granted. To them ether was no hypothesis. The null result was the surprise and Lorentz's hypothesis of electron elongation was the "best" explanation. However, the elongation of the electron was an ad hoc explanation which Lorentz never fully justified. Einstein made the ether irrelevant (not non-existant as the popular media have you believe) by removing it as an absolute inertial frame. When he also postulated the constancy of the velocity of light in all inertial frames and, from this, derived a set of transformation equations, these equations turned out to be the very ones Lorentz had derived under the assumption of electron elongation. Einstein's was attempting to resolve the inconsistency between the fact that mechanical phenomena were invariant under a Galilean transformation, but electromagnetic phenomena, the Maxwell equations, were not. He felt that all natural phenomena in all inertial frames should be invariant under the same transformations. The "dismissal" of the ether was a consequence of the postulates under which the relativistic transformations were derived and was instantly recognised as such. They were not a consequence of the tranformation equations themselves. Of course, you can argue the postulates were ad hoc but, so far, relativity has stood all experimental tests. I have a fascinating book of the early work, including the Lorentz paper on electron elongation and another evaluating "field theories" going back to Descartes "plenum" field theory, which I will be glad to send you, if you're interested in ether and field theories.

Anyway, the macrocat will not diffract (visible) light, the microcat will, depending on it's size. The structure of any reflected light is the far field Fraunhofer diffraction pattern. The larger an object is, the less the diffractive structure is visible, and conversely, the smaller an object is the more prominent is it's far field Franhofer pattern. Without actually calculating it, I'd suggest that a 10nm particle will have a significant diffractive patern. If the particle is uniformly round, this will be an diverging Airy disc. This will be very small for a macrocat, but as I say possibly significant on dust motes. If the dust particle is 100nm across, we may be getting to a point whereh the low frequency diffractive pattern is small as a ratio of the reflected light. Again, this ratio needs to be calculated. to find this critical size. The holographic plate will be directly illuminated with the reference beam which will be hundreds of times more intense than the reflected light from a dust particle. Visibility alone does not indicate the possibility of good ratio. The eye is a logarithmic detector with sophisticated mechanisms for detectting edges, tilts, colour variations etc. The holographic plate can only depend on direct, linear intensity variations. If the dust particles were somehow reflecting with a Lambertian profile, the reflectivity may improve, but so would the corresponding diffractive pattern.

I recommend "Introduction to Fourier Optics" by Goodman ( [http://www.roberts-publishers.com/goodm ... raise.html](http://www.roberts-publishers.com/goodman/Goodpraise.html) ). In the case of "dust" as in my description of dust motes in a swirling cloud in the model I used, the structure of the far field fraunhofer pattern would overwhelm any uniform illumination. The individual phase componnets of the Fraunhofer pattern would interfere with the reference beam, however the beam modulation would be so low (again I stress dust motes in swirling household dust) that the fringe visibilty may not record, depending on the medium and it's properties.
