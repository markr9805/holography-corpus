Din Tue Mar 31, 2026 12:39 pm #3
Well clearly, the fault is in that the Bragg planes are twisting along the hologram, from top to bottom. This will cause the planes to Bragg-select different wavelengths relative to the recon light, ie to be on-Bragg for different wavelengths; you may have noticed that when you view a Single-Beam Denisyuk under collimated white light, eg the sun, then, as you twist the hologram, the colour changes due to the fact that the angle between the Bragg planes and the recon source has twisted, causing different wavelengths to be Bragg-selected.

Why this is happening is difficult to say, given the lack of information. So, speculation:

1. The difference in beam divergences from the original H1 ref beam divergence to the recon-H1/ ref-H2 beam divergence may be diverging (sorry!)

2. The H1 and H2 are not exactly parallel. The difference in your image from top to bottom seems to be about 100nm, ie from about 670nm at the bottom to about 560nm at the top. This difference being small, the angular shift between H1 and H2 plates may be small.

3. There may be non-uniformities in the processing, eg a temperature gradient in any of the alcohols. This will cause differential thickness across the hologram.

4. There may be coating/drying post coating non-uniformity, but you've coated enough plates not to take that seriously; it would be a factor for a beginner.

5. In the water soak phase, do you put the plate in an already water filled pan, or do you put the plate into an empty pan, then run the water? If the latter, the plate may have tilted due to something in the water, and so the lower edges started to swell before the upper edges causing this red-yellow-green shift from bottom to top.

The reason you don't see the 488 is because DCG swells causing the Bragg plane separation to increase. It's very (very!) difficult to keep the recon wavelength to be the same as the recording wavelength. The reason DCG holograms are so bright is that it's a very broadband material; as an aside, it's also the reason it's difficult to make holographic optics on DCG, the optics have to be narrow band.

Well, the broadband nature of DCG is due to differential swelling. As I mentioned earlier, when you immerse the hologram in water, the top of the emulsion starts to swell, while the water has not yet penetrated into the lower parts of the emulsion (nearer the base). By the time the water gets down to the lower parts and starts to swell it, the emulsion at the top has been continually swelling, causing different rates of swelling along the depth of the emulsion.

There are a few ways to mitigate the problem:

1. pre-harden the surface before exposing. The harder the upper layers of the emulsion, the more it resists water penetration, causing a more uniform swelling. This was done with a UV exposure.

2. Harden the emulsion after the exposure, either with UV exposure or excessive fixer/hardener times. For display DCG, I'd normally fix/harden for about 1 minute (you want broadband), but for technical optics, I'd go three or four minutes for fresh plates (we'd coat on Friday afternoon, and shoot on Monday morning, so "fresh" plates were two days after coating). I've experimented with UV instead of fix/hardener, but getting the exposure right was dodgy; sometimes I'd overharden and get nothing, and other times I'd under-harden and get broad band anyway.

3. Leave the plate alone for about a week between exposure and development. Dark reaction causes hardening.

My normal display procedure was:

1 minute fix/harden

5 minutes running water

1 minute 25% alcohol

1 minute 50% alcohol

1 minute 75% alcohol

1 minute 100% cold (room temp) alcohol

1 minute100% hot (~70 deg C) alcohol.

For technical, narrow band optics, I'd go 3 minutes on the fixer, 1- 2 minute in running water, and direct immersion into the 75% alcohol, ie I'd skip the 25% and 50% baths. This gave me about a 10nm bandwidth
