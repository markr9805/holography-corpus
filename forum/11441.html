Meta has announced an Augmented AR glasses with AI that they call Orion: [https://www.meta.com/blog/meta-ray-ban- ... 8tMwHc9fMM](https://www.meta.com/blog/meta-ray-ban-display-ai-glasses-connect-2025/?srsltid=AfmBOors1fQsPr5vr0n8cReIIGroJLRJBqC46A5RRMbs2G8tMwHc9fMM)

The basic principle behind the glasses is the holographic waveguide; this is a glass waveguide with an entrance hologram and an exit hologram. The image enters the waveguide from a digital display, travels along the waveguide, and, at the exit hologram, exits at the eye(s).

Below one of our (Triple Take) waveguides made in 2018. You can see a laser beam in the back enter the entrance hologram, travel along the waveguide, and exit at the exit hologram. The bright green light (514nm) on the right of the picture is the exiting beam. In the actual glasses, it would not be a laser beam, but an image.

We worked on several different designs of waveguides for a lot of different companies.

Din Wed Sep 24, 2025 12:14 pm #3
Yes, essentially it's the same design we've all been using for two decades; in fact, even earlier since some of the designs are similar to the Air Force Heads Up Display (HUD) from the 1980's. The problem is that you do get noise and double imaging, and a narrow field of view; although the narrow field of view didn't exist in the Air Force HUD because we used a different geometry at NTS, where I worked in the Research Dept in 1986. For the Air Force application, you had to have a very wide field of view.

A point they make:

"Conventional CNN-based AI models fail to accurately predict complex light propagation in...waveguides, partly because of incorrect assumption of the light source being fully coherent."

was a problem for us (Triple Take), as well as others in the field I've talked to. I did model a sort of quasi monochromatic source, but did not take the M² into consideration. I considered not just one input hologram and one output hologram, but a series of holograms along the path inside the waveguide, but still there were problems. Each 'bounce' inside the waveguide creates a dispersion, such that, at some point, the images overlap and you see double images. This paper takes a different and, I think, clever solution by implementing aberration correction into the design:

"Our optical architecture...solves limitations of similar design by removing unwanted diffraction noise and chromatic dispersion using a volume-holographic waveguide and holographic lens respectively"

I didn't read the paper carefully, but I assume that the holographic lens corrects aberrations by distorting the wavefronts, based on some AI model.

Din Thu Sep 25, 2025 12:35 pm #5
As I see it, there's a neural net controlling the input angle, which is interesting. I assume the lens is not holographic (it's not easy to create a large angle holographic angle, as I can attest!). Their lens is certainly impressive. But, I'm a bit confused by their statement:

"...our couplers are constructed of uniquely designed volume Bragg gratings (VBG's) instead of SRG's. ...VBG's exhibit a type of diffraction where only light that satisfies a specific incident angle and narrow spectral bandwidth is diffracted to a single diffracted order with high efficiency"

They seem to be saying they're the first to use Bragg selective waveguides. I assume that's why they're saying that they're correcting for aberrations - the conventional knowledge being that if a Bragg selective hologram is recontructed with the correct wavelength and angle, the reconstruction is 'perfect'.

But, these Bragg selective waveguides already existed. The picture I showed of one of our waveguides is a volume Bragg selective waveguide and I know others have made Bragg waveguides before us. Our gratings were made on a DCG emulsion, for high efficiency and large frequencies. So, in answer to your original question: "Is this the one described in "Synthetic aperture waveguide holography for compact mixed-reality displays with large étendue" ", yes, it's also a volume Bragg selective waveguide. But, we still had problems with dispersion, because it's difficult to get a narrow band DCG hologram because of wet processing, you have to process it carefully. I presume they're using polymer, which is a narrow band material because there's no processing. However, the conventional knowledge is that there's also no shrinkage in the polymer, which is not true. This shrinkage, if non-uniform, which it usually is, will introduce a certain amount of dispersion. So, I take the statement that they're correcting for aberrations with a bit of salt if they're implying that there's no aberrations because of the Bragg selectivity.
