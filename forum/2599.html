Tony Sat Jul 03, 2004 11:10 am #1
As I was playing around (which means doing serious scientific experiments on a weekend without writing down the results) with my new table, I learned something about inner tubes. They need to be able to breathe!

I had the inner tube in a layer between two sheets of plywood. I noticed that if I tried to remove the top sheet, it was really stuck by suction/air pressure to the tube.

The inner tube forms a pretty good seal to the plywood. What this means is that even thought the top layer can rock back and forth, it is actually pretty stiffly connected to the bottom piece by the air chamber formed inside the inner tube. Although it's an air chamber, as a spring analog, it is very stiff. This means that vibrations passed straight up from the floor will be well coupled into the table above - not what we're trying to achieve. The large area of the chamber means that a small displacement of the top will result in a large change in volume, pressure, and force. What we really want to do is use only the area contained inside the tube itself, which is much smaller, therefore a lower effective spring constant.

The solution was to drill a large hole in one of the layers of plywood to allow the volume of air in the center to change. Now it is much more springy in the vertical dimension.

For those of you who like the idea of resistive damping, you could play around with the size of the air hole to control damping. Personally I think you don't want any damping, but thats an old thread (see forum54 archives).

Tony Sun Dec 11, 2005 11:51 am #9
John-

Yes, with no way for air to enter/exit the space in the middle, the blocked inner tube arrangement actually acts like a large diameter piston. The effective spring constant is not significantly determined by the inner tube, but by the pressure/volume relationship of the piston. This yields a very stiff spring constant. If the air is not trapped in the center, the spring constant is determined by the rubber of the inner tube, as it gets 'squashed'.

Tony Fri Jan 06, 2006 9:21 pm #13
My short answer is that you want to use a number of inner tubes with an inflation pressure such that the natural frequency of oscillation of the table is as low as possible. Your intent is to make the resonance of the table act to block higher frequency vibrations from the floor from getting to the table. This means use a very low stiffness spring.

Furthermore, it's my opinion that you don't want any damping.

I tried to find a post I made in the old forum, but was unable to search for it. So at the risk of being too lengthy, I'm going to repost it below.

-Tony

> Damping - the meaning of the term relative to optical tablesThere seems to be somewhat of a misconception as far as the techniques used to make a useful optical table, as far as vibration control is concerned. Some of this might be in the terminology, but some of it begs clarification.The purpose of the optical table is to provide a stable platform to set up optical elements like mirrors, lasers, film, etc. to make holograms. The requirement is that the optical elements remain in stable positions relative to one another within a small fraction of a wavelength over the time period of the exposure.The stability requirement is usually obtained by constructing the table to:First, be isolated from sources of vibrations, andSecond, to be rigid so that any vibrations that reach the table will affect all the optical elements equally, thereby inducing no relative movement.The usual practice is to:1. Find a stable place to put the table, like on a concrete floor in a basement.2. Use a stable pedestal to position the table at a convenient height - like a stack of concrete blocks.3. Use a springy element such as inner tubes or bubble wrap.4. Use a heavy, stiff table top, such as concrete.The structure formed by this is a simple harmonic oscillator, right out of the freshman Physics text. The spring constant of the springy element works with the mass of the table top to form an oscillator with a natural resonant frequency. This is the frequency at which the table top will bounce up and down if disturbed.The input energy to the oscillator is the undesired source of vibrations from the floor, coming from appliances, furnaces, people walking around, vehicles, etc.By using a springy element with a low spring constant (low deflection force per unit length of deflection), and a large mass, the resonant frequency is made very low. Strategically, we want the resonant frequency much lower than the typical frequency of the energy from which we wish to isolate the table.The amount of isolation of the table movement will be increased if we make the table resonant frequency much lower than the source of vibrations.Each of the optical elements on the table, such as a lens on the top of a rod mounted in a stand, itself is also a harmonic oscillator. We wish to avoid allowing energy to reach these near their resonant frequency. This is why we try to make the optical elements as stiff and rigid as possible - to make them have a higher natural frequency.If there were no losses in the system, and some undesired vibrations were introduced, the table would start oscillating (bouncing) and never stop. But in real life, there are losses, and the energy imparted to the table does get dissipated. The oscillations gradually die down. This is called damping. Damping can be due to air friction, or frictional losses in the system, especially the springy element.It might be thought (as has been alluded on this forum) that more damping is a good thing.If we intentionally added another element designed to provide additional damping, the oscillations would die down quicker. This would be in the form of something similar to an automotive shock absorber connected from the floor to the table (in parallel with the inner tubes). The down side is that this also provides a more direct path for vibrations from the floor to reach the table. As the stiffness of the shock absorber is increased, the vibrations damp down more quickly - but the initial displacement of the table in response to the undesired vibration is greater!! This may not have been what we had in mind. The frictional element (shock absorber) reduces the effective isolation between the floor and the table, at low frequencies - right where we wish to obtain isolation.It’s my personal opinion that as long as the table and optical elements are rigid, some table motion is tolerable. So I believe that what works most effectively in this case is to have the most compliant, lowest spring constant spring element that can be found.Or, a closed-loop inertial gyroscopic active stabilization system would be nice too…
