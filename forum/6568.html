Joe Farina Sat Dec 01, 2007 1:01 pm #4
Michael, with all due respect, I think you're going off on a tangent regarding DCG. I can understand that you want the benefit of increased speed (like all of us) with G307, but if you're just starting out in DCG, there are already too many variables to begin with. Why are you using a desiccator to dry your plates? I've never heard of that. You may be dehydrating the daylights out of it, causing it to get too hard too soon. I just did the fingernail test on one of my processed DCG holograms, and yes, the emulsion gets very hard, and I couldn't scratch through it. But this is for a processed hologram, I haven't tried it on an unexposed plate.

I can understand that you eventually want to use G307, and feel that you might as well learn the idiosyncrasies of that particular method, but I would resist this temptation for now. Whatever method you use eventually, whether it be G307 or a dye-sensitized emulsion, you should know what to expect with basic or "classic" DCG. If you only had a HeNe, I wouldn't say this, but since you have a 315M, there is nothing holding you back. What you really need right now (in my opinion) is to reliably produce really bright DCG holograms, no matter how small.

Here's my two cents:

Mix dichromate, gelatin, and water using some kind of double-boiler method, and keep it under 60C. Coat plates using whatever method you prefer. Let them dry under a gentle air flow for 4 hours. Store your plates in the refrigerator (use lock & lock or some other kind of airtight container). Make as many plates as possible to enable lots of tests. Do not desiccate or do anything else to them. (By the way, use Knox gelatin from the grocery store.)

Now, do tests at your lesiure. Just take your container out of the fridge, let it get up to room temperature, and take a plate out (or you can immediately take it out if you hit the surface of the gelatin hard with warm air from a hairdryer to prevent excess condensation). Expose Denisyuk style with 100mW at 532. (I would say 2.5" X 2.5" plates would be good.) Let them set in the dark for 5 minutes after exposure. Rinse under cold tap water for a minute until they yellowness goes away. Soak in room-temperature water for a minute. Soak in 91% for a minute with agitation (room-temperature). Soak in 99% for three minutes with agitation (room-temperature). Then dry with hot air.

If there is milkiness, you will need to harden the gelatin more after the dark reaction. You can user fixer or a 100W light bulb 6 inches away (for varying time periods) to do this. I prefer the light bulb method.

See how your plates age, and how they perform over time. Change variables to see different results (well, I don't need to tell you this, since you probably know better than I do about trial-and-error work in holography). Just use the same principles you use to get such good silver halide holograms. One last word: don't try to pre-plan things too much. Just use the simplest DCG technique possible (it is really very simple if you have blue or green light). Don't make it any more complex than it has to be. End of lecture

Joe Farina Mon Dec 03, 2007 4:31 pm #12
> JohnFP: I'm a little confused here Joe. You state to use the classic recipe but the classic calls for Kodak Rapid Fixer. Do you elliminate this step all together?

The Knox gelatin I've used thus far didn't need any additional hardening, at least for the simple testing that I was doing. But I'm not using hot alcohol, either, just room temperature. If hot alcohol is used, then it's much more likely that extra hardening will be needed from fixer or the light bulb. As to whether the light bulb hardening is "classic" DCG or not, I can only say that Rallison suggested either approach in one of his early, most detailed papers, so that's classic enough for me. He made no comment about either method being superior to the other, but suggested either as equally viable alternatives. Actually the light bulb is even more environmentally friendly than the fixer, because no fixer is needed in the first place.
Joe Farina Mon Dec 03, 2007 5:58 pm #13
As a sidenote, John, in the original paper by Shankoff, no fixer was involved. He merely states that "The development procedure was optimized by experiment to the following: all films were mildly agitated in water maintained at 35C for 30 sec, dipped in isopropanol for 30 sec, and air-dried with a stream of dry air under pressure."

By the way, if you want broadband DCG holograms, I see no reason to use graded IPA. The only reason I go to 91% before the 99% is to keep the last bath clean. Based on tests done by you and Dave, maybe you can simply use 91% and omit the 99%. I haven't had the chance to test this myself.
