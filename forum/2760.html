Dinesh Tue Oct 05, 2004 6:36 pm #7
Ideally, the fringes are sinusoidal. That is the fringes that exist "in air" independant of the film are sinusoidal. Of course I'm assuming two perfectly flat wavefronts or two perfect point sources over a small interference area so there's not too much phase variation. OK, so the film tries to capture the sinusoidal fringes. If the film were a perfect capture device, it would record "true" sinusoidal fringes by having a density variation that would be truly sinusoidal. However, we have to assume that the MTF of the film is not 1. Thus, depending on the frequency of the sinusoid, the film captures harmonics, as Colin suggested, of the fundemental frequency. Perhaps in just the right conditions, the movement of the fringes gave a fall-off at the edges of an otherwise square-ish profile (what you'd get assuming no movement) to create a more sinusoidal fringe structure in the emulsion. This might account for the brighter image. If the fringes are not captured sinusoidally, you'll get orders. If you twist the plate past the image and keep twisting, you'll see the image re-appear at some other angle. This "secondary" order robs some of the light from the primary order.

In terms of linear recording, clearly the first part of the film that records the fringes is right at the surface of the film, since this receives the greatest light flux. Now, as the film continues to capture the fringes, the already-formed but weak surface fringes start diffracting the incoming light. This slows down the recording deep inside the emulsion since now part of the light is diffracting away. By how much? Also, how prominent is the effect. Do you get low strength subsidiary fringes because these initial diffracted waves also interfered inside the emulsion?
BobH Tue Oct 05, 2004 7:10 pm #8
As you say, fringe lockers are touchy animals. The components of the fringe locking system not included in the recording system (such as the recombining beamsplitter) must be the most stable components on the table. If you get worse results when the fringes appear to be "locked", I'd suspect that one of these components was moving. This would cause the driven mirror (common to both systems) to make the recording system fringes move. If the fringes of the locking system were drifting, you probably had a weak lock and were therefore not moving the driven mirror as much, resulting in a brighter recording.

I think I'm confused about the different meanings of "blazed", "sinusoidal" and H2.

[quote:851a88c897]Since the photoresist result would have been a surface relief grating, I suspect that the movement might have been distorting the recorded fringes from the normal sinusoid shape to create something like the sawtooth structure used in more efficient blazed gratings. [/quote:851a88c897]

Blazed gratings are gratings that throw the entire diffracted order of any wavelength into one direction. They don't necessarily have to have incredibly high efficency, but have a higher eff for a given shape than other shapes. I think the other thing to bear in mind is that a resist plate is shot as a transmission but viewed as a reflection. The diffraction occurs as a result of light reflected off the surface profile.

An ideal sinusoidal surface grating, that is, one with no height at all from the emulsion surface, has three orders: 0, +1, -1. If the surface has a small height above emulsion surface and is shaped sinusoidally with a relatively large period, then this acts as a surface sinusiodal grating. So, if L is the "wavelength" of the sinusoid on the resist plate and h is it's height, the, according to papers by Gaylord and Moharam and others, so long as L/h < 2 (ish) you have an almost pure surface grating with three orders - no blaze. If L/h > 5 you have a very polarisation dependant and angle dependant diffraction. It almost behaves as a volume, Bragg grating. This sort of grating ain't easy to make a shim of because the height is so much greater than the width it's difficult to seperate the Silver mother without damage. However, it is a blazed grating, in that the entire diffracted order goes in one direction. Bragg selectivity applies. Another way to shoot blazed gratings in resist is to shoot them from opposite ends, like a reflection hologram, with one beam head on and another at some angle, say 30, from the other side. Counting from a common axis one beam is at zero and the other at 150 degrees. Because resist can't take Bragg fringes, the surface profile takes on the shape of the cross section of the intersection of the two lines on the surface of the resist plate. If you draw these out, you'll see they're triangular or wedge shaped. (Gary, vis a vis another thread, this was one of my "crackpot" ideas that was never tried because the upper echelon 'names' in the company said "impossible!" I tried it anyway, showed that the grating was blazed and they still didn't believe it! Not that I'm claiming credit - others had done it before me, but I never knew that at the time). These sharp grating profiles again will not copy for the same reason. However, these sharp triangular profiles are more in line with ruled blazed gratings, even though they're produced holographically. They also shoot e-beams into the grating at an angle. These e-beams will "cut out" a triangular profile and so create traditional-like blazed gratings in resist.

[quote:851a88c897]I doubt that blazing would be of much use for the volume-phase gratings that we normally make when we make holograms.[/quote:851a88c897]

I think Bragg selectivity automatically makes them blazed, since all the light is diffracted in the same direction. However, volume fringes don't behave like surface fringes. There's again this confusion between surface/volume and reflection/transmission. However surface fringes are in the Raman-Nath regime (I believe Raman Nath still holds if you reflect off surface fringes as in resist - not too sure about that) and volume fringes are in the Bragg regime. Trying to call Bragg fringes "blazed" might be invading foreign territory.
