Tom B. Sun Apr 23, 2006 3:43 am #7
Glad to hear your results are better. My experience has been mostly with reflection rather than transmission holos, but I'll offer suggestions anyway - maybe someone better informed can correct me

Yes you should rinse in running tap water for at least a few minutes after developing to clear out as much of the developer from the film as you can. If not, the film is liable to "print-out" - becoming dark with exposure to light. Also, residual developer can interfere with the bleach in unknown ways.

Because the grain size of VRP is fairly large , about the same as PFG-01, there will always be SOME scattered light, but this will be more of a smooth hazy background (looks slighty bluish under white light) than the mottled cloudy look that I saw in your pic which made me think it was mineral precipitation.

Another possible source of noise is unwanted holograms made at the same time as your main one. Normally you expect light to come only from the object and reference beam, but in a real setup there will be reflections from other equipment in the setup, from the surfaces of any glass plates used to hold down the film and from the back of the film itself. Tracking down and eliminating these reflections is an art in itself - without getting into it I'll just mention the use of Brewster's angle with a polarized laser and of "index matching" liquids to reduce reflections as keywords for your search.

On your next shot, try firmly sticking a strip of black vinyl electrical tape on the non-object side of the film before exposure, then peel it off before developing and see if the taped part has less haze. What the tape does is absorb light that comes through the film, preventing it from bouncing back toward the emulsion and creating a grating as it would with just an air/film interface. This will tell you if it's worth looking into index matching methods.

penguin7471, I will only tell this to you because it seems you already know how to view a transmission hologram without burning out you eyeballs. Obviously if the hologram is created with the reference beam hitting the plate at an angle, then you can use that same angle to illuminate the hologram and have your eyeballs perpendicular to the hologram, thus direct laser light does not hit your eyeballs.

Hold the hologram as usual and try to get your head as close to the hologram as possible without the direct laser light going into your eyes. It is also best to do this with the beam expanded very much and/or the laser turned down very low. If you look off to the side of the object, you should see the source of the laser, the actual reference beam source. You could also try, from the starting point as described above, rotate the hologram very slowly to see if other objects come in to view. You may also see other sources of light or objects in you hologram. If you do see anything besides your reference light source (laser or spatial filter - which ever is just upstream of your plate) and your object, then those other "things" will all cause haze or noise. You may even be able to see one of the glass cover plates very close to the film.

I find it more fascinating to look at all the other stuff in a transmission then the object itself. Hiding all that "other" stuff can be challenging.

Keep up the great work and I personally think you have done amazingly well thus far.

> penguin7471: ............................................................................................I don't, however, rinse with running tap water after developing... I just dump it in a tray of distilled water and rock the tray for about a minute. Is this okay?....................................................

note that you should first give a brief 5 sec rinse just to get the developer off the surface and then plunge plate into an acid "stop" bath. [In photographers parlence "stop" means STOP development.] or more simply go straight into the acid bleach bath (provided it is not dichromate reversal bleach). I am not clear what bleach you are using.

What you have been doing is wrong because the developer carries on developing for that minute even though you have apparently washed it off. The silver bromide strongly retains its very high alkaline and reducing environment unless it is treated with mild acid so you have been giving an extra minute of dev. time in effect, ie over- developing. Conventionally a stop bath 5% acetic acid will do , it takes less than 30 seconds to neutralize the effect of dev.

For making transmission H's with lowest scatter levels , it is likely to be best to use fixer (eg.~15% sodium thiosulfate "hypo" solution) for ~5 mins with agitation and then rinse well under tap and then use

use iodine -alcohol bleach. Which is a crystal or two of iodine dissolved in about 10 ml of alcohol and then diluted with 3 parts of water.

However this system is not suitable for Denisyuk reflection H's.

We have discussed this recently on the forum and I dont want to bore you with too much info on theory.

I paste the discussion below for anyone interested.

Ferric EDTA bleach is the best type (DEwise) for Denisyuk reflection H's but not transmission ones.

So what happens with that type used WITHOUT FIXING in thiosulfate, is that the developed up silver gets re-oxidized to AgBr. But instead of returning you to square one and leaving you with a uniform coating and distribution of AgBr again just as it was before you exposed , it is energetically more favorable for the re-made AgBr to move over to the adjacent dark fringe made up of virgin AgBr and grow onto that dark fringe using the virgin AgBr grains as seeding centers. That actually requires the bleach solution to have some AgBr solvation ability to enable this carry over effect to occur. It has been found not to occur if the fringe spacing is too great as happens often with transmission Hs(ref. Hariharan). It also cannot happen if the bleach solution has no AgBr solvation ability such as with iodine-alcohol bleach. So the great thing about this carryover effect is that it theoretically uses all the original Ag in your emulsion to make the fringes whereas if you have to use fix you would have removed about half of your original silver content in the thiosulfate solution.

The not- good- thing about it though is that the original sensitizing dyes become chemically locked into AgBr grains making the emulsion very vulnerable to print out. (This doesnt happen with dev-fix-bleach because the dyes get freed up and removed either in the fix or with the help of a bit of alcohol in the final rinse. This is convenient for transmission Hs where printout can be a more serious issue.)

It is probably best is to kill off the dyes with a 2% potassium or ammonium dichromate bath-- it takes less than a minute after you have used the Ferric EDTA bleach.

Jeff

The chemistry mentioned was JD-2 , which is a catechol + ascorbic acid + urea + sulfite + sodium carbonate developer with a dichromate reducing bleach.

I have little experience with transmission holos, but agree that with Jeff that stop bath and/or fixer might be worth a try. BTW, cheap white vinegar is totally artificial, consisting of 4 to 5% acetic acid - I have found it works just fine as a stop bath. I usually dilute to 1%. Rinse briefly before stop bath, otherwise you can get pinholes due to CO2 bubbles forming in the emulsion.

Re-reading your previous post, you mention the film being pitch black after development. I should have caught this as it indicates likely overexposure, especially for a transmission holo which should be medium grey. I can see how this could EASILY happen with a 50 mW laser and the small film size you are using. You might be in the realm where the required exposure is too short to do by hand. Try either putting a neutral density filter in the beam path to reduce the power by a factor of 10 or so, or expand the beam to a much larger size to achieve the same thing and start with a 1 second exposure increasing until you get a reasonable density. Also try developing (but not bleaching) a small piece of unexposed film to see how dark it gets. You want the exposed result to be darker than this, but not pitch black!

Re reflection holos with VRP, past posts have suggested that it's not ideal for this, but you should be able to easily get something visible with a bright object close to the plate. I would suggest easy objects like pearls or bright white figurines against a dark background rather than metallic objects.

The stability requirement for reflection holos are higher so you might need to allow more time for the film and setup to settle.

The history of JD-1,2,3 and maybe 4.

I had known of Photographers' Formulary from black and white photography days. I was using them to get some of the more esoteric chemistry not available at camera stores to make holographic formulae.

I was the Technical Editor of the late great, sorely lamented holosphere, The Advocate of Holographic Science, Technologyand Art, and called up Bill Wilson (may he rest in peace) to advertise in it. He became a regular.

Sometime around this era, TJ (Dr. Tung Jeong) asked me what I was using to get such bright holos. I told him it was the Pyrochrome process, and as much as it gets dissed nowadays it was a big improvement over what was being used at the time. (The original article that popularized it came out in holosphere in July/August 1981, with the originator, Ruud van Renesse, the gentleman who invented it, publishing it in 1980.)

Blammo, out came JD-1 holographic processing kit from Photographers' Formulary! But there were some problems as anyone who ever used that system knows, most annoying the shelf life of the developer.

Then around 1984 or so the batches of Agfa plates had a real hard emulsion, and the hardening action of the pyrogallol developer inhibited deep penetration, so you would get decent developed density but dim holos since all that activity must have ben at the surface. That's when I tried CWC2, and it really worked! It has urea, which I think helps the developer soften the gelatin and penetrate deep to the depths of fringes near the substrate, and keeps things bright. You should read more about this chemistry in [http://holographyforum.org/files/cwc2.htm](http://holographyforum.org/files/cwc2.htm), since we're just going through the history here.

So TJ then asked me if I knew of anything better that lasted longer in the tray. Sure, I said, CWC2. Blammo again, JD-2 kits hit the market, however they used the 'Chrome-reversal-shrinking bleach since CWC2 uses the dreaded PBQ. So the default color for this processing regime in reflection mode was typically green.

Then Jeff Blythe published his copper sulfate bleach recipe, which was a rehalogenating non-shrinking brew, and Ker-Blam, JD-3 went on the market using this bleach!

Then JD-4 is one of Jeff's recipes for a developer with the copper sulfate bleach.

So that is all I know about the JD kits at Photographers' Formulary.
