Dinesh Fri Dec 31, 2004 6:06 pm #4
I'm not sure that the quality of a new product justifies, or not, the claim to precedence or invention. Under this argument one could argue that the Wright Brothers didn't make the first aircraft because it was't a good aircraft. In this situation, what exactly would qualify as "good" since the certainly the first jet (Me 242 in 1944?) wasn't a "good" jet either. Of course, you can extrapolate this argument to pretty much any technological discovery in the last 300 years.

In fact, Leith and Upatniks first discovered the principle of the off axis reference (not, note, a "laser hologram") in the 50's while working on single sideband radar. Since this was a classified project, they were not free to publish until many years later, sometime in the early 60's. This is all laid out in"The Art and Science of Holography: A Tribue to Emmet Leith and Yuri Denisyuk" edited by Benton ( [http://www.amazon.com/exec/obidos/tg/de ... 3?v=glance](http://www.amazon.com/exec/obidos/tg/detail/-/0819450197/104-7289131-1614343?v=glance) ). The technique was invented by Gabor to improve the resolution of optical microscopes (not electron ones). I believe it was never meant as an imaging tool - Gabor used an imaging technique as proof of concept. When imaging was researched in the 50's the on-axis reference posed a serious problem since the -1 and +1 orders were along the same line of sight. However, after the laser was invented, someone almost certainly made a Gabor-type hologram with a laser (hence Leith et al did not make the "first laser transmission hologram") Leith and Upatniks came across the off-axis technique for solving the problem of the -1 and +1 and published first. It may well be that someone else from ca 1955 to ca 1963 may have tried off axis but never published, but the first published use of off-axis reference belongs to Leith and Upatniks - the first "laser transmission holoram" probably doesn't.

By the way, Leith is a very modest person. He's often dismissed questions of his role in holography and downplays it all, as does Yuri Denisyuk. One of the very early workers trying to solve the the +1/-1 problem was El Sum. When I asked Prof Denisyuk about El Sum, the floodgates opened. I got a fascinating look at the early days of holography through his eyes![/list]

"Natural holograms" are pretty old. Butterflies show colors due to diffraction and interference effects. and these evolved many millions of years ago. I'm not an entomologist, but I believe insects were around before dinosaurs.

[quote:070d494b62="JohnFP"]I wonder if those fringes could be studied to determine if there was a rock which modulated the second, receding wave on the coast at some point?[/quote:070d494b62]

The rock, presumably at the bottom of the pond, would not modulate the waves as if it were an "object" in a holographic setup since no wave energy actually hits the rock and returns to the recording surface (the surface of the pond). However, the veloccity and amplitude (height) of a wave depends on the depth to the floor of the lake because the energy of the wave comes from reflections off the lake floor. Hence the rock would change the height of the receeeding wave and the incoming wave. Effectively, the "fringe contrast" would be reduced. By noting the local "fringe contrast" with and without the rock, you could infer the size and shape of the rock.

I'm not sure it matters. Joy and I have had this argument several times: What exactly is a hologram? Is it

A) The process of recording an interference pattern, any interference pattern?,

B) The recorded pattern, irrespective of how that pattern got there (ie, genetically or with reference and object beams)?

C) The reconstructed wavefront, irrespective of how that wavefront is generated?

If A, then the interference pattern on a lace curtain folding over itself is a hologram. If B, then the butterfly's wings is a hologram. So is the interference effects on an oil slick. If not C, then you exclude computer generated holograms.

Firstly, I thought you were talking of waves on the surface of a lake with a submerged rock. However, in this case, I actually don't see any pattern on the sand. The sand at the beach seems smooth with no ripples when the wave draws back. I suspect you'd need some fairly heavy wave motion to create ripples in the sand. Basically wave motion is caused by tidal forces. However, the mass of water moving along a coastline carries kinetic energy. This kinetic energy causes a kind of constancy of mass as the waves move. Thus if the water is going along a shallow area, then the mass increases and so a greater amount of water is transported, hence a higher wave. The greater the energy in the wave, the higher the wave for a given depth of water. If the wave is going in a perfectly perpendicular direction to the wavefront, then the momentum is in a forward direction, but if the wavefront is not perpendicular, then there is momentum transfer into the surface along which the wave is travelling, the sand.

So, if the incoming wave is perfectly perpendicular, then the wave hits the rock and transfers momentum to it causing the rock to move one way and the wave to move another. The returning wave then meets oncoming waves and, since monentum transfer is perpendicular, you get spray (where else can the water go?) If the wave is not perpendicular, then some momentum may be transferred into the surface. This momentum will cause the sand particles to move in some direction, depending on the momentumn vector. The trouble with taking the water analogy too far is that water does have longitudinal modes, which electromagnetic waves cannot (how else can sound be transmitted in water?)

So, I think it's possible that you may form "transmission fringes" in the sand if the sand were very light, the water had tremendous energy with momentum transfer in a slightly downward direction and the rock were very large compared to the wavelength of the water waves. You might want to look up some stuff on acoustic holography, where an ealy scheme was to acoustically record fringes on water to be replayed optically off the water surface.
