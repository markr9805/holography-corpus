When I finally get a new lab built, I plan to start burning flexible film for the first time. Until now, I've been exposing on small glass plates. Plates were so easy! I frankly am finding the thought of dealing with flexible silver halide film rather daunting.

My question being: Will glycerin work OK for index matching of VRP-M film between glass sheets during exposure? I understand VRP-M to be your basic silver halide emulsion (60-80 microns thick) on triacetate substrate 180 microns thick.

> Justin W: Lamp oil comes highly recommended but my lord that sounds like a messy hassle! I don't relish the thought of having to handle the film enough to wash off an oil before development - ug.

Instead of lamp oil (paraffin), you could try a couple of solvents also – see [http://www.holowiki.com/index.php/Index_Matching](http://www.holowiki.com/index.php/Index_Matching).

White spirit and alike may be much easier to remove from your emulsion: prior to development you'd simply apply a hair dryer to have the solvent evaporate.

> Glycerin, however, is clear, viscous and water soluble - yay!BUT.... Is also classified as a sugar alcohol and a humectant. Will it swell my emulsion while all I'm asking it to do is match indices of refraction?

Glycerol is likely to introduce a lot of instability to your emulsion. Depending on your lab's environment, it may attract considerable amounts of water (from air “humidity”). Moreover, small quantities of glycerol may get absorbed by the triacetate film base.

> I'm shooting with green light and frankly don't want anything to do with a pre-swell. Aside from the potential for blue-shift, I also cringe at the thought of this swelling leading to movement of the film. What can I say... I'm a worrywart.

It's true the TEA pretreatment can be messy.

Put aside the blue shift, TEA may have the benefit of doubling film speed.

> My question being: Will glycerin work OK for index matching of VRP-M film between glass sheets during exposure? I understand VRP-M to be your basic silver halide emulsion (60-80 microns thick) on triacetate substrate 180 microns thick.

Due to the instabilities glycerin is causing, I wouldn't use it.

Emulsion thickness is 6-8 microns.

And FILMOTEC film might be an alternative to VRP-M.

Martin Tue Apr 22, 2008 2:01 pm #7
> Justin W: Segue #1 Pretreatments - I have heard that TEA pretreatment of film (for the purpose of a pre-swell) will increase sensitivity and allow for shorter exposures. Is this result unique to triethanolamine or will any pretreatment hypersensitize film for having washed out some of the bromine? Perhaps more to the point: Will a pre-wash in distilled water hypersensitize to the same degree as a pretreatment in TEA?

No, TEA does provide more efficient hypersensitization than a water wash.

There are other means of hypersensitizing agents though – I think we've covered several of them here on the forum. TEA may be the one to have the least side effects (with respect to toxicity, fogging, grain growth etc.). And no, TEA won't wash out the silver bromide.

> Justin W: Segue #2 White spirits for index matching - In my limited experience with volatile solvents, I've noted them in general to be very thin, very runny liquids. Will paint thinner etc stay in place between film and glass for long enough to settle and expose a glass/film/glass sandwich held vertically? That's my fear. That's been my experience the last time I did that with PFG-01 or PFG-03 film. But that's been a while ago. I heard Slavich had improved their TAC substrate film in the meantime. I believe their emulsion now is coated on a 250um TAC film (compared to 180um or something like that previously).

With the Slavich films I managed to get high stability by index matching a plexi sheet on top of the film (which in turn had been index matched on a glass plate). That allowed exposure times up to 15 minutes.

Agfa used to produce films that could easily be index matched with white spirit etc. on a glass plate – no sandwiching (additional glass plate) was required.

Regarding Filmotec, I gathered they acquired one of the Agfa devices to make triacetate film on their own. So this might be good news with regards to the film curling issue.

Jeffrey Weil Fri Jun 06, 2008 11:46 pm #15
Hello Everyone,

Heres another method that I've used many times. Its never been a problem.

Take your film and laminate it down to glass, emulsion out, using a water clear adhesive that is non-birefringent. I used Permatrans from MacTac. I'm sure there are others. They might call their products something else by now.

After you lam the film to the glass let it “set” for a day or more. You will notice it gets nice and flat. After that....you have a glass plate! None of our chemistry will mess with the adhesive, it will never turn yellow or anything like that. I have masters that are 20 years old produced this way. Not one has failed over the years.

This type of adhesive is a plastic carrier with adhesive on both sides. Also on both sides is a clear poly release liner. What I used to do was peel one side, lam the film down and trim the adhesive so it was slightly larger than the film. I would then peel the other side and laminate it to glass. Then I cut off the excess laminate with a razor and peel it off the glass.

I used to do 4x5's with a cheap hand roller from the art supply store, worked like a charm if you keep things clean. Back then I did not have any clean hood equipment. Just windex and some paper towels.

I also bonded the film to glass that was 1/4 inch thick. It was very nice working with plates that thick.

Good luck
