I purchased the Student version of the Integraf holography kit (contains 12 plates). It arrived on Friday. This weekend I made six holograms. All of them produced visible results, although some were better than others.

I used a bathroom as my darkroom. I put the laser on the left side of the bathtub (made of metal), held by a clothes pin in a cup of sand on a glass support. I used a mouse pad to hold the subjects. When developing, I left the plates in the developer for 2 minutes, followed by first rinse for two minutes, then bleach for 2 minutes. Finally 2nd rinse about 3 minutes, followed by about 30 seconds under gently running cold water from the tap. I used distilled water to make all the chemistry, but used tap water for the rinses.

Image #1 was the standard coin setup (reflection hologram) recommended for first hologram. I set the plate (emulsion side down) directly on the coins and exposed for 10 seconds. After the 2nd rinse, I was able within a few minutes to start seeing the shape of the coins (even with drops of water still on parts of the plate). The image gradually improved over a period of several hours until it became quite clear and 3-dimensional when viewed with a point source of light (small penlite flashlight in a dimly lit room).

Image #2 was another reflection hologram. I used a white round plastic lid about 3/4 inch tall. Inside I placed some small electronic components. I placed the plate on top of the lid and exposed as before. The result was a hologram with much more depth, but it was also a lot dimmer than the first hologram.

For #3, I attempted to make a diffraction grating. With the beam incident at an angle of about 45 degrees from horizontal, I leaned a mirror (first surface) against a sturdy cup at an angle of about 10 degrees from vertical such that the reflection interfered with the original beam. I laid the plate emultion side up and exposed as before. I got a strange result from this. By looking through the processed hologram with a magnifying glass, I was actually able to see the individual lines in the grating! I estimated only about 10-20 lines per mm. When I used a red laser pointer, the diffracted images were less than 1/2 degree away from the center beam. I'm not sure what happened here. If I'm looking at the geomety correctly, I would expect the angle to be twice the angle from vertical that the mirror was sitting at. This would produce hundreds of lines per mm. I suspect what I am seeing is some type of interference from the two sides of the glass? Later I tried shining a bright green laser pointer through the plate and was surprised to find weak diffracted images at about 30 degrees. The images were extremely weak but were oriented as expected based on the plate's original position in the beams.

For #4, I attempted to make a reflection hologram that shows a different image depending on the direction of the white light striking it. I used another plastic top, this time black and about 1/2 inch in depth. I placed an electronic component in this, and placed the plate on top. I exposed for 5 seconds. I then replaced the electronic component with a different component, turned the plate 180 degrees (keeping the same side up), and exposed for another 5 seconds. Unfortunately, only the first image is visible. The second must have been too dim or else there was motion.

For #5, I made a transmission hologram. I shined the laser beam on my object (electronic components). I placed a first surface mirror against a small support ("D" battery) and allowed part of the beam to hit this. The rest of the beam illuminated my scene, with the plate also supported vertically by a "D" battery. This scene came out pretty well (clear and fairly bright). For viewing I used a green laser pointer with a diverging lens to spread the beam. Even the mirror itself is visible, along with reflections one of the objects near the edge of the scene in the mirror. Unfortunately, the beam from the mirror was nearly straight into the plate. Thus when viewing the image it is brightest with the laser beam almost dead ahead (shining near one's eyes).

For #6, I again attempted to make a double exposure reflection hologram. I went back to using coins. I set four coins on the mouse pad, heads up. I exposed a plate for 5 seconds. I then turned the coins over (tails up), rotated the plate 180 degrees (keeping the same side up), and exposed another 5 seconds. Although this did work after a fashion, the results are not real good. It is always possible to see both images of the coins at the same time, although the heads image is brighter when illuminated from one side and the tails image is brighter when illuminated from the other side. Note my beam was at about a 45 degree angle from horizontal.

Also, I noticed that film is about half the price of plates (or at least nearly the same price for twice the width, so I could cut each piece in half). Frank-

Sounds like you're on your way! Congrats on joining the 'Wow, I made a Hologram!" club...

As for some of your questions:

film vs plates vs how many:

Film is definitely cheaper, although you will have to work out a way to hold it motionless. This isn't as simple as just clamping it between two pieces of glass - I spent considerable time engineering this, and could help out if you decide to go this route. Long term, I think you want to be able to use film for testing setups - such as the holograms I sent you.

The emulsions should last a reasonable length of time, especially if they're not (almost) out of date when you get them. Keeping them in a fridge helps, but means you have to let them come up to temperature before you use them. The holograms I sent you are made on Agfa 8E75 (no longer available) which has a date of 5/1994.

You may not want to buy too much of any one type of film - I found I experimented with many different types before settling on ones I liked.

The large stripes you see may be 'woodgrain', an interference pattern formed by reflections from the front and rear surface of the plates. This can be eliminated by getting the polarization of the laser oriented correctly with respect to the plate. It's a lengthy explanation on how to do it - I suggest you look up 'polarization', 'Brewster Angle', or 'woodgrain' in the forum. Or, find more texts on holography at your local library.

I'm assuming from your description that the mirror-plate assembly formed an "L" with the "mirror arm" of the "L" slightly tilted at 10 degrees. In this case, your two beams came in at +45 and -45 with a 90 degree seperation. Actually +45 and -65 if the mirror were 10 degrees from the vertical. I'm also assuming that these were largely parallel. In this case, the fringes would probably be interference lines from the light going through the front-end emulsion, hitting the back of the glass and reflecting back to the front-end emulsion. Due to refraction, there's an angle between the front-end beam and the beam reflected off the back of the plate and this variation in angle causes low frequency fringes. If you used a collimated beam, these fringes would all be the same seperation, if you used a divergent beam, the fringe seperation would vary from the middle to either edge of the plate. The angle of the input front-end beam varies across the plate if the beam were diverging.The effect is a sort of Newton's Fringes effect, so you may want to look that up. This effect is also sometimes called woodgrain because in a more comples setup, the beams off the object illumination come in at all sorts of varying angles and the pattern looks like woodgrain. You may also want to look up "Wood's anomaly".

Going back to your grating, the expected line frequency is given by

nu =[sin(theta_1) + sin(theta_2)]/lambda

I hope the maths doesn't annoy you (there are people on this forum who scream bloody murder when I put down equations!). Any, in your case

theta_1 = 65 degrees, theta_2 = -45 degrees. Thus the expected line frequency is:

nu = [sin(45) + sin(65)]/0.000633 l/mm = 2549 l/mm (approx)

Assuming you shone the laser pointer a the finished hologram normally (ie at right angles) then the expected diffraction angle is:

theta_d = (inv sin)(lambda*nu) = inv sin(1.6) = impossible!

You therefore needed to hit the plate at +45 or -65 in which case you'd see diffraction come out at the other angle. The 30 degree dim hologram you saw may well have been caused by spurious gratings caused by random reflections inside the plate. The light bounces all over the place and you usually get a whole load of unwanted "spurious" gratings.

The actual efficencies and output angles is a complicated effect depending on how thick your emulsion is and how high the spatial frequency is, but generally, the beam coming in at one shooting angle, diffracts into the other shooting angle. If you input the beam at an angle other than the shooting angle in a transmission geometry, then you can work out the angle of diffraction using the equation above, bearing in mind this is an approximation. Other factors that cause low efficencies have to do with beam ratio, exposure development and motion. This is more a matter of trial and error. Personally, I think if you're bleaching takes two minutes, you may be overexposing. But this is just a glib, off-hand statement so don't take it too seriously.

[quote:cea98eddee="fkienast"]Third, what is the best way to store my holograms, and how long can I expect them to last? [/quote:cea98eddee]

Tough to say. It depends a lot on temperature, humidity and original age of the plates. Putting them in a fridge is a good idea if you let them come to temp before you use them. On the other hand, when you do take them out due forms on the plates causing high humidity, which is counter-productive so don't leave them in the fridge too long or ina fridge whose temperature is not constant over the period. I've had plates left in ordinary room conditions for years. In London, we used 10 year old plates. In San Diego, CA, I have plates about 5 years old kept in a steel cabinet at room temp which are fine. As for holograms themselves, there's usually no need to worry about age if they're kept indoors. If they're kept outdoors in sunlight they may print out. This is due to unused silver halide crystals still in the emulsion. Over time and with exposure these start darkening and the whole plates gets black. Good development minimises this risk, exposure to sunlight enhances it. Again, I have holograms that are 20 years old on silver that are just fine. On the other hand, I've seen 5 year old holograms that printed out while in a closed box.

Hope this helps.

An angle is measured as positive or negative depending on the sense of rotation. If you start from the horizontal line and measure the angle in a anti-clockwise sense it's defined (arbitrarily) as a positive angle, while if the angle is measured in an clockwise sense, it's known as negative.

This is a positive angle:

because the angle is measured "up" from the horizontal. If the angle were to be measured "down", it'd be negative. In the case of trig functions, the sin of a negative angle is negative:

sin(-thate) = -sin(theta)

The standard equation for the fringe density of two off-axis beams is:

(lambda)(nu) = sin(theta_1) - sin(theta_2)

where:

..............................X..............Y

............................X.............Y

..........................X.........Y

theta_1 (say) is made by the 'X' beams and theta_2 is made by the 'Y' beams and 'P' is the plate. Nu is the spatial frequency (fringe density) and lambda the wavelength. In your case, and most common cases, the 'Y' beams comes from the other side of the axis, from "below" as it were. In this case,theta_2 is now negative, since it's measured clockwise (down from the horizontal). In this situation:

nu = {{sin(theta_1)} - {sin(-theta_2)}}/lambda = {{sin(theta_1)} - {-sin(theta_2)}}/lambda = {sin(theta_1) + sin(theta_2)}/lambda.

The fact that one angle is positive and the other negative gives the '+' sign for the sin functions.

In your case, you're quite right, the angles would be 25 and 45. Thus, the fringe density is:

nu = sin(45)+sin(25)/0.000633 = 1784 l/mm

In this case, if you hit the plate with a normal laser beam at 530nm, you'd expect diffraction at

theta_d = (inv sin)(0.000531784) = 71 degrees.

Bear in mind, however, that this calculation depends on the fact that the surface grating is also at 1784 l/mm, since that is what's doing the diffracting for an off-Bragg input beam. Any volume gratings inside the emulsion would not be diffracting because they're off-Bragg. In this case, with a relatively high frequency, the surface gratings may be weak and so you may get a pretty weak diffracted beam at the 71 degree angle. If you came in at 25 with the Integraf laser, you'd see diffraction at 45 on the other side of the plate.

Dinesh Mon Aug 08, 2005 1:24 pm #16
Motion will cause dimness. You're trying to record a very small set of fringes which should have a sinusoidal variation. As with any photograph, if you try to record the fringes as they move, the image of the fringes will blur. This will cause the fringes to "smear" and so the recorded fringes will no longer be sinusoidal. The diffraction off these fringes depends on the sinusoidal nature of the fringes, therefore the greater the blur the less the sinusoidal variation and so the less the diffraction leading ot a weak reconstruction. Clearly, if the fringes blur to such an extent that the whole fringe set simply becomes a uniform grey, there are no fringes and you get no reconstruction.

However, motion is not the only thing that can cause a dim hologram. The density you take the development to may be a cause, the amount that you bleach is also important - you need to bleach until the film goes clear and then another 30 seconds to a minute. If you're using a rehalogenating bleach, clarity of the film doesn't mean the silver has been all rehalogenated.

If you suspect motion, try this. Take two mirrors and glue them together in a broad "V" shape, with the apex angle at about 80 degrees. Now glue the base of the V directly onto the edges (or face, if the mirror assembly is small) of the emulsion side (let's call it side A) of the plate. Now bring in light from the back, non-emulsion (B) side, through the film, onto the V so as to cover both halves of the V. Tony Sat Aug 13, 2005 10:37 pm #26
Frank-

Your getting close to the point where you might benefit from some of my experience with clamping film between glass...

You noted that you needed larger pieces of glass so you don't get shadows of the clips on the film. If you simply do this, with larger pieces of glass, and the clamping pressure is outside the perimeter of the film, unless the glass is very thick, it will bow outward a bit due to the leverage of the clamps acting on the periphery of the film. This bowing results in enough room for the film to move around a bit during the exposure.

What I recommend is shimming the glass plates with a couple layers of clear tape around the outer edge of the film area, where the clamps apply pressure. This way the plates remain flat and will prevent film motion in the central area. You want to match the film thickness with the right number of layers of tape. It also provides a convenient way to locate the film in the same place every time...
