Dinesh Fri Jun 25, 2004 5:00 am #7
About your question of coherence lengths. If you could get the whole scene of 50 cm in the H1, then you do not need a 50 cm coherence length to image the H1 onto an H2. The original H1 has the depth encoded into it through the spatial frequency. the greater depth will have created a greater spatial frequency. Once the object waveform is recorded, the reconstructed object wavefront only has to be coherent vis a vis the reference beam. The "object depth" is encoded within the object wavefront. The shape of the object wavefront will carry the phase information of the depth of the original object scene.

Mirror alignment will affect the power of the beam, not the coherence length. At least, not much. The laser cavity supports many 'modes'. This means the number of ways a complete set of waves fit into the cavity. The cavity can also support a range of wavelengths. The greater the number of modes or the wavelengths, the shorter the coherence length. If the laser cavit has only one wavelength, say 633 nm, and only one mode so that the whole cavity takes half a wave (TEM00) then your coherence length is infinite. In reality, there is a range of wavelengths, called the 'bandwidth' of the laser which reduces the coherence length. If the mirror is misaligned, you'll lose power but, unless it's very badly misaligned, you should not introduce extra wavelengths or extra modes. To reduce the bandwidth, use an etalon. This 'purifies' the range of wavelengths so that this range is reduced and you get better coherence length.

I like PBQ better than PBU. it's more dangerous, but it's a little faster. The extra spedd seems to make less noise.

Dinesh Fri Jun 25, 2004 1:21 pm #9
[quote:a810edfa07]I don't think the spatial frequency is related to the depth of image, or coherence length, but simply to angle between the object and reference beam. [/quote:a810edfa07]

For two point sources - true. For an extended source - not true. The object field recorded on the plate is the far field Fraunhofer diffraction pattern. This is represented mathematically by an FT with certain terms as coefficants. The larger and more extended the object, the greater the variation in the amplitude field and so the larger the number of terms you need to go before truncating the series on the FT. These higher order terms constitute a higher set of spatial frequencies.

You may remember the early examples of Polaroid film. They used to send out a hologram with a single coin on them. Once more the "Kaveh Roar" went up: "The only reason they're shooting coins is that their film can't take the spatial frequencies for a deep image!"
