Ed Wesly Mon Sep 10, 2007 7:55 pm #21
Pillars of Light Dot Matrix pattern is available from most of the wide web manufacturers in one form another. The “pillars” come about when a column of dot matrix gratings diffract light into the viewer’s eyes.

But what are these Dot Matrix gratings? The Dot Matrix machines were invented in the mid to late ‘80’s. Some people say that Frank Davis’s Light Machine was the first, but certainly Craig Newswanger invented his own device independently.

The Dotz! as Craig christened them are holographic diffraction gratings exposed in small shapes, usually circles or squares, with packing densities from 100 per inch to 1200! Some machines have a shape wheel, which gives a choice of circles, squares, triangles, and in the case of the Newswanger style of machine, squares that have an alpha-numeric character in them. (For an example of this type of micro-text, check out the border around the Windows Logo on Microsoft Windows disks, with a magnifying glass.)

There are a variety of machines in use around the world, the above mentioned Light Machines of Davis, the DOTZ! machines of Newswanger, Steve McGrew’s devices from New Light Industries, the Dazzle and Sparkle machines from Ahead Optoelectronics, and something or other (I don’t know the name) from Spatial Imaging. Undoubtedly there others lurking here and there.

What they all have in common is the ability to expose the small dots with two interfering beams. In the Newswanger machine (with which I am most familiar) both the beams are incident symmetrically, about 25 degrees apart at He-Cd or 405 diode wavelengths. The raw beam is apertured off by the selected shape’s opening to form a regular grid on the holographic plate.

Some other machines have one beam incident to the photo-resist plate along the normal, the other at an angle. These devices can vary the intra-beam angle on the fly, increasing or decreasing the grating’s spatial frequency so that a series of dots next to each other can take on different colors!

What makes those shimmery effects in the pattern is the other parameter in the optical path, the grating rotation. The orientation of the fringe pattern can be horizontal, vertical, or anywhere inbetween. When the pattern is viewed with light from above, all the gratings whose fringes are horizontal will light up, plus those whose fringes are close to horizontal. But those dots whose gratings are vertical will not be glowing to this observer, but they may be visible to someone else.

If the pattern is rotated, so that those gratings whose interference patterns were not horizontal during exposure, but now are horizontal, will light up, but those previously lit up are not visible. This is how the Pillars of Light move across the package.

Conversely, the reconstructing reference angle may be stationary, and as the observer walks around the pattern different areas will light up, depending on the orientation of the gratings. So as someone walks down the aisle of the supermarket, the cans of shaving cream or boxes of toothpaste will be casting rainbows their way, catching their eye, screaming “Buy me! Buy me!”

Since the DOTZ! patterns are surface relief gratings, there is always light diffracted in some direction, which makes these holographic tricks appealing to packaging manufacturers. Since the DOTZ! are so small, decent reconstructions are capable under the broad sources of fluorescent fixtures.

It takes a long time to run one of these patterns, even in a 6” by 6” format. (Consider 300 dpi, that’s 1800 X 1800 = 3,240,000 exposures!) The machine will start, find its home position, go to dot #1, rotate the optics to give the required grating angle and spatial frequency, expose, (usually on the order of milli- or even micro- seconds, since it is such a small area), stepper motorized table moves to the next position, settle for a few milliseconds, expose, move, settle, and on and on.

So if you look closely, with a magnifying glass at the above-mentioned Rolling Stone cover, along with a lot of other packaging, you can see the DOTZ! If you run your undiverged laser beam across the DOTZ! you will see the diffracted orders spin around the zero order.

In answer to Colin’s question above, the angle between the two beams that made the original pattern can be calculated by measuring the angles of diffraction from the interrogation. Usually the angle during exposure is designed to diffract red out of the pattern at 35 to 45 degrees, depending on whose machine is being utilized.

The Pillars of Light patterns are usually available in 1” or 2” pillars; in other words, as the table moves from dot to dot positions, the grating is rotated, so that it goes through 180 degrees of rotation over 1” of table movement. For some reason this is one of the most popular patterns of all time!
