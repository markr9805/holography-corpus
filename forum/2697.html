Dinesh Tue Aug 31, 2004 2:00 pm #5
[quote:19402dd65d]Struggled to get any depth beyone about an inch[/quote:19402dd65d]

I assume you're doing what known as a "Denisyuk" hologram. That is, the object is on one side of the plate and the beam comes in from the other, through the plate and hits the object, causing it to reflect onto the back of the plate. If this is so, the depth of the object depends on whats known as the "coherence length" of your laser. The laser has a property that it's "laser" propertiies - it's coherence - die out after a particular length. beyond that length, it's no longer a laser, but a very bright LED. If the distance from plate to back of object and back to plate is greater than this distance, you will only image those parts of an object that's within this distance. So, if your object length were, say an inch, and you placed the object half an inch behind the plate. Now the coherence length needs to be an inch and a half to back of object and an inch and a half to the plate again - three inches. If your actual coherence length were only an inch, you'd only capture half an inch of the object. You could always choose thinner and thiner objects closer and closer to the plate to find the actual coherence length. There's also a technique known as a "Michaelson Interferometer" with which you can immediately know the coherence length. It's in any book on holography or interferometry. Basically, take the beam along two different paths and let them recombine down a common path. You'll get interference fringes. As you vary one or the other path of the two paths, the fringes get dimmer (you lose contrast), until you get no more fringes. The path difference between the two paths is the coherence length.

[quote:19402dd65d]- Would a transmission hologram ALWAYS produce the image infront of the slide? [/quote:19402dd65d]

Depends on what you mean by "in front". A transmission hologram is one where both the object and the reference beam are from the same side of the plate. Generally the other side from where you are. In other words, when you put your plate into the plate holder and look through it, you see an illuminated object behind the plate. I'm assuming plate, but the argument remains the same if you're using film, so long as you mount the film so you can see through while shooting. If you now turn your head you see the reference beam as a point of light somewhere off to one side, but again, on the other side of the plate from you - the object side. If the plate/film is developed and replaced in the same orientation, the holographic image appears in the same original position, ie, behind the plate from where you're standing. If you flipped the plate so that the part of the plate that was originally on the other side from you is now at the closer side to you, then the object appears in front of the plate, ie between you and the plate.

[quote:19402dd65d]Would a transmission hologram be able to capture more depth[/quote:19402dd65d]

Again, depends on the coherence length of your laser. This coherence length effectively sets the maximum depth of the object you can shoot.

[quote:19402dd65d]can anyone show me a good setup for a transmission hologram[/quote:19402dd65d]

There are two ways, essentially the same way. The idea is to have the same beam both illuminate the hologram and form a reference. The "simplest" way is known as a single beam transmission hologram. You expand the beam, place the plate/film at an angle to the incoming beam, say about 60 degrees. This means that if you look directly into the plate, you'd need to turn your head through 60 degrees to see the source of the expanded beam. The beam should be sufficently expanded so that some part of the beam completely misses the plate. Place your object there so that the part of the beam that misses the plate is shining on the object. Rotate the object so that this reflected light falls on a white card that you previously placed in your plate holder (you did previously place a white card, didn't you?

) This is "simple", insofar as the setup is simple - an expanded beam, a plate and an object. The difference between the distance from the source of the beam to the plate and between the source of the beam to the back of the object and then to the plate must be within the coherence length of your laser. Obviously this restricts the kind of object you can make since a non-flat object will shadow or not throw any light onto the plate except from it's edges. When I first started, I started with a single beam transmission and could not figure out how to shoot my porcelain clay bear without shadowing.

Another method is the two beam, or split beam, transmission. In this method, you split the beam into two with a beamsplitter. This splits the beam into one that transmits through the beamsplitter and one that reflects off it. One of the two, I'd recommend the transmiitted light, then goes through an expanding lens and various mirrors to finally fall on the plate at some angle. Most holographer use 60 degrees because of something called "Brewster's angle". The other, reflected, beam also goes through an expanding lens and various mirrors. However, this time the beam illuminates the object. By placing your mirrors carefully, you can choose the direction from which the illuminating light comes in. It needs to reflect off the object onto the plate so that if you look through the plateholder, you see a very brightly lit object, but from any other direction, the object looks dim or non-existant. Again, the path difference from beamsplitter to plate and beamsplitter to back of object to plate needs to be smaller than the coherence length of your laser. This set up is much more complex, but has greater flexibility. You can pretty much choose any object so long as it's not deeper than the coherenvce length and is solid enough. The lighting of the object is the key. If you choose a variable beamsplitter so that the proportion of light going through it to that going off it can be varied, then you can vary the ratio of ligth from reference to light from object. This ratio should be about 4:1, you'll get a bright hologram, depending on how you process.

If you don't get a bright hologram, give up and take up card tricks! No, I'm joking! Very few people ever get a good hologram the first few times. This game requires patience and dedication. Keep at it!

Tom B. Wed Sep 01, 2004 5:25 am #7
When viewing a Denisyuk reflection holo under white light, objects get blurrier as the distance from the plate increases and they become harder and harder to see. To see all the depth in your holo, view it in a darkened room using the spread beam from your laser - this only works if the chemical processing did not shift the color of the hologram away from the original laser color, which is the case with JD-4 processing.

In my experience, if diode lasers work at all, the coherence length is quite large - much larger than the useful blur-free depth of the reflection holo, so I doubt that's your problem. Re single-beam transmission holos, it can be tricky but it is usually possible to llght objects without shadowing the plate - if they are not too close to the plate. Usually at least one mirror is needed to bounce light on to the object. As with reflection holos, the (rainbow) image gets blurrier with depth and they are clearest when viewed with laser illumination.
