This is a bit low, but my plates never go out into direct sunlight. If you intend to expose it to direct sunlight, wash longer in both cases.

[quote:abb14d3d44="andy75180"] I did read about polarization of the beam. So tried turning the diode 90 degrees, but made no difference.[/quote:abb14d3d44]

Polarisation may affect the brightness of your image, but will make no difference to fogging or printout. Well, almost no difference. It may cause scatter within the emulsion which may spread the printout, but this will be a small effect. It does cause "woodgrain" if the reference is at the wrong angle and/or the wrong polarisation. The "right" angle and polarisation is about 60 (Brewster's angle) with horizontal pol if you're shooting with a horizontal setup and vertical if you're setup is vertical. However, take this with a pinch of salt. ( [http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1576](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1576) )

Dinesh Tue Nov 09, 2004 3:19 pm #24
I've always kept the film in the dark until after bleaching, under the theory that the unexposed grains will gain specks. However, when you reversal bleach, as you do with PBU, you just put the silver halide back in anyway. We covered this at some length in the old forum.

However, thinking about it, if you don't at least stop the action of the developer before you turn on the lights it seems you have a race condition. The unexposed silver halide grains develop specks, depending on the strength of the white light. The developer still in the emulsion "finds" them and reduces the entire speck to silver - fog - depending on the developer concentration still in the emulsion. If you either wash the plate and/or don't expose them to too much white light , as Tom does, or put it in a stop bath, as Martin does, then it seems that the specks produced on the grains by exposing the developed film will not reduce, since there's no more developer. Now, since the bleach itself does not reduce the silver grains (quite the opposite!) the plate should be relatively safe. It seems to hinge on how much developer is still in the plate when you expose to white light after development and before bleach.

[quote:692316c5f2="andy75180"]So I give you this example. Experiment 1: Plate close to laser and less exposure. Experiment 2: Plate further away from beam but longer exposure.

I was thinking that if the plate was exposed to exactly the same amount of light in both experiments, both plates should be identical. Is this so? [/quote:692316c5f2]

Depending on exactly how much longer the exposure is in experiment 2, yes this is so. The beam energy falls off as the square of the distance (assuming you're using a divergent or convergent beam - not true for collimated beams). So ideally you need to get expose such that the ratio of the exposures is in ratio to the square of the distances:

E_2/E_1 = (d_2/d_1)^2

However, there are some conditions you may need to watch. The beam profile is gaussian. This means that the center of the beam is hot with a fairly fast fall-off. This means that if you're fairly near the plate, or the plate is large relative to the beam diameter, the center of the object would be in the hot zone of the gaussian while the outer edges of the image would be in the fall-off zone. Thus, the center third of the image might be receiving twice or more of the light than the outer 2/3rd (1/3 on either side of the center). I think this is what happened to your image. If you now pull back, the entire image will be inside the hot gaussian center and the entire image will receive the same amount of light. Thus your experiment 2 will show a more uniform result than your experiment 1. In this case, you need to compare the entire image in expt 2 with only the outer edges of the image in expt 1. This is a common problem with single beam Denisyuks, that the center is hot due to the gaussian profile so the center of the image is dim/fogged/non-existant relative to the outer edges. The solutions are to lower the exposure or pull back so that the gaussian covers the entire image. If you do pull back, use the exposure that the edges of the object saw, since that gave you a nice image.

The other thing to watch is that you use the same development times for both experiments. You have to watch that the exposures are in the linear zone of the exposure curve (the H&D curve) otherwise the darkening of the film is non-linear. This is probably not a major concern. I haven't seen H&D curves for PFG-01, but I'm pretty sure tha unless you're shooting a bright, reflective object you're pretty much on the linear portion.

[quote:692316c5f2="andy75180"]Also would the fog problem decrease if I attempted a split beam reflection, which I'm hoping to soon?[/quote:692316c5f2]

yes it might. As I mentioned above, the ratio decides where on the exposure curve you're exposing. A good ratio will get you in the linear portion which avoids overmodulation of the fringes on some parts of the film.
