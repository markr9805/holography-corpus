So I decided that I've outgrown the optical bench that I've been using for the past 4 years or so. For the last several weeks (on and off) I've been working on Ron's holography table 2.0. It's still totally Maguivered (Sp?) but will give me more than twice the space and should perform much better as well. It's built around an industrial lab bench that I bought second hand and then reinforced with 2 by 4s for added strength and stability. On top I put two sheets of 7/16 inch thick smooth steel plate. The working surface is 34 inches by 62 inches. The steel alone weights about 320 lbs. The table (by itself) weighs about 75 lbs and I added about 200 lbs of sand to a basin under the table top for a total weight of 500 to 600 lbs. The whole thing sits on 4 partially inflated inner tubes (from lawn tractor wheels) which then sit on carpeting on top of a concrete floor. I've also been testing stability as I go along with the aid of a Michelson interferometer. The table itself is rock solid! Then I installed a new, much larger (12" by 10"), overhead mirror for my reference beam. When I included that in the path of the interferometer I couldn't believe how much it destabilized my interference fringes! So now I'm in the process of installing added support and stiffness to both the mirror itself (it's only made of 1/4 inch plate glass) and to the framework that I built to support the mirror. Hopefully, I'll have that done by the end of this weekend and I'll run another interferometer test. I am soooooooo glad that I took the slow, careful, meticulous approach. I could easily have thrown the whole thing together and wasted weeks and $$$ before figuring out why I was getting s* holos!

Dinesh Thu Aug 16, 2012 7:49 am #5
> rzeheb: I am soooooooo glad that I took the slow, careful, meticulous approach. I could easily have thrown the whole thing together and wasted weeks and $$$ before figuring out why I was getting s* holos!

This is very, very important. So many people simply assemble stuff together and insist that it must work because they saw it in some publication or another. Some people have actually got a little annoyed at me when I say, "Test, test and re-test". When it doesn't work they either insist that there are gremlins in the system and if they keep on it'll somehow correct itself, or they'll start from scratch and do pretty much exactly the same thing! If and when I'm ever asked, I stress that you need to always have a series of tests at every stage and determine pass/fail criteria. If it fails, you only need go back one stage of the build.

As for your mirror, remember that glass flexes. Large sheets of glass are not particularly rigid and a sheet 10" x 12" may well be bowing in the centre, causing oscillations of the mirror's centre with respect to the edges. You might try adding metal cross bars on the back of the mirror. Try various parts of the mirror, not just the centre, as one of the reflecting surfaces for the Michaelson.

Hey Ron,

Great meeting you too. MIT was a classic for sure!

On this stability thing I have a few bits of advice. John is right about filling the pipes with sand. He beat me to that one. It makes a major difference. Just tap the pipes with something metal with and without sand. You can already imagine the difference in the sound I'm sure. One thing about that isn't so obvious. Don't just fill them up and use the pipe fittings to hold in the sand....one day you will regret it! Cap the ends with some hardening putty or glue or whatever. Use the pipe fittings to only hold it together. Not seal it. Have the sand permanently sealed up in the pipe.

Jam some paper or something inside the pipe a few inches down. Use that base to seal the first end with some permanent glue or putty. After it's dried fill the pipe with sand and use that sand as the base to cap the other end with putty. Putty is much easier to use this way than glue.

Another thing that I've found over the years is the number of parts something is made out of. The more parts, the more flex. Whenever possible make your stuff out of one part.

For example, if your making a big mirror mount of of pipes, when you get to a corner, instead of using extra pipe fittings use a pipe bender if you have access to one. That's the kind of thing I'm talking about. Same with plate holders. I mill mine out of a single piece of metal. No screws, no welds, no nothing. Solid as a rock.

Jeff W

rzeheb Mon Aug 20, 2012 4:20 pm #11
Thanks Dinesh. However, I still have some work to do. In addition to increasing the size of my overhead mirror, I also bought a larger collimating mirror. My original mirror is an 8 inch parabolic reflector mirror, about f/3.7 (29 inch focal length). I bought a used 10 inch, f/4.9, parabolic reflector. The mirror and coating were (notice the use of the past tense ) in excellent shape when I bought the mirror. However, like virtually all mirrors made for reflecting telescopes, this one had a center mark. I knew it when I bought the mirror and I did as much up front due dilligence as I could to convince me that I had at least a reasonable chance to remove the center mark (which for this Orion mirror was simply a sticker) without damaging the coating. Well, I did successfully remove the sticker, but the process left a fair amount of the adhesive behind. I have very slowly and gradually been experimenting with solvents to remove the glue, starting with detergent in deionized water (what was used to soak and remove the sticker in the first place), moving to reagent grade ethanol and finally to reagent grade acetone. The first two solvents did nothing to remove the glue but didn't hurt the coating either. The acetone seems to be dissolving the glue but very very very slowly. In the meantime, it evaporates very very very fast. The net result seems to be that I have been redistributing the glue from a spot in the middle of the mirror to a film precipitating at the edges of the acetone pool (oh, and there is still glue in the middle too ). Nevertheless, the mirror's aluminum coating still seems to be intact and unharmed (I think, not sure exactly what lies under the central mass of glue) so there is still a glimer of hope. Today I brought home yet another organic solvent and I will see if that works any better. If all else fails I am prepared to have the mirror stripped and re-coated. That wouldn't be my first choice, but the cost of the mirror plus the cost of shipping and re-coating is still acceptable compared to the cost of a new mirror (which would still probably come with a center mark gosh darn it!).

Hi Ron, ive been camping last week so i was "off line" , generally good aluminum coated mirrors have a protective over coating of Magnesium-Fluoride allowing you to clean it quite easily, acetone, toluene or the like should work to remove the glue. Finish off cleaning by running warm water (and fingers too)over the face and introducing typical dish washing soap and then doing a quick blow to remove droplets. The alignment dot is added @ the end and is usually a sticker, a slight warming prior to its removal will help to remove it entirely (next time)................

years back when cleaning FS mirrors/lenses in Fairchild Reconnaissance cameras, i was instructed by my mentors to try to clean off the stubborn spots using spit and it worked !

Hi Ron - I've been following all your posts as you improve your table and note that some concern has been expressed in the postings about large 1/4" thick mirrors flexing. I had similar concerns and glued my 1/4" 12" X 10" mirror to a slightly larger sheet of 1/4" aluminium. I used the slow setting Araldite epoxy and warmed the ali. to about 45 deg C so that the mixed Araldite flowed to a thin layer and then dropped gently the mirror on to the epoxy. By that mechanism I believe much of the undersurface of the mirror is actually stuck to the ali and also that it's a very thin layer.
