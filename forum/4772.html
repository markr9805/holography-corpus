Dinesh Tue Apr 19, 2005 3:20 pm #3
Off the top of my head, I'd guess your laser had a pretty good coherence length. If your laser is polarised, the extra time could also be the result of de-polarisation. When the beam goes through the fiber, the light bounces around inside and there is loss at each bounce, depending on the index of fiber/cladding. If, as you say, there was no cladding, then the surprise is that the light stayed in the fiber at all! A lot of these clading-less fibers are used to bleed light out of the edges to create "lines of light" along the fiber length. Anyway, the number of bounces means that the path length of the beam is uncertain to an extent (there is an equation that gives the path length inside the fiber as a function of the fiber length). So, firstly, you need to havve a laser with a long enough coherence length. Also, the fiber depolarises, so that the light coming out of the end is not polarised the same way as the light going in. However, the thinner the fiber, the more it maintains polarisation. Also, the thinner the fiber, the less the actual bouncing. The light travels through the fiber as a sort of "field" (A Bessel function of sorts). These thin fibers are called "single mode" and are very thin - on the order of a few microns.

Of course I don't mind! You're thinking along the same track as I. A couple of things I recently found out:

Launching a beam into fiber via a direct diode connection isn't very expensive... around $400, but the connection needs to be tailor made for a specific wavelength. Splitters run about 200, but polarization maintaining splitters jump up to $1200, so your idea of splitting the off reference beam before hitting the launcher may be better. Another thing.. the couplers typically are only about 20 - 40% efficiency. In my case, my 25 mW diode would only emit 7 mW at the fiber output. And that's before splitting.

As Saxby states, randomized polarization really only becomes an issue if the fiber run is stressed or too long, but that's only with a single run. There are no guarantees when running the beam through a non-PM splitter.

Ed Wesly Mon Apr 25, 2005 12:38 pm #24
There was an airplane pilot named Bill Durrell who got the holography bug and took the Lake Forest workshops. He built and designed a contraption that would launch a laser into three separate fibers, all single mode, polarization-preserving. It used polarization type beamsplitters, and each fiber had its own alignment mechanism.

I got the pleasure of Beta-testing this thing. I had been shooting unslanted transmission gratings with each beam incident at 45 degrees to the plateholder. We plopped two of the fiber ends in front of the spatial filters, but they had to be fairly close.

Since incident flux was down from coupling losses, I adjusted the exposure to compensate. Developed nicely, but that's the only good thing to say about it. Shot some test strips bracketing up and down, but still nada.

Since I had just shot some kick a** bright gratings I knew the table and chemistry were OK. I put a Newport disc at the plateholder position and used it as a beam combiner, turning the set up into a Mach-Zehnder interferometer (rectangular layout as opposed to the + of the Michelson.)

The fringes were dancing around like crazy! The fibers weren't standing still for more than a couple of seconds!

So when Bill returned with the launcher he added sheaths to the fibers plus we clamped them to the table. This time it worked OK.

But we usually defaulted to using just one fiber in the Densiyuk mode. We even tried launching RGB lasers into three individual fibers and holding the ends in a common clamp, but even though the fibers are small, they do have finite dimension, so they were coming in a slightly different angles so there were colored edges to the shadows of objects.

Based on my experiences with fiber optics in holography I'd rather not. The spread needs to be controlled better; it comes out at an angle determined by the NA of the fiber, so to change the spread you need to put a lens after it, which makes another optic to keep clean, which is a waste since the light coming out of the fiber is as good as spatially filtered. Except you can't change the divergence as easily as changing an objective.

With polarization preserving fibers you need to be able to rotate the ends to align your vectors, not a big problem. And you have to work hard at alignment to keep your losses down.l But the worst part is the major battle with movement when there are more than one beam path because the fibers work by internal u]reflection[/u] which magnifies motion.

If anyone is interested, I can send you a pdf of Durrell's brochure by leaving a message in my mailbox on this forum. I believe that Graham does mention this device in some incarantion of his book.
