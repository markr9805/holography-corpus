wler Mon Apr 17, 2006 6:30 pm #2
The frequency doubling is a non-linear process and therefore its efficiency improves dramatically with power. Now, within the Yag resonator there is a much higher power density as compared to what can be coupled out. That's why a KTP is usually placed within the cavity ("intracavity doubling"). If you have quite a high output power an external KTP makes sense (eg for pulsed Q-switched lasers).

On the other hand, there do exist high efficiency doubler crystals that allow for external doubling even in the 100mW range. I guess that these setups are simply much more expensive, and/or the output efficiency is still low as compared to an intracavity configuration. One difficulty is to obtain a small enough focus point for achieving a high enough power density, and thus efficiency. This can be hard, given the typical poor beam profile of high power diodes.

wler Fri May 12, 2006 7:16 pm #4
> Hans: So how about if I would include the KTP crystal inside the cavity (between the diode and the grating)?

Well as a hobbyist I am not an expert on this, but this is what I think. First, the intra-cavity power of a diode laser with external resonator should be much less as for other lasers. Say, if a gas laser has 1% transmission through the OC, then the power within the resonator will be roughly hundred times the actual output. For laser diodes, as far as I know, due to the high gain the transmission is more like 30%, which does not give such a high internal power density. Also, for those ECDL the external grating is, in a sense, an extra resonator besides the one formed by the diode boundaries itself. Means that the power density between diode and grating is not the full intracavity density as it is within the diode, but smaller.

Then, practically speaking, there is the problem of getting the right KTP. Those crystals must be specifically tuned to the application (the orientation of the cut with respect to the crystal lattice must be optimized for phase matching, etc.) While those KTP's intended for Nd-Yag DPSS's are mass-produced and easily available, it may be next to impossible to obtain a suitable crystal for this proposed application with a red diode, and having it custom manufactured may well be more expensive than a nice complete 532nm DPPS laser system. So my guess is that using the classic configuration, ie, Nd Yag crystal plus a KTP, is still the easier way to go.

Also, coherence length is usually not the problem for laser diodes, as the intrinsic line width is small anyway, but rather mode stability. An extended cavity design may be beneficial but perhaps is overkill, as a line width in the sub-Mhz range is not important for holography (in other words, several m of coherence length are enough for our purposes, we don't need hundreds of meters).

> Hans: Why do some lasers use etalons and others use gratings for wavelength selection? Is there a good book out there to help understand the nuances and formula's that are out there about lasers?

Gratings are useful for having a wide tuning range, and are common for laser materials with a broad gain profile (like dye lasers). Also many tunable diode lasers use gratings in an extended cavity design. As far as I know, etalons are not so much used for broad band tuning because of the quite limited wavelength span, but rather for enforcing single mode operation.

Practically all what you ever may want to know is explained in Sam's laser FAQ, see specifically the section of distributed feedback lasers etc.
