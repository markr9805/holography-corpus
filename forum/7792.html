Ed Wesly Fri Mar 27, 2009 8:16 pm #3
Here’s that nasty old nay-sayer to fibers, and it’s because of what a pain in the you know what they were to work with. But you guys seem to be persistent and super enough workers to get results in spite of me, or actually in spite of what the fibers can do to get in the way of making things work!

So here’s my experience and thoughts again, gained working at Lake Forest, as TJ was somewhat evangelical about spreading the word of fibers.

There was always concern about the fibers being polarization preserving, and I vaguely remember something about d-shaped cores being the key. And their alignment to the polarization vector of the laser. But what it boils down to is launching into the fiber and checking with a polarizer if the output is still polarized. If it isn’t that great of polarization preserving, rotate the fiber at the input and see if that helps things. It might or might not.

There was discussion about what would be the right diameter of the core, and if it were the right one for the wavelength. You could predict that diameter by using the same equation Newport has in their catalog on predicting the correct size of pinhole, but in the real world, you of course test the fibers you get and measure how much you could get out of them. There was a length of fiber we got from our friends at Northwestern University who had a fiber optic drawing tower for experiments, and it was real good for single mode polarized output in the blue! Of course not commercially available.

The fibers I was playing with were like human hair, and once I took off my glasses and just laid them on a fiber I couldn’t see, and fractured it. One reason to not like them, although it sounds like you found some industrial strength ones.

Then of course there is the motion sickness with the fibers moving during exposure unless pretty well fixtured. This was a big problem when you tried to do anything with more than one fiber. But for beam delivery for single beam reflection and other single beam projects they are not so bad.

If you can tolerate the rather wide divergence coming out of them. It’s kind of like a built-in 60X microscope objective spread (which isn’t surprising, see below), which is kind of quick. But if you are lighting up a collimating mirror, or your setup requires a certain kind of distance from point source to plate or object, you might have to put a lens of some sort to control the spread. But one of the advantages of the light coming out of a fiber is its spatially filtered-like quality, and more optics means more cleanliness required.

So I have test driven two types of Newport couplers, the Durrell system (I have one for sale) and a screw on launcher from Oz Optics, who I don’t know if they are still in business. The Newport systems were plagued with the typical Newport-it is, meaning bad engineering, but you could still get a decent amount of light into the fiber. But there was some malevolent hysteresis in one of them, meaning the motion would be applied after you let go of the screw. And another one would drift out of alignment, so you would have to check before exposing. Some of it may have been due to warming of the fiber from a decent power laser, also, since you are usually using a 60X objective to focus the beam down to the 5 to 10 micron core.

The Oz Optics coupler was a real cute piece of equipment as it would screw onto the threads on the output of many He-Ne’s and had a GRIN rod to focus the light onto the fiber. But it had three tiny set screws that you tweaked with a tiny Allen wrench to wring the best alignment out of it. And the Oz fiber had a connector, (SMA is the acronym?), so you could leave the coupler on the laser and disconnect the fiber, or put other lengths on it.

And I think that Oz was the one that had the evanescent wave coupler/splitter that I mentioned in the thread about color holography. There was some mechanism that brought two naked to the core fibers close enough for light to jump from one to the other so you could control the Beam Balance Ratio between the two fibers. I don’t have a good mental image of what it looked like, but I remember it was fragile.

Getting decent coupling was always the problem. And it could be traced back to how good of a cleave you could get on the fiber end. And since these things are human hair sized with the jacket, you need to really examine it with a microscope. Polishing the ends wasn’t a bad idea, the problem was the chuck to hold the ends straight up and down. The best stuff to polish with was a 3-M sheet that looked like blue plastic, it was some sort of abrasive but you really couldn’t feel the texture since it was so fine.

So that’s most of what I know, form experience, which has pretty much soured me on them. The tediousness of coupling or launching, the vulnerability of motion, and the spread of light fixed by the Numerical Aperture of the core don’t outweigh the benefits for what I do. But give all this a try, and report back your success!

PS: I did have some experience with launching pulsed Ruby laser light into fibers when I worked at FermiLab, but that will have to wait for another day since I am roped into going to a meeting right about now! Needless to say, the project was doomed!

Anthion Fri May 01, 2009 5:46 pm #20
I've had some experience with single mode polarization maintaining fiber and a HeCd photo-resist exposure system. The manufacturer was Point Source.

Using 2 fibers and then attempting to interfere the output from them did not yield suitable fringe contrast. The path lengths were just too different and were constantly changing.

These fibers had a built in collimator on the end and injection optics on the other end (beginning?).

So I just used one fiber to deliver a single beam to the interferometer head. You end up losing about 70% of the beam power. Which, depending on your set up, might turn out to be either more or less efficient than your current set up.

Fiber diameter plays a large role in efficiency and it closely related to wavelength. Using a fiber designed for infra-red will not perform the same as one designed for visible.
