Joe Farina Tue Sep 08, 2009 5:16 pm #2
Hey Tony. Yes, this idea intrigues me. I have always wanted to do tests with ultrasonic treatment of DCG. There is a large amount of literature regarding ultrasonic treatment of silver halide, both in papers and in the patents. However, there is not a single reference, to my knowledge, to using it with DCG.

An ultrasonic cleaner provides a fairly weak effect. In the silver halide literature, they do not use cleaners, but rather sonicators. I haven't used one myself, so I can't describe them in detail, but there should be plenty of references to sonicators on the internet, and I'm sure they have them for sale on eBay.

In the patent literature I've seen, sonicators are used to disperse silver halide grains in gelatin, to make the solution very homogenous, and densly packed with uniform grains of silver halide. This improves the quality of the emulsion considerably.

However, there is an older literature, perhaps originating in the 1930's (?) where sonication is used in practically every phase of silver halide emulsion preparation (I think it was in Europe), and the author claimed an improvement in qualities with practially every phase of treatment. These results were disputed shortly thereafter, because the new group couldn't duplicate those results. But the idea was picked up again in the 1950's, and this group confirmed the benefits of ultrasonic treatment (though they only used it for one phase of emulsion preparation). Today, I think the benefits of sonication are pretty much acknowledged, based on the quantity of patents out there.

A couple things I would suggest:

First, I wouldn't limit my testing to just the final IPA bath. I would consider applying it to the liquid gelatin/dichromate in particular. And second, I would suggest trying a sonicator in addition to the cleaner. Considering the surpising results for silver halide, who knows what might happen with DCG.

Joe Farina Wed Sep 09, 2009 3:09 pm #12
It's my understanding that ultrasonic cleaners work by "cavitation," in other words, tiny micro-bubbles are forming and collapsing at very high frequency. This has some kind of scrubbing or cleaning action. Beyond that it's hard to say. I guess you might say that some kind of "violence" is being done to the object, but to know what is really happening, and why, might be quite difficult. (But they clean well, mine certainly does, and they will even damage the surface of objects if they are cleaned repeatedly or for too long.)

This is one of those cases where I would begin without any theories, preconceptions, or expectations. I would just try to do some careful tests and take a good look at the results. The theory can come later.

Joe Farina Wed Sep 09, 2009 8:17 pm #16
I see what you're saying John. Of course, we all have ideas, and there needs to be some kind of rationale in what we do. But in this particular case, the "unknowns" are pretty much overwhelming. When I say "expectations" I mean very specific items, say for example a more narrowband result. If I were doing the experiments myself, and I had no other goal than making a hologram narrowband, then I would be really disappointed if that didn't happen. So I might discontinue the experiments.

In other words, my "outlook" might be narrow as well, and I could easily overlook some other things which may actually be very useful if the results were developed further. In still other words, I would have a stong "bias" regarding narrowband results, and might not be able (due to my "mind block") to see a totally different benefit, like low noise, etc. I definitely consider myself imperfect in this respect, someone else might have a clearer picture of all the qualities or aspects that make up a holographic image. So, maybe I should have just said "be sure to keep an open mind, and look for accidental or side effects that you weren't expecting." I just think it's basic human nature to focus on one thing, while possibly ignoring other things that weren't expected.

The best analogy I can think of is archeology, which is an interest of mine. Usually, an excavator goes to a site with some definite expectations about what he wants to find. Once he starts digging, it often happens that very important discoveries are made, but in a completely different context or point in history than what he was expecting. If Tony decides to carry out those tests, I just hope he takes a good look at the many aspects of the resulting images. I don't think that's an easy task.
