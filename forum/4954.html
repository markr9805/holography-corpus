Holograms do not "project" in the sense you mean. A better way of thinking about it is that they kind of congeal, like jello moulds, but only within the limits of the plate.

A "projection" is the focusing of a flat image on a distant screen by means of lenses. So if you had a flat image of, say a cat, on some medium through which light could be shone - say, a slide or a transparency - then shining light through it onto a white wall will not show the cat on the wall, but just a vague smear of light and shadow. Every point on the slide produces a blown up circle of light on the wall. If however, you stuck the appropriate lens between the slide and the wall, every point on the slide will focus to a point on the wall and you'll see the image of a cat projected on the wall.

In holography, if you make a hologram of a cat, you expose a medium, usually referred to as a "plate", to light reflected off a solid model of a cat. This light hits the plate. If you actually look at the plate while the reflected light off the cat hits it, you'll simply see a smear of light. But that smear of light came from the cat and so has all the information about the optical properties of the cat. It's "cat light", if you will. This same "cat light" is also the light that's received by your eyes, is passed on to your brain and is processed by your brain. The brain recognises it as "cat light" because it's seen this kind of smear before and knows you call this particular smear of light "cat". So this "cat light" hits the medium, usually a piece of glass 4in by 5in and is captured uniquely. Now you go through a processs that fixes this "cat light" in the medium and you have therefore a hard record of the cat light. You can "read" the medium by shining a light through the piece of the glass - the plate - and while doing so, looking into the glass. The iinformation hard coded in the plate causes the light you're shining into the plate to congeal into the shape of your original model cat. So long as you look through the plate, you'll see a 3D picture of a cat, because your eyes see the trapped "cat light" in the plate, pass on the light to the brain and is processed exactly the same as the real thing. Your brain doesn't know it's looking at "saved cat light" (saved in the computer sense, not the religious sense!). However, once you look outside of the plate, the cat light is no longer being reproduced and you lose the image. So, a hologram can only be viewed within the dimensions of the plate.

Can you "walk around" a hologram? Well, you can if the "plate" happened to be a large cylindrical medium. This way, while you walk around the large cylinder and look into the cylinder, you're looking into the plate and still see whatever light is saved on the cylinder. Some holographers have made holograms on such media.

Can you convert a flat image on your computer into such a "walk around" type of hologram. Probably not. 3D data is more than just 2D data expanded. Without the 3D data set, you can't reproduce 3D. However, if you had a 3D graphics package, such as 3DMax, and made a 3D object, you could make it into a walk around 3D object. One way of doing this is done by Gregg Favalora, who posts on this forum, in a company called Actuality Systems ( [http://www.actuality-systems.com/index. ... to_gallery](http://www.actuality-systems.com/index.php/actuality/products/photo_gallery) )
