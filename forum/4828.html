It takes a while for measurement from the normal to soak in. The reason why the normal is the way to measure angles in optics is because most optical surfaces are curved, and how do you measure angles between a ray and a curved surface?

Most lens shapes are spherical. The radius of a sphere is at right angles to the circumference at that point. (If this doesn't seem correct, think of the spoke of a wheel, which is a radius, and there will be a time when the spoke is at right angles to the ground as the wheel revolves.) Extending the radius into the normal makes a convenient standard for measuring angles from curved surfaces. Since we holographers usually deal with flat plates, the measurement from the normal seems like one more goofy contortion we have to go through!

[quote:2c9de24f65="MichaelH"]Doesn't the brewsters angle change depending on the frequency of laser you're using?[/quote:2c9de24f65]

Welll, yes and no. The reason for Brewsters Angle is that the electrons in the surface of the material oscillate in the same direction as the polarisation of the light hitting the surface, since that's the direction of the electric field. These electrons then re-emit light in two directions - into the material (refraction) and away from the surface (reflection). However there is one particular polarisation and one particular angle of incidence such that the electrons are oscillating along the same direction as the reflected light wants to go. In this case, the electrons cannot transmit light in that direction and so there is no reflection. The "one particular polarisation" is p and the "one particular direction" is given by

tan B = n

where B is the direction, called Brewsters angle, and n is the index of the material. Commonly (but not always) glass has an index of 1.5, so, from the above equation

b = (inv tan)(1.5) = 56.31 degrees.

However, a couple of points:

The index of a material is wavelength dependant but the change in index betwen blue and red for glass is probably too small to show a large change in Brewsters angle. However, for the more pedantically inclined:

n for zinc crown glass at 434nm = 1.528 => Brewsters angle = (inv tan)(1.528) = 56.797degrees

n for zinc crown glass at 768nm = 1.511 => Brestwers angle = (inv tan)(1.511) = 56.503 degrees

p polarisation is defined by the plane of incidence. If the beam is "wonky" then the plane of incidence is no longer horizontal or vertiacl. In this case, even Brewster angle won't kill off the reflection. Again, unless the beam is a lot off, the effect is small (for display, anyway).

[quote:bed316c79d="Tony"]Do different materials have drasticly different Brewster angles? I have variable ratio beam splitter made of BK7, which seams to need a much lower angle to normal for minimum reflection...[/quote:bed316c79d]

Not too much. We see from [here](http://optics.mellesgriot.com/opguide/mp_3_1.htm) that for BK7:

index at 441 = 1.526 => B = (inv tan)(1.526) = 56.76

index at 514 = 1.520 => B = (inv tan)(1.520) = 56.66

index at 633 = 1.515 => B = (inv tan)(1.515) = 56.57

So it's still around 56 degrees with a 0.2 degree variation between blue and red. Opticians use very high index glasses with indices in the 1.7 and 1.8 range ( [http://www.revoptom.com/archive/FEATURES/ro0400f1.htm](http://www.revoptom.com/archive/FEATURES/ro0400f1.htm) ), but even here Brewsters angle is about 61 degrees. You can also see that the angle decreases as the index decreases. Therefore to get a lower minimum reflection angle, the material would have to have a lower index.

So I suspect the low angles for minimum reflection is not Brewster from BK7. Does the light come back up as you pass through the angle for minimum reflection. If so, then it seems there is another material over (or under) the beamsplitter. (you've checked there's no protective plastic coating, right?). Is it possible that the surface is wet? The index of water is 1.3-ish. If not, then it's possible that with a low angles, more light is being refracted and therefore less is being reflected. At low light levels the light seems dimmer than it actually is because the eye is a logarithmic detector. It detcts the log of the light level rather than the actual light level.
