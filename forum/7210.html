Ed Wesly Wed Mar 12, 2008 10:13 pm #1
The preferred form of a lens to spread a pulsed laser beam would be a negative meniscus type, with the convex side toward the incoming beam.

Certainly a positive lens is out of the question, as even a few 10's of milliJoules focused to a spot will break down the air. Ditto for concave mirrors.

A negative lens with its virtual focal point wouldn't necessarily break down the air. But if a double concave or plano concave lens were used, with the concave side toward the input beam, then the reflection (who can afford AR for pulsed?) from the concavity will focus, and break down the air again. The flat side of the plano-concave could reflect the beam right back into the output aperture and cause some serious damage.

With the concave side of a meniscus lens toward the beam the same thing could happen as above. Even though there would be reflections from the convex side of the meniscus, it spreads out, so the back reflection wouldn't be as dangerous as the above.

Convex mirrors could also work, but the usual aluminum coating would take it on the chin and blow off after a pulse or two. Holicon used to use convex mirrors that were coated with high energy coatings for 694nm, the only problem being that you would have to come into the mirror off-axis, which could introduce astigmatism in the beam if the angle were big
Ron Michael Thu Mar 13, 2008 12:23 am #2
Ed,

You made good points to consider. If I may add some additional considerations that I am sure you already know but others may not. I use plano concave lens for beam spreading and wedges for beam splitters in my setup for pulsed holography. I have used CVI laser for coated lens at 694. I orient the plano side toward the exiting beam and verify with a HeNe laser that the reflections land off axis. In fact the issue is the same that none of the amplifiers or any of the down-stream optics should reflect directly back up into the mopa beam axis. I like 2 degree tilts on my amplifier rods for example in addition to tilting the cavities.

What can get you on amplifiers is the negative lensing of the rod during q switch operation and this first surface of the rod as the beam enters will focus back and destroy previous stages unless you again tilt off axis the reflections to land harmless on cavity external parts instead of re-entering a previous stage.

I also like using an a good quality bk7 uncoated wedge window for the beam splitter since I can pick off just the first surface reflection for my reference beam and allow the second reflection to be used for energy measurement.
Colin Kaminski Thu Mar 13, 2008 1:53 am #3
This is a little off topic but how can I measure the back focal length (BFL) of a negative lens? Or the effective focal length if that is easier.
Ron Michael Thu Mar 13, 2008 2:57 am #4
Colin,

Use a collimated diode or hene laser beam and project the beam thru the lens and measure the radius of the beam at a distance.

In the near field you can use a geometric interpretation instead of a more accurate hyperbolic calculation that a guassian beam propagates.

So using the triangle rule you can derive the smaller focal length triangle from the larger measured triangle. Then you decide from the EFL and the lens thickness, the BFL.
Ron Michael Thu Mar 13, 2008 1:38 pm #5
okay, I will show a basic example if my memory is correct on this.

a collimated laser with a 2mm beam diameter hits a -100mm efl lens. The ratio of 1mm beam radius (height of the small triangle) and 100 mm base of the same triangle is 1/100. So at 1 meter you should have a beam radius of approximate 10 cm. Likewise the reverse is true. A measured projected diameter and a known beam diameter at a known distance from the lens will allow you to determine the basic fl of the lens.

Assumptions here. TEM00 mode of the laser and consistent method of measuring diameters. If you do this electronically with a photodiode you can better determine the diameter based on the spatial power of the original beam and the projected beam. But for our purpose you can roughly look at the spot size on the lens and the spot size on the wall and be close enough to figure out what lens you have.
