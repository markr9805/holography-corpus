Every time I use my positive flow bench I think about what I can do to make it better. First the working surface it too low. I need to raise it up such that I am not bent over when working in it. A simple fix. Next why have all that head space when all I do is just coat in it? I plan to reduce the work area's height by 2/3's so it is only about 1 foot tall. Thiw will allow a faster flow of air. Not so much to correct any problems with the design but DCG loves air flow when drying. Then I want to heat the air slightly. Just under the fan I want to put in a very small heater with temperature adjustment (I have not found what I want to use yet). I like the area just under the fan because I belive with the fan blowing on the heater and the fact that the air has to be forced through the two cylindrical heppa filters I should get a very good circulation and thus a even flow of warm air. Finally, I wish to put a work surface in the bench that allows temperature control of the table top (heating-cooling). It uses running water and can be switched from warm to cool in few seconds.

So in essence I want to convert my positive flow bench into a dedicated coating bench specifically made for DCG. I am loving the rod method in conjunction with taping 6 or 8 plates together so much that I want to move forward with that thought.

Joe Farina Wed Nov 08, 2006 3:06 pm #2
John, the idea of taping the edges of the glass before coating is an excellent idea. I will probably try to include this in my coating method.

The only thing I'm wondering about is coating multiple plates at the same time (as you do). It seems simple enough to make a single pass with a Mayer bar to coat multiple plates. But I was planning to do it a bit differently, and not cut the plates into smaller pieces beforehand. I have a tile cutter from Harbor Freight which is very efficient for cutting glass, and produces straight lines. (The tile cutter is held in a frame, and a carbide wheel is pressed against the glass. Then wheel is pulled across to provide a nice, straight cut.) It's really simple to use, and quite cheap, around 30 bucks.

I guess there's no reason why the plates can't be cut into smaller pieces beforehand (say 4 X 5's), taped along the edges, and then coated. It just seems like it might be a bit more awkward to handle.

Instead I was planning to coat a single large piece of glass, say 14 X 14, but with certain areas masked off by tape. Then put the plate into the tile-cutter and make the cuts later.

I would rather not criss-cross Scotch tape across the the surface, because this would create bumps where the tape overlaps, possibly causing problems with the Mayer bar. Instead, I was thinking about taking a large piece of adhesive film (the kind they use to line the bottoms of kitchen cabinets, etc.) and cutting out certain areas (to expose the glass) using an x-acto knife. I haven't tried this yet, so I don't know if it will work.

The tape-masking method has a lot of appeal, since it's no fun to scrape the gelatin away by hand.

JohnFP Wed Nov 08, 2006 3:52 pm #4
Joe, I too like the idea of coating multiple plates that are still attached. A while ago I came up with this technique. I pre-score the plates as shown below. Then after they harden I break them. I still use this technique when I buy fresh glass. I never cut my sheets up into smaller plates and tape. I always use this technique first with large sheets. ![Image](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/ScoredPlate.jpg)

But since I play so much I like to reuse the glass that I already have, thus the development of taping the individual pieces together. I can even use two whole plates at the top where the puddle is so as not to have any wastes or odd size cut glass. I just clean 8 pieces instead of 6.

![Image](http://www.holograms3d.com/Illustrations/CoatingTech.JPG)

I too thought that where the tape criss-crossed would be a problem and I never tried it. Then I decided to just give it a try anyway. I have to say I see no adverse effects of where the tape crisscrosses. I might believe that it is because were the tape criss-crosses and is a double layer, is where the edge of the glass and tape is down the line which only adds a miniscule amount of extra gelatin at the edge of the plate and mostly over the tape which is removed. In fact it is really strange but I notice that as gelatin sets it seems to be drawn to the center slightly. If I have a thick layer and the ambient temp is warm and humid, I notice the emulsion has actually thinned near the edge with this method. If the ambient temp is cool (about 68F) and the humidity low then I don't see it as much. I guess the emulsion is gelling faster, not sure.

Anyway I would love to be able to adjust the temp of my work surface and be able to adjust the temp of the air flow over the plates.

I like the idea of the adhesive film and have thought about this which is on similar lines. I have wanted to try to make a rectangular jig out of very thin but high tensile stainless steel so it was springy. It would have an inner square cut out about 4 1/2" by 6 1/2' for a 5x7 plate. The outer diameter would be about 6x8". The inner cut would taper down from the outer edge slightly such that when you placed the jig over the glass it would be held tight by tension. You could then coat the glass with a Myer bar. It sounds great in theory but I can imagine harder in practice. Let me know how the adhesive masking works. What I really like about your idea is that; let's say you wanted to do a final hologram that looked like a disk. Then you could mask out a disk onto a plate with your adhesive "stuff". Coat, dry and peel. Then expose and process the square plate, the square plate being much more user friendly on the table. Then after processing seal another square or disk (slightly large then the emulsion disk) cover glass to the hologram. Then grind to a disk shape leaving that still all precious boarder of no emulsion around the disk of gelatin.

Yes, it is very nice to have the already cleaned edge.
