It's not a hologram in any sense that anyone here would think of as a hologram. There's no "refrence beam" or "object beam" or "beam ratio". The word "hologram" is used as a metaphor for information, or, more accurately, as a metaphhor for entropy. This follows from the work of Hawking through to Leonard Susskind. Basically, the information content of a 5 dimensional "sphere" can be derived from the "surface area" of the 5-dimensional "sphere", giving us the laws of physics in our 4-dimensional surface to the 5-D sphere. The analogy - or metaphor, if you prefer - comes from the "fact" that all the information of a hologram is spread over every part of the hologram, ie if you break a hologram then every piece contains the entire image. Not so. It's a hangover from an earlier era.

The test itself has been derided by most physicists. I believe Susskind has called it "silly".
Ed Wesly Wed Apr 18, 2012 4:47 pm #4
Having worked at FermiLab, home of the HEPcats, (High Energy Physics, although when I first started there and saw posters for the HEP meetings I thought that was where all the jazz aficionados hung out) I am always wary of stuff like this, in fact for the most part I don’t care.

The problem comes from the hubris that comes from these characters, especially including someone on this forum. I worked as Hans Bjelkhagen’s assistant as a holographer for Fermi’s 15 foot bubble chamber, and we were using the world’s largest pulsed ruby laser designed for holography, a JK/Lumonics 4 rod, conservatively rated as a 25 Joule unit. (It’s for sale at Anderson Laser if anyone is interested; it cost $135k of our tax dollars in 1986.)

But the holograms weren’t coming out, and the blame was flying all over the place. At the meetings we constantly heard, “but our calculations show that…” Even if you have equations, there are variables and coefficients, and if you plug in the wrong values, especially optimistic, best-case scenario ones, reality has a habit of flipping you the bird.

The major issue at the bubble chamber was motion, the #1 killer of holograms. The holographic set up was a Gabor type scheme: a diverging laser beam entered the bottom of the fifteen sphere filled with liquid hydrogen, and travelled diametrically to the holographic film on the top of the BC. Light going directly to the Agfa 10E75 film was the reference beam, and light scattered off the bubbles formed by the neutrinos colliding with the hydrogen were the objects.

Bubble chambers become sensitive to incoming radiation by increasing their volume, lowering the pressure. This promotes boiling of the liquid, and the bubbles formed are tracks that show where the sub-atomic particles went. In the case of the 15’ at Fermi, this was accomplished by dropping a circular plate at the bottom of the sphere with a hydraulic piston slightly before the neutrino beam arrived at the chamber.

The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle, which states that you cannot observe something without changing it, reared its ugly head in the recording of the holograms of the particle tracks. The chamber was sensitive to any input of energy, and since about 4 to 5 Joules of 6943 Angstrom radiation was passing through it, a beautiful cone of bubbles was formed when the Q-Switched pulse recorded the hologram.

The holograms were OK, in spite of the boiling, which occurred after the exposure. However, this cone of boiling hydrogen obscured the tracks that were being photographed slightly after the holographic exposure.

Photography had been used from day one to record the images of bubble chamber tracks, the little curlicues and split ends, and analyzed for their size and shape and direction to match up with paths predicted for the quarks, bosons, muons, etc., all good names for aliens or band. But the one they were looking for would theoretically make such a tiny track since it was so short-lived, and the bubbles would be so tiny, that they might be smaller than the resolution of the conventional cameras.

So the physicists calculated that a 70 mm diameter hologram would have better resolution than the conventional camera photographs, which is why they wanted to use holograms to record the tracks, but the photos were necessary as a guide to begin to know where to look in the hologram. But since the laser beam boiled the liquid before the photos were taken, obscuring the tracks, something had to be done.

The first step was to cut down exposure, but we were using the fastest holo film available, the much mal-aligned Agfa 10E75, and developing the piss out of it for 6 minutes in D-19 doped up with a couple of grams of phenidone, and not bleaching it, so we were at the practical limit of holographic recording there.

Since exposure is a function of time and intensity, and the boiling would occur above a critical peak power, it was calculated that if the exposure were lengthened and a lower power were delivered, the holographic film would get its proper energy dose without boiling the chamber and ruining the photographs.

The first obstacle to overcome was stretching the pulse of the ruby laser. This gets rather insanely technical, but thanks to a couple of Electronic Engineers, (one of whom patented his pulse stretcher, and the circuit is used by Innolas, the successor to JK lasers), the pulse was stretched from the typical 20 nanoseconds of the stock Q-switch to 10’s of microseconds, a factor of 1000! So there was no more boiling!

But then there were no holograms! As short of a length of time 10 usec seems to be, the bubble chamber itself was moving significantly during that exposure! And wiping out the fringe contrast!

As the above-mentioned hydraulically activated bottom plate dropped, the 15’ stainless steel sphere with 50 kiloLiters of liquid hydrogen in it moved and the building shook! You could always tell when the physics run was going on by the commotion, 3 pings per minute as they called them.

There was a wire stretched across the bubble chamber as a marker, and it cast a shadow in the reference beam. If film was exposed with a Q-switched pulse, or if the chamber were still, a textbook perfect diffraction shadow of the wire would develop, so good that you could see a bright fringe in the middle of the geometric shadow! And you could see fringes getting smaller than what the eye could see, and follow them down with a magnifying glass.

But the stretched pulse in an active chamber yielded a rather fuzzy diffraction shadow, and sometimes none at all. I’d shove these low contrast images in the face of the “our calculations show that shouldn’t happen” crowd, who obviously must not have been plugging in the right numbers in their calculations, and they would make it seem that I was personally responsible for the bad holograms. The pinheads wouldn’t realize you don’t put reference beams through turbulent liquids.

Holy cow, I’ve been typing for over an hour! Another case of logorrhea! (It’s like diarrhea, except with words!) So anyhow, to answer your question, Simon, I need to quote the Sex Pistols Pretty Vacant, “Don’t ask us what we think, ‘cause we’re not all there, I don’t pretend, cause I don’t care!” If their calculations prove it to them, that’s just fine and dandy! Same thing for all this nonsense about the universe being a hologram! And Tupac being a hologram!
