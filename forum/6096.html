JohnFP Tue Mar 06, 2007 1:09 pm #3
Well if you were in microgravity, you would probably need some type of special holographic table.

You would need a table that:

I1. s anchored somehow so that when you placed a object or moved a mount the table would not move in the opposite direction forever.

2. Would need vibration isolation from this anchoring system.

3. Would need to be heated such that the laser light does not cause gross thermal expansion of optics and object.

4. Has the capability of allowing clamping of the objects and mounts.

5. Most importantly, and I don't even know how to address this, it would have to be able to allow dampening of the natural resonance of all the parts of the table and optics. I think were are actually lucky here on earth with gravity as the component resonance is dampened somewhat by gravity. In space I would imaging the natural resonance could be measured with a michaelson and thusly cause problems for holography. Now if you don't heat and allow all to come close to absolute 0 then see issue 3.

Any other thoughts?
javamiggs Tue Mar 06, 2007 5:33 pm #4
If you are in outer space (outside your space vehicle I assume), I believe you wouldnt need an isolation table provided you would not anchor them in your space vehicle since it may somehow vibrate a little. In outer space, your space vehicle, your self, and all your optical components are basically traveling with the same speed relative to Earth. Relatively speaking, if you float your optical components in space, they are at rest with respect to each other. The only problem is how well can you position all your components to make them relatively "at rest".

Even if you succeed in making them relatively at rest, gravitational pull of Sun, Moon, or any other space objects will scatter and float them apart after sometime. Even when you switch your laser on, the light could possibly "kick" your mirrors apart. Maybe you should need to screw all your optical components to some kind of flat plate.

I also dont think the optical components will heat up. It's very very very cold up there.

I'm still looking for my holosphere copy with it, but I did find the original article in the classic SPIE 215, Recent Advances in Holography, (1980). Holography on Space Shuttle, Ralph Weurker, et al., TRW Defense and Space Systems, that Ed Bush must have cribbed from.

In the abstract they mention "A holographic recording system will be flown on the NASA Space Shuttle (SL-3 mission) to record fluid phenomena under low-zero gravity conditions..." I leave it up to other forum members with more time on thier hands than me to find out if it really did go up. But if anyone would like a pdf of it, please PM me via this forum with the address you would like it to be sent to..

They used the S-P 107A OEM He-Ne for photons, and Kodak SO-253 High Speed Holographic Film for the medium. Some bon mots from the article: "Recording holograms in space introduces challenges quite unfamiliar to the terrestrial holographer. The recording system must survive launch. It will be operable by (non-holographic) flight personnel. It must not be hazardous. It must be reliable. It must not perturb the growing crystal (<10-5g forces)..."
