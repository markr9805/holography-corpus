zipsister Mon Mar 10, 2008 3:31 am #1
I've often read about set-up stability problems that beginner and even experienced holographers have which eventually, more times than not, are linked to thermal currents. The reality is this; lasers and diode lasers generate a lot of heat which floats about the table like leacherous bosses at a Xmas breakup. This heat has lots of minute effects: film creep, metal expansion/contraction, adhesive tape slide, curtains breathe in and out creating more turbulance and lastly the emitted hot air moves continuously towards cold air. The accepted solution is to enclose the optical train/table with a box of sorts (I use a discarded cardboard box which fitted perfectly) and isolate the laser, and probably a directing beam mirror, outside away from the rest of the setup. I have a small Newport table and have bolted some 5mm thick aluminium flat ramps for the laser and mirror to sit on which allowed me to enclose the table completely while still isolating the laser. Hope this is of some help to those whose holograms seem to lack punch.

JohnFP Tue Jun 10, 2008 1:19 pm #7
My laser has always been to big to put on the table. Table space is a premium. So I have always thought that mounting the laser off the table has benefits that greatly outweigh the negatives.

The benifits are, conservation of valuable table space, no vibrations or air currents due to laser, easier to keep light leakage from laser from hitting plate (my laser has lots of vents that shoot light to walls and ceiling) and no mechanical shutter vibrations as it too is off table.

The negatives are possible allignment problems as stated but here are my comments on that. I have the laser mounted on a firm base with the shutter then a transfer mirror. The key is the transfer mirror. I have never seen an alignment problem in 8 - 10 hours. But I have seen it occasionally when I come back in days. With that, 99.9% of the time, a very small tweak of the transfer mirror off the table and just in front of the laser after the shutter easily and quickly corrects the problem. I have done this with three spatial filter and it is surpisingly easy to find the optimal of all three again this way, so long as they have not moved relative to one another (ouch that would be bad, bad, bad for making long exposures anyway). It's even easier then alligning a spatial filter, surprisingly easy.
