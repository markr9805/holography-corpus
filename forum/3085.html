The simplest way to achieve a color shift towards the blue might be to dissolve parts of the silver halide/metal silver subsequent to development, that's to say, fixing or reversal bleaching. Reversal bleaching is probably the better solution. The upside of the whole process is you'll need less light exposure (the downside being a slightly reduced image brightness). potassium permanganate.0,5g

sodium hydrogen sulfate..10g

water..................................1L

Use a 50g/L ascorbic acid solution to get rid of the pink stain.

Ed Wesly Wed Feb 02, 2005 2:02 pm #8
All bleaches for silver based photo processes need an oxidizing agent, whether it's for B & W photo toning, reducers, color photo processing, B & W reversal processing, etc. These oxidizers attack the developed elemental silver so that they are ready to accept bonding with the bromine in the holo bleach solution.

Originally the holographic bleaches used the typical metal-based oxidizers common to photo processes: potassium ferricyanide, copper sulfate, potassium dichromate, mercuric chloride, and ferric nitrate for example. Then Nick Phillips introduced p-benzoquinone, aka quinone, or more commonly, PBQ, an organic compound as the oxidizer.

After the developing agent hydroquinone does its job developing silver halide crystals, its depleted or wasted state is PBQ. It's quite poetic chemistry that a developing agent in an alkali bath reduces silver halides to silver, while its by-product in an acid bath can oxidize the silver so that it can change back to silver halide!

Using this philospohy Hans and Nick decided to formulate a series of bleach baths that would change developing agents into oxidizing agents as an academic exercise! Thus were born the PBU series of bleaches, Phillips-Bjelkhagen Ultimate!

Common to all of them is the use of potassium persulfate to oxidize the developer into its bleach form. Hydroquinone oxidizes to p-benzoquinone, but who knows the name of the changed Amidol, Ascorbic Acid, Metol, etc.? The rest of the PBU formula includes an acid to drive the reaction and of course a source of bromine, potassium bromide.

Because some shrinkage is usually experienced with the PBU bleaches it shows that the gelatin is collapsing due to damage or some material is dissolving out of the emulsion, although the colors can be quite nice. The CWPBQ2 bleach keeps the fringe spacing pretty much in its original dimensions, probably because the PBQ tans the gelatin as it bleaches, so that it retains its structural rigidity.

So the role of Amidol in this bleach is to first get converted from its reducing form to its oxidizing form, (name unknown at this time) where it attacks the developed silver grains and lets the bromine combine with it to become a rehalogenated silver halide. (Please excuse the redundancy.)

Development is the first stage anyway, so whether you rinse, fix or bleach is moot. You can always see if there is a hologram just after the development stage. I frequently do this to judge the proper OD for development. You can even see an achromatic image while developing a reflection image. Note: while, the image will come up almost instantly and get brighter, provided the developer is properly made up. As for bleaching the entire plate and re-shooting using the fact that PBQ is a by-product of hydroquinne, not quite. First, the PBQ (or any bleach) needs an acidic environment (pH <7) while a developer needs an alkiline one (pH>7). Just re-dunking the plate back into the hydroquinone developer will not bleach it. Secondly, PBQ is a rehalogenating bleach which can be used with a non-fixing development scheme. Even under ordinary developing conditions the silver halide is still there (since you haven't fixed it) but the exposed grains have been reduced to silver, however the PBQ simply re-converts the reduced silver back to silver halide. You apparently now have a plate with nothing but silver halide, just as you had before you ever exposed. But this time you have an image. This seems counter-intuitive. We (Tom B, Martin, Sergio, Jeff Blythe and myself, I think) talked a lot about this on the old forum. So if you have a plate developed in a hydroquinone developer and see no hologram, there may be fringes present but just not bright enough. Certainly the very act of exposure has altered the grain structure. If you put it in a rehalogenating bleach, you wont take it back to it's "virgin" state, you'll simply re-expose a second set of fringes on it, or re-create a new set of exposed grains. However, I'm working on a scheme to rehalogenate an exposed plate back to it's "virgin" state (with a little help from forum members).
