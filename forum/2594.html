VRP is capable of incredible brightness, the trick is in the ratio and in taking it dark. As Bruce says, be careful of self-referencing. Also be careful to baffle well, at this level of light any stray light will be strong enough to create visible effects.

The transition from Agfa was a problem for me. In the beginning I used the pyro process on Agfa and got pretty bright holograms. When I transferred to VRP, I was gtting almost nothing. However, with a little help from Jeff Murray I changed my processing to CWC2/PBQ and realised how sensitive the stuff is to ratio. There's almost an exponential peak at the right ratio which falls off as you go off the ideal. It also seems true that the ideal is a touch more object than reference. Be careful of going too high, you'll end up with noise. There's a trade-off between brightness and noise which is pretty delicate at these levels.

A travelling band is ALWAYS a sign of motion, unless your laser is unstable and changing.

That's interferometry, the various parts of your egg and the various lengths of your object beam have a chronological relationship, something to be discouraged in CW imaging.

I would guess that your egg was falling over backwards, if nothing else in the scene has this brightness quirk. If everything has the bands, it's the plate that's falling.

Dinesh Wed Jul 14, 2004 1:17 pm #16
The travelling light band is a band of light, not a dark band. The egg looks overall a little dim, but as you twist the plate to and fro, where the egg throws the greatest amount of light due to it's curvature is where there is a bright band. Brighter than the rest of the egg, that is. I suspected that at these places, the ratio was greater than the rest of the egg since more object light was thrown out in this direction. Just like hitting a real egg with light, as you view it from different angles from top to bottom, using the same source, it shows a band of reflection highlights.

[quote:f43885fcbb]but how is it that a wavefront can be planar after reflecting from an ovoid surface? [/quote:f43885fcbb]

It can't. Well, it can't within a certain distance. But reflection from a surface depends on the angle of the plane tangential to the surface. For an egg, the tangent planes twist as you go up the egg, the twist rate depending on the rate of change of the radius of the surface. From my web site: (The author of the notes has been lost by my poor record keeping )

[http://www.designerinlight.com/holo/chem.htm](http://www.designerinlight.com/holo/chem.htm)

CWC2 - two-part DEVELOPER

PART A solution

500 ml. warmed distilled water.

(Pyro)Catechol 10 grams

L-Ascorbic Acid (Vitamin C) 5 grams

Sodium Sulfite (anhydrous) 5 grams

Urea 30 grams

PART B solution

500 ml. warmed distilled water.

Sodium Carbonate 30 grams

________________________________________________

Part A is good for one month, Part B indefinitely.

Add equal parts A & B to activate just a minute or two before use, just enough to cover one hologram. Mixed solution is active for 20 minutes. Discard after one use to assure each hologram has optimum development.

Develop time:

at least TWO minutes @ 68 degrees F. with constant agitation (AGFA).

FIVE minutes for low power lasers, for HRT plates and PFG-03M plates.

Rinse in distilled water.

View a green safelight through rinsed plate to judge density - some variation is OK.

Adjust exposure/developing time to achieve a final developed density of:

D 1.5 - 2 - medium gray (for an unbleached transmission hologram)

D 2 - 3.5 - very dark (reflection holograms).

D 4 - appears mostly opaque (good for HRT reflection holograms).

Do not use fixer if it will be bleached (reflection holograms are usually bleached).

Notes on developed density - this stage is where you figure if exposure, ratio, gleam spots, beam centering, even illumination, and overall light levels and their recorded patterns are OK for the next shot as well, or need adjustment. After the plate is bleached clear, these clues are gone. Although dark, wet, and hard to see, observation of different gray levels is important, hopefully understanding what caused each visible pattern. A good safelight is important. My favorite is a commercially available four-foot fluorescent fixture with plastic tube filters.

I use 50 g/l but only because Hans's book says so. Since it works well for me with VRP, I saw no reason to change.

[quote:db2f598de9]Mixed solution is active for 20 minutes. Discard after one use to assure each hologram has optimum development. [/quote:db2f598de9]

While true, I've actually used it all day and into the next, passing about 6 or 7 small pieces of film (which use to test for exposure/dev) and 4 to 6 4x5 plates. When carrying it over to the next day, it's important that the developer be as isolated from air as possible, since absorption by air will raise the pH. I cover the processing tray with a sheet of glass and then place the bleach tray, with bleach in it, on top. I also cover the bleach tray with a sheet of glass. I've never noticed any real degradation with VRP. There is a another method where you "float" a processing tray over the developer tray so that no air gets into the developer. I've never tried it, so no personal experiences there.

[quote:db2f598de9]at least TWO minutes @ 68 degrees F. with constant agitation [/quote:db2f598de9]

I generally process very, very dark at about 75 F which takes about 2 to 4 minutes with VRP. There's a fine l;ine between bright and noisy so I don't take it too dark. I believe the noise actually comes from the excess bleaching from over-darkening. The bleach causes lthe particle sizes within the emulsion to grow and so forms scattering centers. Whether or not it's Rayleigh, for me, is an open question. It's the traditional wisdom, but I can't help noticing the Rayleigh svcattering is very blue-predominant (Rayleigh scattering is proportional to the 4th power of frequency - the higher the frequency the -much! - more the scattering). yet, scattering seems uniform over the entire spectrum
