I assume that the fluorescent material is butted right up against the plate and only on one side of it. In this case, you need to follow the wavefront as it starts off as a reconstruction wavefront and passes through the plate becoming an object wavefront on exit. This object wavefront is composed of phase factors that define it as a specific object wavefront and is basically the Fraunhofer pattern. However, when the wavefront hits the back of the hologram (the exit side) , it's a Fresnel diffraction pattern. If this Fresnel pattern causes fluorescent scattering within the fluorescent material, then a certain proportion will be fluorescently scattered and a certain proporrtion will not and will pass through the "scattering screen" and form the image. Thus, depending on the actual flourescent material and it's density, there will be a glow characterised by Fresnel diffraction and a loss in brightness of the image. If viewed, the hologram will appear to have a halo behind the image which may, or may not, be uniform depending on the exact object wavefront and the density of fluorescent matter. The proportion of "halo" to "image" will obviously depend on the density of fluorescent matter and the shape of the wavefront.

[quote:2a4ba595f4="JohnFP"]Is there a material that we can add to the emulsion such that the fringes bend the light, this light hits the flourescent material but still preserves the wave front thus enabling it to be viewed with a black light?[/quote:2a4ba595f4]

I never needed any special material. I did however need to alter the density according to Kramers Kronig. Basically, you need to look at the dispersion equation at the area of interest.

Silver halide crystals embedded in the emulsion are pretty effectively masked from the UV, and specially sensitized UV films have to have the halides more or less sticking out of the front of the emulsion, which also makes it very delicate.
