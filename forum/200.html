Dinesh Fri Apr 08, 2011 11:41 am #4
> PinkysBrain: I'm guessing they mean it shows a colour hologram even with diffuse lighting?

You're guessing right. There are a number of basic errors, which is usually the case when reporters try to write up this sort of thing. For one thing the electrons cannot be unbound. Unbound electrons don't oscillate simply because there's no restoring force. The electrons would accelerate to under the influence of the surrounding E field, gradually lose energy and come to a halt. This plasmon effect is a quantisation effect where the k vector of the E field parallel to the surface of the metal-dielectric interface couples to the k vector of the light field - by the way, photons don't have E fields and so cannot have a k vector. This will result in quantised energy levels. I don't suppose anyone wants to know this, but it still fascinates me that people are fascinated with "physics", but don't have the wherewithall or patience to actually understand the physics behind the "physics". (I suppose at this point I should put that yellow face showing someone trying to impress their dentist...)

What they have here is an idealised Raman Nath diffraction process, ie almost pure surface diffraction, upon which they appear to have recorded a Rainbow. There can be no colour recording on a Raman Nath process because there can be no Bragg selectivity of specific lambda components. So, it's not a "colour" hologram in the conventional sense of an rgb recording or a pseudo-colour swelling technique. Therefore, it must change colour as you move your head across the slits, but will not change colour if you move parallel to the slits - a simple effect of Rainbow holograms. However, as Pinky says, it will diffract under diffuse lighting because of the fact that Raman Nath diffraction is not Bragg selective. It'll diffract any light from any direction. It won't show the image in the best light (sorry!) because light coming in from the wrong direction will simply show a fuzzy distorted image. Dot matrix holograms take advantage of this effect, by the way.

Nasser's comment is interesting because he has stated that he has a "real time" Princess Leia-type hologram and wants to discourage others from claiming the same. The fact that his hologram is neither "real time" nor a "Princess-Leia-type" is neither here nor there.

Mike Bove was probably being diplomatic. This technique will not be able to "mass produce holograms cheaply". However, I like the reporter's assertion that MIT had "debuted an updatable 3-D holographic video earlier this year." I saw a demo of this around 89 or so.

"For now, then, plasmonic holograms don’t look as if they’ll change the world " - Nope! (But, will any hologram create World Peace, stop Global Warming and keep the price of beer down?)

"the physics behind this approach is very interesting," - yep!

Dinesh Sat Apr 09, 2011 8:29 am #11
It's not "edge lit", in the conventional sense. The "edge" is less than lambda ("The entire assemblage was 230 nanometers thick.") and you cannot launch an edge lit reconstruction beam when the edge itself is less than the wavelength of light! It's a bit of a metaphor to connect an edge lit hologram, one in which the light is launched via a thick piece of glass, to a surface plasmon, where quantum coupling is the major factor in the light propagation. You certainly can't make a volume hologram in a structure that's less than half lambda!You'd have zero Bragg planes. Consider also that a single molecule of photoresist is probably on the order of 10's of microns, so you're dealing with, perhaps, 10 molecules or so. There are no volume effecs and certainly no rgb effects. The fact that the researcher uses three lasers does not mean that it's a "full colour" rgb hologram. You can get full colour in a Rainbow (although you probably need to understand the holography to know how to place the slits). We used to make full colour Rainbows all the time at Banknote and, in fact, we (Joy and I) made a full colour Rainbow for one of the PCCG contests; the one with Calvin and Hobbes.

The three lasers is probably that he chose his energy levels in the plasmon propagation to correspond to three wavelengths and the reporter assumed that it was "rgb", though of course, we don't know know which r, which g and which b.

By the way, we've just made an edge lit on dcg. I don't know of anyone else who's reported doing that.

Well, yes and no. You'll notice from the papers Martin has put up (thanks, Martin) that they still used a base plate to launch the light. It's not as large as the prism Colin talks of (and like the one I used back in the 80's when I did it at POC). I never liked using a base plate because it involves index matching, which I hate using! The stuff's messy, gooey and gets everywhere!

Anyway, yes it's easier since you control the emulsion. However, it's harder because it presents ratio and measurement problems that you don't have with non-edge lit holograms. All the light is ideally inside the glass and so difficult to measure. I suppose you could measure the light before it entered the glass edge. I tried that but my Newport meter has a 1 cm square detector and the entry beam was only a few millimeters wide, so again it was difficult to work out how much light the emulsion itself was seeing. I used an H1 for my object and getting a good ratio was also a problem for the same reason.
