Larry wrote:

[quote]Sorry Ed, I've only been involved in this stuff for 2.5 yrs and I'm not as up to speed on this stuff as you, but what reason do you have (other than an obvious bias against Dinesh) for saying we should ignore anything he has done with this fuji film? quote]

The reason why I would have to say that I would ignore the results that Dinesh got is that he first off took a wrong approach to making a transmission H1 to H2 copy. Possibly the reason he got off on the wrong track is lack of experience with silver halide materials and reliance on equations that a lot of time don't hold up to the real world.

When I worked at Fermilab with Hans Bjelkhagen, we would constantly hear "but our calculations show that..." and the resultant holograms were either non-existent or dim. The problem with plugging numbers into higher order equations is that there are parameters like coefficients that have to have values that need to come from somewhere, like real life experience.

Plugging and chugging into stuff like Raman-Nath equations rely on a certain set of conditions. When dealing with silver halide holography materials, when they record fringes, they are not represented as solid shapes or blocks but something more on the statistical side. Find some pictures of fringes in a paper, (I am at work administering a test so I can write but don't have access to my library to cite papers) and what passes for a recorded interference pattern looks more like a bunch of dots laid down by a Sharpie. So right off the bat, you have a new parameter to fit into the curve; the lack of completeness to the fringes. How this fits into the Raman-Nath equations I don't know, but unless this quantity is available how can you come up with the right or even sensible answer?

What I find hilarious about the Forumwrecker's (my hat's off to whoever coined that term!) approach to the duo-color H1 is that he has both of the colors' reference beams coming in at approximately the same angle and expects one to not reconstruct the other. Maybe this might work with some thick material, like a 50 micron DCG or polymer, but my experience (heavily AgX, lots of resist, DCG and both duPont and Polaroid photopolymers) shows that there is always dispersion, usually of the complete spectrum if the geometry allows. The recorded wavelength is of course the brightest, but never does any other color get completely extinguished.

If the layer in a transmission hologram is so thick that its structure can only support one color out of a broadband replay source, it also becomes extremely critical of reconstruction source angle and distance. The only sample I ever saw of something like that was an early '70's duPont polymer laser transmission hologram that TJ had made at Lake Forest. The goo had been poured inbetween two glass plates separated by a piece of masking tape, approximately 100 microns I guess. We had to

light it with a white light a long distance away, and when done right, there was a monochromatic reconstruction, although a narrow viewing angle. But just bump one of the optics and it disappeared! This is an extreme case.

Going back to the ancient ages of laser transmission holography (and even in a patent filed by Leith and Upatnieks) it was always believed that the reference beams should have wide angular separations because of the crosstalk. If you want to talk equations, what are the gospel are handed down by one of the gods of Mt. Holympus itself, Dr. Stephen Benton, in the link above. Some might argue that thick or volume grating effects are not taken into account, but we hold these truths to be self-evident, as witness all the white light transmission display holograms whose existence hinges on them. And what they show is that when a grating or hologram is made with one wavelength, all the other lambdas pop out somewhere.

So maybe Dinesh is being chauvinistic by always referring to his fellow countryman, Raman, but somehow or other these equations are failing him with the real world of silver halide materials. I have always used the Benton equations for setting up rainbow hologram set ups or transmission HOE's since they deliver from a practical standpoint.

Whenever you reconstruct a classical laser transmission hologram's virtual image with a white light source, you get this blurry spectral smear. Studying it closely, you can witness the Benton math at work, with a smaller blue image closer to the plate and a red one further away. If the object is somewhat planar, you can see the colors pointing toward the replay source, the so-called alpha or tip angle. Tilting the holo plate moves the colors around, until finally they are extinguished, but that would be a more severe tilt than the 5 degrees or so he described. With observations like this, I find it hard to believe that anyone would start off with such a cockamamie (spelling?) set up.

So if I'm a mindreader, your next question would be what would you do, Mr. Smarty-Pants? Let's look at the options.

#1 Transmission H1 to Reflection H2:

From the experience of making registered color rainbow holograms (but with a single color laser, a pseudo-color approach), the concept of using a single plate for the separations is the most appealing, as juggling three masters is nothing but headaches. But in the case of this type of rainbow holo, the RGB slits are spatially separated, so a single reference beam can cover the whole plate, with the plate partitioned a la Benton as the different color separations are exposed.

But the project we are discussing is not about working in a pseudo-color regime here; we have a real colored object and the three laser wavelengths will do the RGB separating for us. So if we have only one collimating optic for the reference beam, we must put the three H1's on separate plates and hope our plateholder is kinematic enough to allow the shuffling of the 3 H1's for an accurate reconstruction.

Now if we had everything we wanted, like 3 collimators, (hey, we're dreaming, we got lots of lasers!) then we can have three discrete angularly separated reference beams. But this makes life easy for the recording of the H1, but then it boggles my mind as to how to rearrange all these optics to flush out the conjugate real image for the H2.

#2 Reflection H1: I started this thread a while ago to see if anyone had tried it, and since then JohnFP had performed the experiment. It was in DCG, but I don't think that makes it irrelevant. If I had easy access to two or more different color lasers I would try it in silver. The idea is to use similar object to holoplate distances as used in recording a transmision H1, but just bring the reference in from the other side like in a reflection hologram. Basically it would be a very deep multiple beam reflection hologram, that when reconstructed with white light would look pretty fuzzy since whatever dispersion you get in the reflection mode is increased by distance from the holoplate. But with the monochromatic reconstruction of the laser, along with a good conjugate shaped replay beam, the image should look as perfect as the mold that made the object.

The problems that would arise, as noted in the earlier post, are the decrease in efficiency with the multi-wavelength recording, and the criticality of exact fringe recording, i.e. no shrinkage. But that would take care of the knotty registration issue.

But on a more practical level, basic Single Beam Reflection true color holography, what I would do and have done to conquer the beast is to make exposure and development test of my standard object at all the lambdas concerned. For my technique, see my write up of CWC2 posted on this forum.

I had used this technique to test Ilford blue and green, Agfa 8E56HD, 8E56HD AHI, 8E75HD, Kodka Type 120, Bulgarian Academy of Sciences HP-650 and 490 plates, (how about that for something obscure!) a variety of duPont photopolymers, DCG plates to characterize them. This was when I worked at LFC under a grant from a major medical instrument maker that was interested in using HOE's instead of conventional optics in their diagnostic devices. I could get "black holes" in thE zero order with most of these materials if I stroked them right. I also did a "Holographer's Consumer Report" poster paper for the 2000 ISDH, available upon request by contacting me via this forum.

So if I got a sample of this Fuji stuff, I would pick a standard development time, say 3'. I would set up the film on my Krylon #1401 Bright Silver (the one with aluminum flakes for the pigment to preserve polarization)sprayed waffle iron and put 4 different exposures on one plate, in a geometric sequence: 1, 2, 4, 8" for instance. I would find what is the threshold, where I get just a hint of diffraction, and saturation, where the image goes to milky oblivion. There would be an optimum as far as brightness versus signal to noise for each laser.

Then I would start exposing with a base of 1/2 or 1/3 of the best blue exposure, and add a similar fractional amount of the green and the red. See what the color looks like, and undoubtedly have to play until I get something that works.

So it amazes me that anyone could think that their first shot in a two color set up would work at all, that's why I dismiss the work of Dinesh, although he did play a bit with the exposure tweak. But he still hasn't posted any further results.

Ed Wesly Wed Feb 15, 2006 5:57 pm #34
Logarithmic numbers are useful because they save space. They are used in photography because the response of our eyes are geometric. What looks like an evenly spaced grey scale has squares that are either twice or half as reflective as its neighbors, depending on which way you go.

The pattern: log 1 = 0, as 10^0 =1; log 10 = 1, as 10^1 = 10; and log 100 = 2, as 10^2 = 100. Can you see it?

It gets nutty when dealing with numbers other than powers of ten. Milestones along the logarithmic path I teach my photo students are:

Neutral density filter of .3 passes 1/2 of the light through it;

ND of .6 = 1/4, ND 1 = 1/10. Stacking and ND .3 on top of ND .6 for ND of .9 lets only1/8th of the light through. ND numbers being logarithms allow you to add them while the transmission numbers are being multiplied. For shear mind-blowing mathematical trivia, 10^.3010 = 2.

Looking at the Fuji spectral sensitivity curve. Looks like He-Cd 442 has a relative sensitivity of 1.0; 560 at about .4. The difference is .6, translating to 1/4th of the sensitivity. It looks like doubled YAG at 532 has a relative sensitivity of 1.3, so it has 1.3 - 1.0 (@442) = .3 or antilog .3 = twice the sensitivity.

This curve is quite roller coastery, implying some strange brew of dyes. On the other hand, the Kodak Spectroscopic Plates 649-F that were used for early laser transmission holograms because it had good enough resolving power had an almost level panchromatic curve. (Pan = thoughout, chroma = color)

Preliminary testing

Under very dim lighting I opened the cardboard mailer to find a 4x5” stack of film packed in a tightly fitting black plastic bag. Very tightly fitting. Getting the film stack back in was a pain. The film was sandwiched between two small pieces of cardboard. I haven't counted to make sure there are 10 pieces of film but that's what's listed on the label.

The film is very flat but does have a very slight, gentle curl which I'm going to presume is with the emulsion layer on the concave side. I have to presume because the film has no notches (as otherwise stated in the tech documents) to indicate which is the film side. For anyone that cuts smaller film from larger stock that is notched (Dave) make sure you notch the film similarly to the Fuji docs so you can more easily find the emulsion side in the future. The lip test won't work on this film since there's gelatin on both sides.

For this test I cut a 4x5” piece in half twice and simply stuck the new 2x2.5” piece in my existing copy setup (using the penguin master) and exposed for the same time I would when using PFG-01 treated with TEA. Ideally I would have either treated the Fuji film with TEA or increased the exposure time slightly but as this was intended to be a quick “let's see what we get without trying” kind of test I didn't worry about any of that.

Twenty minutes and thirteen seconds later (the latter being the exposure time) I headed back into the lab to develop the hologram. I ran the film through a pre-soak bath, JD-2 developer (roughly equivalent to CWC2) that was heated slightly under regular agitation for 90 seconds, stop bath for 20 seconds, rinse bath, EDTA for two minutes (the film cleared quite slowly) and then through filtered running water for another minute.

During development the film went fairly dark by 30 seconds but I wanted to keep the film in slightly longer than I would if I'd been doing a PFG-01 copy of the penguin so 90 seconds it was. Fuji recommended four to five minute development and said that the film would go dark fast (sounds a bit like PFG-03) but should remain in the developer for the full time to get the best results. Since this was a quick and dirty test I increased my own time but just gave a nod toward their recommendation.

After processing, the film was a light gray color reminiscent of Kodak SO-somethingorother I used twenty years ago and looking at the hologram in transmission mode under a white light showed some decent penguin-shaped diffraction patterns.

So... After drying what sort of hologram did I get? Not a great one but not too bad either. The emulsion obviously shrank because it's replaying in a pleasing golden color but is dimmer than the same hologram I made on PFG-01 a few days ago. That could be due to any number of factors such as over/under exposure and/or over/under development that I'll track down in my upcoming formal testing.

One further observation is that my drying methods will have to change for this film. With PFG-01 I normally just squeegee it dry and leave it emulsion up in a clean area and let it air dry. With the extra gelatin layer I'm as likely to end up with the hologram stuck to something if I do that, so I'll have to start hanging my film again.

This is a report on some qualitative results of tests I carried out this week at CalArts, where I teach, on the new Fuji film. The film was supplied by Mike Jeffries, a west coast rep of Fuji-Hunt, who has already posted to this Forum category. He attended the test session to watch me and two of my students, Mark Johnson and John Bryant, carry out the tests and check out our setup.

In brief, we got very good results, with some unexpected features.

The tests were carried out using the class' standard setup and photochemistry, so the results could be compared with the usual class results. We shoot remaining Agfa 8E-75 film and new Slavich PFG-01 plates. The setup is a Denisyuk stand, with the beam traveling upward at 45 degrees through a glass platen, and the plate or film lies on it, with the object on top. For film, the object rests on a separate sheet of glass that pins the film. No index match is used. The laser is a HeNe of 7mW, though that figure has not been recently checked. The beam is spread to overfill a 4x5 plate by about 20%, and no spatial filter is used.

Our photochemistry is normally: develop in Kodak D-19 for 90 seconds, rinse etc.; bleach until clear in the 4-component FeEDTA bleach (see Ed Wesly's article years ago in Leonardo), redevelop in dilute ascorbic acid (same article) until tomato-grey (film) or tomato-brown (PFG-01), D = about 1.5 (visual estimate), to remove the bleach fog. We normally get a red image after the last step, just a bit short of the laser color.

For the Agfa film, best results are usually from a 7 second exposure, and for the PFG-01, without distilled pre-soak, 11 seconds is usually optimum.

Based on the Fuji data sheets and previous posts to this Forum, we chose to try exposures that about bracketed our usual: 8 seconds and twelve seconds. In addition, we painted stripes of TEA on a third sheet, and exposed for 6 seconds. The stripes were for 2%, 6% and 10% concentrations; normally these give yellow, green and blue images with the above processing.

The object was a metal hand tool, with a rigid white tile about an inch back, to form a bright background, with even scattering and a good shadow of the object.

We examined each shot after each stage of processing, and found the following: for the 8 second exposure, we got a strong image after development, yellow in color, quite clear, with a strong shadow on the tile, and a D = about 2.0. The image took about 12 minutes to clear in the bleach, fairly typical for the usual plates and film. The image was then considerably brighter, with higher contrast, the color was yellow-orange, and the scattering was very low. It was much clearer than either Agfa or Slavich at this stage. It was also colorless in transmission before rinsing; Agfa is normally very pink and the Slavich slightly green at this point. Redevelopment did very little, and I halted it when my patience ran out after 12 minutes. It was then just slightly darker and grey in transmission, density maybe .5. The color of the image remained about as before, and the image was perhaps slightly darker.

The 12 second exposure was similar after development, but started out darker, color green, D= about 2.3. Bleaching clear (12 minutes) made the image brighter as before, and the color was yellow-orange. Redevelopment gave similar results to the above, but the image was a bit darker, and orange. Photoflo made both these images red. This has not happened before with our usual materials and I suspect that the concentration, which was mixed by another student and abnormally high, made it act as a swelling agent. Overall, the quality of the 8 second image was better.

The TEA shot turned out after development to give a clear image. The yellow (2%) was yellow, D= about 1.8; the green was green, D= about 2.0; and the blue was green too, D= about 2.3. It bleached clear in about 14 minutes. The colors were then orange, yellow and green. Redevelopment made the film slightly darker, and the colors orange-red, yellow-orange, and more green. Photoflo left these each a bit more red.

We also put some chips out to check for fog. The 3 cases tested were on the table under general fluoresent light, in the darkroom 6 feet from a sodium vapor safelight, and in the darkroom 2 feet under our 25watt Wratten green filter (number forgotten). I estimate less than a minute in the first case will develop fog above D= .5, after 3 minutes under the sodium lamp, and after 4-5 minutes under the green safelight. The Fuji literature says to use a narrow band source in the range 570-590nm range, and their sensitivity curve shows a big dip there, as in the Agfa and Slavich. My conclusion is that we should have no problem using our standard development under our usual green safelight. The holograms confirm this.

The film was as easy to handle as the Agfa, easier than Slavich, which is thinner. There is a notch on the large sheets. (We cut them to 4x5. Mike Jeffries says that they are likely to supply this size too.) If the notch is on the upper right with a long edge to the right, the emulsion is facing you. The emulsion side is the concave side. It was not possible to tell the difference by dry touch or moist touch, though I will try the latter again. Pre-exposure, the film was transparent and colorless. It was possible to scratch the emulsion, but it is harder than the PFG-01.

Overall, the images are very bright. They are almost comparable to the PFG-01, and have very low scattering. It seems clear that our third step, of redevelopment, may be omitted with reliably clear excellent results. This was really unexpected. The one downside is that it appears impossible to get a color close to the laser color with this Fuji film and our photochemistry. Perhaps with a pre-hardening step, a different developer (CWC2?), or modifications to the emulsion, this may be achieved.

We plan to carry out more tests next week. Any comments or questions are welcome. If anyone can drop me some how-to hints I may try to transmit color images of our results. Where would I post them??

Bill Alschuler
