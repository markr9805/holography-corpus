Joe Farina Tue Aug 22, 2006 5:56 pm #1
Since variable beamsplitting can be difficult (without substantial losses), the other day I was wondering if you could just intercept the beam with the edge of a mirror. (That is, let part of the beam pass unobstructed, while reflecting the other part using the very edge of a mirror.) Of course, polarizing cube beamsplitters (with half-wave plates) are available, but these need the proper coatings if losses are to be minimized. And they're not cheap. Metallized beamsplitters involve some pretty terrible losses.

Maybe most holographers have wondered about this at some point. The Gaussian profile will be "cut" but I wonder if this will really have an adverse effect during spatial filtering. (I haven't tried it yet.)

Then last weekend I found an old paper in the American Journal of Physics (Volume 44, Number 7, July 1976) wherein they actually do this. They chose to cement two mirrors together, and do some "retro-reflecting" as well, but this seems superfluous to me.

If it actually works, then one could use a high-quality broadband mirror, carefully grind one edge down (if necessary) and losses should be very low. Seems too simple to be true.
Tom B. Thu Aug 24, 2006 4:22 am #2
In which Saxby calls a "bypass" setup the beam is split spatially after expansion and collimation - part of the beam illuminates the object, and the remainder serves as a reference to illuminate the plate, usually with an ND filter inline. I've done this and it works fine.

Doing this before beam expansion seems feasible - there would be some diffraction ripples from the sharp mirror edge that splits the beam, but if it was a problem I think spatial filtering would clean it up. Or if this was a hassle, then doing SOME beam expansion (i. e. expanding from 1 mm up to a few mm or more before splitting) would reduce diffraction effects and would make alignment easier.

Definitely worth a try.

I am a little confused on this whole thread.

Joe, you state that originally the subject focus was variable beam splitting without too much loss.

Now, how would you use a broadband mirror's edge to vary intensity? By intercepting more or less of a beam thus reflecting more or less? If so I see a couple of flaws here and your tests would be a good argument for or against.

As I see it, and this is from first hand observation and as Tom pointed out, when you hit the edge of anything you will get some destructive interference diffraction lines. Even something as nicely "edged" as a razor blade. This introduces losses. Then, as the beam is not Gaussian, and you feed it through a spatial filter you will either have to throw away anything that is not round (provided your pinhole is round) to get a clean beam or if you encompass the entire beam so as not to waste light you will get additional diffraction rings. Then you are going to have additional losses in trying to evenly illuminate a square or rectangular plate or film with an off axis Gaussian profile.

Either way I would have to say you will introduce more losses and have to waste more light then a silverized beam splitter would produce.

How lossy are silverized beam splitters? I thought only about 10%.

But again the proof is in the pudding and your tests will clearly show if this would be a more efficient way of beam splitting. Keep us posted!

Cementing a thick piece of glass to a thin beamsplitter works well (to separate the back reflection). I've done this a couple of times with the Edmund rectangular variable beamsplitter. I would suggest using a good-quality UV optical adhesive, and avoid storing it in a high-humidity environment, or it may begin to de-laminate.

Another factor affecting the efficiency of metallized beamsplitters might be the angle of incidence. At steep angles of incidence, the beam will go through a larger amount of metal, so I would expect higher losses.
Joe Farina Mon Aug 28, 2006 1:26 pm #14
John, I forgot to clarify a couple of things. The link you provided to the Surplus Shed seems to show a circular variable neutral density filter, not a beamsplitter. Also, I wanted to define what I mean by "loss." Say you measure the input beam to the splitter as 10mW. Then measure the "throughput" beam as 6mW, and the reflected beam as 2mW. Adding 2 to 6 yields 8, and 8 divided by 10 yields 80%, or a 20% loss.

John FP wrote:

> I am not sure how to make it variable though.

Just vary the exposure! Make a series of different ones from threshold on up, and you will have a variety of fixed ratios!

Polarizing beamsplitting cubes are the most thrifty when it comes to light losses. A broadband one may be kind of expensive. Wavelength specific ones will work at other wavelengths, but a whole range of ratios may not be available. But it will split to some degree!

An alternative to wavelength specific quartz half-wave plates are mica! See Melles Griot Optics Guide, polarization components. When I got my 10 mm diameter ones, they were only $40!
