Dinesh Thu Apr 21, 2011 12:52 pm #2
Each colour laser is independent. So, the polarisation of the object and reference beams have to be the same, as much as possible, but there doesn't need to be any correlation between lasers. None of the three lasers know that there are two other lasers in the neighbourhood. Treat it just like mono-colour holography, ie just treat each laser as if you're making three separate holograms, except that they're all on the same plate.

The colour balance is a delicate mix of mixture ratio and exposure. It's usually the most difficult thing to get right, especially for yellow. On the other hand, judicious use of subject matter may make colour rendition moot. For example, if you shoot M & M's, all you need are three colours. No one knows the original colours of the M & M's so if they see M & M's in different colours, they really won't see any colour mismatch. The most difficult thing to do is the human face. If people see a human figure, the first rhing they notice is the face. If the face is wrong, the whole picture is seen as wrong.

So start with M & M's!

Tom B. Fri Apr 22, 2011 2:32 am #5
I think mismatched color polarization could be a problem with some shiny or semi-shiny objects. Essentially, the shiny highlights in one color would not match up with those in a different color (polarization) because while the object angles are constant the polarization angle changes with color. But the results could be interesting. It would give you a color coded map of the polarized reflectivity of the object at various angles. With the right object, this could be attractive. I expect that for most objects, the effect would be subtle but since we tend to focus on highlights, the color imbalance would be bothersome if it was not intended.

Maybe if the reference beam for each color was parallel to the nominal object beam for an white reflector it would be OK, but a real object reflects light depending on angle of incidence, surface properties, and polarization of the light source.

Dinesh Mon Apr 25, 2011 11:54 am #13
> John Klayer: Doesn't it scramble the polarizations?

It doesn't matter whether it scrambles the polarisations. At worst, you'll get woodgrain on two wavelength components. The object itself will depolarise anyway if it's a diffuse object. If the polarisatons of the reference of the three components are all different, It's always possible to rotate one of the wavelength contributions to get p polarisation and get woodgrain on the other two. Remember that the fringe structure for each component is independent of the other two.

What I'm not seeing is that in Danny's picture three beams are going in and one is coming out. But, in the sub-picture, one beam is coming in and three beams are coming out, making it seem like a splitter as opposed to a combiner.
