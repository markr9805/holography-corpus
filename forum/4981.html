It would be nice to find a material (paint) that can be used to coat a material then coat with emulsion on top of that. Then expose with a single object beam from an object and have the object beam hit the emulsion, go through the emulsion and diffusely reflect back off the paint to the plate as a reference beam to create a reflection hologram on an opaque surface.

Yes, the idea is pretty much, almost completely, half-baked or even whole-baked! Anything or anyone else that may be half baked is only a complement to the idea itself! No, resist will not work.

[quote:94bf78058d="JohnFP"]It would be nice to find a material (paint) that can be used to coat a material then coat with emulsion on top of that. Then expose with a single object beam from an object and have the object beam hit the emulsion, go through the emulsion and diffusely reflect back off the paint to the plate as a reference beam to create a reflection hologram on an opaque surface.[/quote:94bf78058d]

In this scenario, the emulsion is sitting above the paint. In other words substrate (glass, eg) - paint - emulsion, ie the paint is sandwiched between glass and emulsion, and you're shooting from "behind" the plate - ie in a "reflection" geometry but with no reference. Firstly, you're probably going to find it difficult to coat such a system without the emulsion coming off during procesing. I suspect that bonding between paint and emuslion will not be very strong. The chemists will have to confirm this. So now the object beam hits the emulsion, continues on and hits the paint beneath it. The paint will cause the object waveform to scatter. The idea is that this scattered waveform will now apparently act as a reference.

Firstly, the paint will scatter light sideways, thus very little light will even enter the emulsion. You're ratio will be way-y-y-y off!. Secondly, the paint will scatter the "reference" randomly, thus it'll be impossible to reconstruct. If the paint is black and matte(no mention of the colour or type of paint) then most of the light will be absorbed by the paint. There are many more problems, but these are the main ones.

[quote:94bf78058d="JohnFP"]So hypothetically one could coat a solid object with resist, expose it to a reference and object beam in a transmission set up, process it and see a reflection hologram relay? Forgot about that one. [/quote:94bf78058d]

Resist will not take volume fringes. You cannot get Bragg diffraction off resist, only Raman Nath diffraction will work (However, note papers I've mentioned by Cheng and by Moharam and Gaylord). The reason is that the etch will not penetrate the resist to differentiate the more exposed parts of the resist. Also there's that I_1 + I_2 term which swamps the I_1I_2cos(<phase difference>) term. Resist is a funny beast, in that you actually routinely see a resist hologram in "reflection", even though it's made in a "transmission" geometry. I've heard of (cruel, in my opinion) interviewers asking potential employees what the difference is between transmssion and reflection is. When they give the standard answer, they're shown a resist plate, which apparently contradicts them!

[quote:94bf78058d="JohnFP"]Might one be able to do this with a very thin layer of DCG?[/quote:94bf78058d]

Depends on how thin and what the spatial frequency is. In general, I'd say no. Having said that, DCG was first used as a printing material around the turn of the last century in which this sort of scheme was actually used. I've speculated on whether this can be done on DCG and I've played with it with so-so success.

[quote:94bf78058d="JohnFP"]The "Paint" would need to be reflective.

The dried "Paint" would need to only reflect light that is polarized in one direction. Or at least act as a polarizing filter when dried.

The "Paint" would need to have organized reflective suspended bumps such that the light reflected would seem to eminate from a single distant point source.

The reflection bumps would also need to be phase selective such that only light at a particular phase would be reflected.

(Did I miss anything?) [/quote:94bf78058d]

Yes.

If the paint acts as a transmission medium, as it does if it's between emulsion and substrate, it will not reflect but transmit, albeit, in a random, diffuse manner. The random, distinct bumps would act as Rayleigh or Mie scattering centers, depending on their size. At these levels, the standard laws of reflection no longer apply. Certainly, the Fresnel equations would not hold in a predominantly diffractive regime, thus polarisation considerations are also invalid. Also, I suspect that the main diffractive regime would be Fresnel rather than Fraunhofer, which would add further complications. You cannot "phase select" a bump (whatever that means!). The phase of the scattered light would depend in random to the velocity of these bumps, the exact shape, the angle of incidence, the incoming phase of the light that hit a particular bump after having reflected off a nearby bump and the electronic structure of the paint. In general, you'd have to tackle the problem by first determining the sigma of the sizes and their orientations. You'd also have masive cross-talk which you'd have to take into account.

These are some of the objections. There are more.

By the way, The Denisyuk scheme of "referenece-less" holograms is nothing like this!

Some substances are opaque in white light, but may be transprent in the very smmall bandwidth of a laser at a particular frequency. For instance, if you coated the back of a substrate with dyes that transmitted only a, say 50nm bandwidth of gree around 500, ie it transmits everything from 475nm to 525nm, then it would be tranmissive to 514 but would appear opaque because it's reflecting the other 250 odd nm of white light. If the coating were very thin, for example a thin gold plated substance with a plating thickness of about a micron or so, might transmit enough light. Metals have a "skin depth" of a few microns, which is why they're shiny. However, some materials, like copper and gold, have absorption in the yellow-green region, which is why they're yellow/green. Their skin depth to these wavelengths might be shallow enough to transmist 514.

Dinesh Fri Aug 19, 2005 10:55 pm #8
You're probably aware of this, but let's recap.

The fringes form throughout space, all 3 dimensions of it. They form some form of 3D conic section, a hyperboloid for example. The exact form of the conic depends on the sources for the two waves. You place the plate somewhere in this volume. Exactly where you place the plate determines whether or not you have a reflection or transmission. The sheets of interference planes either are parallel to the emulsion surfaces or they're perpendicular to them. However, the interference planes exist throughout the emulsion volume. In all cases the volume gets affected, but in some cases the volume cannot be accessed, such as in photoresist and thermoplastics. In these cases, the processing method distorts the surface but cannot alter the volume inside the emulsion. OK, let me say, the "standard" processing method, so Bob, Jeff or Martin or some of the chemists don't jump down on me!. In these "surface" holograms, the light need not enter through the emulsion, because any actinic reactions within the volume can't make any difference anyway. However, if the material has an index higher than air, then it's better to view them as a "reflection" because the light diffracting by reflection off the surface profile causes a greater phase change than light diffracting by going through the material. If you have a piece of resist with a hologram on it, you'll see that it's stronger in reflection mode than transmission mode. In this sense, you can make a hologram on resist in transmission geometry and view it in reflection geometry. However, bear in mind that this is Raman Nath diffraction and so, according to Kogelnik, you can get at best 30% odd de. I've often not even got more than 10.

Can you make a reflection-geometry hologram on an opaque substrate? As I said earlier, yes it's possible. "opaque" generally refers to photometric opacity, not radiometric. Thus it may be possible to have something that photometrically opaque but not radiometrically opaque.

A bust of Newton? If you can find a high-index, transparent plastic, cover the bust with this plastic, coat the plastic and mould it around the head then zap it with an appropriate (ie reproducible) reference then the beam hits the emulsion, goes through it and reflects off your high-index plastic. Since the plastic is high-index and moulded right on the face, it may be possible to recover the contours of the face. If you now ca-a-a-refully remove the plastic from the head, you now have a hollow piece of plastic which, when reconstructed, seems to have a bust of Newton in it. Now you replace Newton with an Einstein bust.. Since the plastic is transparent you'll see both Isaac and Albert superimposed. Of course, the fcial profiles won't match. Einstein had a round head while Newton's seems to have been long and narrow. Genius comes in all shapes!
