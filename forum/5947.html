Joe Farina Wed Dec 27, 2006 1:29 pm #5
I think that humidity control may be the most important part of DCG technique. It is also a frequently overlooked factor affecting the holographic stability of the islolation table and components.

I have, and treasure, a short paper called Application Note No. 21: Complete Fringe Stabilization Techniquesby a company called Data Optics, Inc., in Ypsilanti, Michigan, which is just outside of Ann Arbor. I don't know if this is available on-line, but it was part of a catalog that I have from them on a CD. These are the best notes that I have on holographic stability, and are more extensive and credible than anything else I have. They state on page 3:

"Although the effects of laser mode hopping and temperature change are well known, the effect of humidity change is not well characterized. In order to maintain fringe stability, it is imperative to maintain a relatively constant humidity in the surrounding environment."

So regarding your specific question, Cristiano, I would give some thought to controlling the humidity in the entire working area, if possible. If you used a small sealed container for your plates, the moisture content would begin to change as soon as you remove them, and you are using very long exposure times. If you control the enitre working area, you won't have this problem, and it will likely enhance your table stability as well.

I haven't done this yet in my lab, but my plan is to use an automatic dehumidifier (these aren't too expensive, maybe $125 around here) in combination with a humidifier (less expensive). In my case, I will probably use one of those air-conditioning units which stand on the floor and which also have a dehumidifying function. All of this will require testing, and in my case I haven't gotten that far yet.

Yes and a simple idea that I have found to work to make shallow Denisyuk holos in a controlled atmosphere is to take a polystyrene plastic box fitted with a lid and cut

away (or melt away with a soldering iron) a rectangular section in the lid and replace that section with a sheet of glass. Polystyrene in particular and not polythene, polypropylene or PVC was needed because it was then easy to seal the glass in with any old transparent solvent based glue. You can then set up your object in the box with the unexposed plate for overhead spread laser light through the glass window in the lid. So you can have a little tray of say saturated calcium nitrate solution with a bit of cloth or blotting paper sticking out of it to speed up the time to get to equilibrium in the box after you have sealed the lid on with a bit of easily removable

pvc tape.

Actually for the advanced experimenter I will mention the really useful feature of this set up was that I could make a little hole in the corner of the lid to fit a little nozzle tube and another hole in the opposite corner to fit an outlet nozzle . I could then flush the box with nitrogen gas to drive out the oxygen ( I knew the oxygen content was low enough when the gentle gas stream from the exit nozzle blew out a lighted match.) This then enabled me to experiment with free radical polymerization

processes like the original Polaroid photopolymer system or my own variant of it using zinc diacrylate. --These systems are rather inclined to be quenched or slowed down by arial oxygen.--- Hans brought up on the Forum this type of system last February here:

[http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4284](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4284)
