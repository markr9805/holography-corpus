Hi everyone.

I'm a semi regular (reading only) guest here since years

and have fallen in love with the idea of making holograms more then five years ago.

Some months ago I finally had all the materials together and made some space in my bedroom for my mini-holo-table.

About a handfull of 30+ shots where somewhat successful . But more complex scenes than a stack of coins (under the filmplate) never worked.

The problem was.. I had to manually lift a piece of cardboard (my shutter) to shoot my holos. So.. after letting everything settle for a half hour in the pitch-dark lab,

I had to open the door, sneak in, shut the door in slowmotion and hold my breath to finally unblock the laser beam.

No wonder a lot of them didn't work out at all..

With not much hobby-money and no mechanics or electronics skills I feared I'm doomed to shoot coins for the rest of my life

But theres some cool metal wire called Nitinol and a special version of it called Flexinol witch contracts when heated (or when power is applied to it).

This is the simple idea: Shutter = cardboard moving up , and Flexinol = Electro-Muscle which can lift stuff when powered per battery.

I built a first primitive version and it works perfectly fine. Maybe there are lots of ideas for simplest electro-shutter out there but since I didn't find one that I fully understood and didn't scare me I put my findings online.

[http://bedroomholography.weebly.com/diy-shutter.html](http://bedroomholography.weebly.com/diy-shutter.html)

Hope some other beginners have a use for this. And can build upon it.

I'm ready now for more adventurous shots

Yes, very cool design.

I figure I will throw the three shutters that I made out there.

The easiest remote controlled one was a simple servo and controller, the kind you use on a model plane. Hook a blocker up to the servo. And with the remote control, just control it. You cold be pretty far away. You can hack one of these out of an old radio controlled plain, boat or car.

Another one was an analog volt meter. Take the cover off the needle and attach (glue) a pice of foil (blocker) to the needle such that it stands up. You could also use a piece of black tape if your laser is not too powerful. Then simply put the meter with the blocker blocking the beam. When you want to open the shutter, apply a voltage to the meter and the needle will swing the blocker out of the way of the beam. This shutter you mount such that the meter is not lying flat but vericle in such a way that applying the voltage makes the needle move up again gravity. When the voltage it taken away gravity pulls the shutter back closed (the meter is relaxed) This is nice because you can run wires from the + and - where the batteries go and remotely apply a voltage away from the table. To be more creative you can use the parallel port (printer port) of your computer to apply the voltage if you know the pin out.

Finally, I made one using the shutter in an old manual movie camera. Same idea as abover. Just apply a voltage (mine was 3V as the camera took two double A batteries) and the shutter would open. The shutter was static (which I liked for long exposures in that the voltage to open it did not have to be constant) and I had to reverse the polarity to close the shutter thus I needed a relay system in which I rigged up two sets of wire to the shutter, one reversed polarity to the other set. I bought relay system and connected it to the parallel port so that I could plug in a settle and exposure time in a batch file and the rest was automatic.

Very nice Thomas, well done.

A while back, I had the same idea and bought some muscle wire for a shutter, but never got around to building a prototype. I'm glad you've developed this idea, and proven that it works.

Some people have stressed the importance of using multiple shutters in complicated setups, with the shutters being close to the hologram being recorded. The reasoning is that the components warm up as soon as the laser beam strikes them, and continue to do so for the length of the exposure. This might be a factor to consider when using a higher-powered laser like a 100mW 315M in a split beam or transfer situation. If the shutters are way downstream, then the components (mirrors, etc.) will be pre-warmed prior to the exposure, thus reducing path length variations for longer exposure times.

With such a simple method like you developed, it would be easy to add multiple shutters for that kind of setup.

It's not very significant. Lenses, by their nature, do not absorb a lot of the incoming radiation (providing they're clean!) hence they do not warm up. MIrrors may, depending on the angle of incidence and the polarisation. A quick and dirty way of finding out whether this is true is to measure the light hitting the mirror and the light reflecting from it. Whatever left over is absorbed. So, if the light hitting the mirror is I(h) and the light reflecting off is I(r), then:

I(h) - I(r) = msdelta(theta)

where m is the mass of the mirror ( probably a few grams)

s is the specific heat of glass (obtainable from tables)

delta(theta) is the rise in temperature.

The expansion in thickness, delta(t) is then:

delta(t) = alphatdelta(t)

where alpha is the coefficient of linear expansion of glass (obtainable from tables)

t is the original thickness of the glass

delta(t) is the change in thickness.

From this, the change in phase due to the expansion is:

delta(phi) = (2pi/lambda)delta(t).

I suspect however, that this will be small. I also suspect that if anyone is worried about this, they should also be worried by air currents flowing over the table.

> Joe Farina: I wonder what would be absorbed by a plain glass beamsplitter (or maybe a polarizing cube beamsplitter) and if the warming-up could be a problem. The expansion of glass is quite small, but the reflected beam would be shortened by a small amount (for the plain glass) as it warms up. I guess not enough to be an issue?

With beamsplitters it's a little more complicated because while one beam is shortened, the other is lengthened. I suppose it would all have to do with the angle of incidence and the polarisation. The steeper the angle, the less is transmitted, but he increase in path length is a sin function of the expansion and the angle of incidence, which is increased. Despite all this, I don't think it's much of an effect unless really high powers are being used, higher than the hundreds of milliwatts typically used in holography. If you're concerned, the easiest way to test this is to use the beam off a beamsplitter as one mirror of a Michaelson and watch the fringes long(ish) term. You might also turn up the power of the laser gradually (if you can do this) and watch for fringe movement. You might even set up two Michaelsons, one from the reflected beam and one from the transmitted..

Ed Wesly Mon Apr 30, 2012 1:18 pm #11
The main reason to use separate shutters on reference and objects beams is when you are putting tens or hundreds of milliWatts into spatial filters. A not very well-tuned one, or a drifting one, can go completely out of whack during exposures of seconds on up. Putting the shutter after the spread and cleaned beam helps ensure success.

I even have a CCTV camera monitor my set ups to make sure that both beams are arriving, after having my pinholes being "burned" a few times.

Both Bill Molteni and Craig Newswanger turned me on to this trick.
