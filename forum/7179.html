Ok, here is a thought problem. Again, this is a mechanical thought problem. I hope is sparks some conversation!

Let's make a reflection hologram of a small 1mm^2 diffusing object (we will call it a star), 8 inches from plate. I would start with a 2:1 ref:obj beam ratio and probably be pretty close to optimal ration for brightness.

Now let's take 500 of those little stars and place them randomly, all 8 inches from plate. Obviously the amount of diffused reflected light for each star cannot be equal to the amount of diffused reflected light for the single star example above or we would have 2:500 or 1:250 (ref:obj). So we have to give each star about 1/500th of the light we gave the single star while maintaining the reference intensity so that our average ratio at the plate is again 2:1 but our ratio of a single star in the multi-star hologram is 1000:1 to the reference.

We look at the two holograms and compare brightness. Each star on the multi-star hologram is as bright at the star on the single star hologram. But we see from above that the ratio of a single star to reference is very different in the single star and the multi star hologram.

Why is this? Ed Wesly Thu Feb 28, 2008 7:14 pm #7
This thought problem is a variation on something Hans Bjelkhagen and I had discussed many times in the past when he was writing his book, but for one reason or another we never ran the experiment. (And I may have described this earlier on this forum but it may have suffered collateral damage during the forum-wrecking episode a couple of years ago.)

Bjelkhagen’s postulate is the inverse of John’s. Set up a piece of groundglass in front of a holo plate, and shine light through it so meets up with a reference beam, preferably collimated. Could be transmission, could be reflection. For the sake of this argument, let’s say hologram and diffuser are 4 by 5 inches.

Set a beam ratio 2:1, 4:1, whatever, and make a good exposure. Replay the real image of the diffuser onto a power meter, and read the intensity of the projected image.

Keeping all parameters the same, expose a second plate, but this time mask off all but a square centimeter (or whatever the detector size of the power meter is) of the diffuser. Obviously when the beam balance ratio is taken, the object side is down low, in the proportion of the surface areas of the whole versus masked off diffuser, but when this holo is exposed and developed and interrogated, will the small spot be any less bright than its corresponding spot on the whole screen? We didn’t think so, but it hasn’t been verified experimentally.

Going in the opposite direction, setting the beam balance ratio with the small spot to say 4:1, and then unmasking the groundglass to its full aperture, would give a BBR heavily on the object side, so much so that probably noise would dominate.

My basic rule for determining the “right” ratio is to start with something sensible, like 4:1 or so, make an exposure test to get the best brightness. If this ain’t bright enough, then drop the ratio to 2:1. But if the black shadows are full of snow (noise) or there’s a halo around the object, another indicator of too low of a ratio, then raise the reference beam intensity.

There really is no ideal ratio for holograms, except for maybe making gratings where you really want 1:1 for best contrast, and you can get away with it because there would be no intermodulation noise. (Noise caused by the object points interfering with themselves.) Some photo-resist holographers use 40:1 as the ratio on transfers! Emmett Leith in one of his seminal papers noted that holographic images could have a longer tonal scale than photographs, like a million to one! And when Hans and I worked at Fermilab, he calculated that you could probably get an image with a BBR of 10,000,000 to one! It’s all a matter of taking a test shot, then deciding which way to go if it’s not bright enough or too noisy!

The idea of exposing many different objects noted above onto the same plate has the advantage of lowering noise, since not all the object points are there to self-interfere simultaneously. The trick then is to balance the exposures so that all the objects come out sufficiently bright.

Another possible experiment would be to holograph a density step wedge backlit by a diffuser. Since the usual progression on these is by stops, doubling or halving intensity, the ratio of each step on the wedge to the reference beam is a fraction of the one next to it, but you will still see the whole wedge, even if there are seven steps, meaning a dynamic range of 2 to the 7th power, or 128!

Beside this, we must first agree what "brightness" means,

as there are more than 10 radiometric and photometric terms describing this.

When a hologram is illuminated, we can use the following radiometric terms, to measure its brightness:

Radiant Flux, W.

Irradiance, W/m^2. (The corresponding photometric term is illuminance, lumen/m^2)

Radiant Intensity, W/sr, sr=steradian.

Radiance, W/(sr.m^2), m^2 refers to the objectdimension (photometric -> Luminance)

It the last one that is perceived as object brightness by our eyes,

therefore, by constant diffraction efficiency, the holographic image of the 1"cube is perceived as

20 times more bright of that of a 4"x5" cube.

The irradiance (W/m^2) as well the illuminance of the holographic plateare not affected.

I think this is the answer to your thought experiment Ed. If you take a hologram of the ground glass as a whole it will be x bright. If you mask it all down except a small area, and expose it the same, that spot will be the same brightness as the same spot when it was not masked down.

Now we all know that with less angle of view the brighter the hologram so how can the above be true. Like this. Forget about the ratio. Look at the total exposure. In the first case the hologram was receiving all that object light and was fully exposed. Now in the second case the object was masked down and there was much less total light hitting the hologram. The spot is the same brightness but now you can expose much more. That brings up the brightness of the spot well above its brightness when it was not masked down.

I agree with Jeffrey. In the original thought experiment, let us assume that each hologram has been ideally exposed and processed so that each one is '100% efficient'. Now let us take the original single star and 80 star holograms and replay them both with the conjugate of the original reference beam. Each will produce a 'real image' of the star or star field, and a photodetector can be placed at each real image of the star to measure the amount of energy in the image.

For the single star hologram, all the energy in the replay beam impingeing on the plate will go to reconstruct the single star. For the multiple star hologram, all the energy in the replay beam impingeing on the plate will go to reconstruct 80 stars- so the input energy has to be divided by 80 for each star. If each of the 80 stars was as bright as the single star in the single star hologram then the principle of conservation of energy would be violated.

Well, my laptop died and what I wanted to post was on it. So without to much elaboration I will try to explain my thoughts in as laymen as possible. I have only written this three times. The first on the laptop that died, the second I did not post as it was missing something, but I think I figured it out and thus can include it in this post.

Let us look at a single wave, crest to crest for the object beam and a single wave, crest to crest for the reference beam. And let us assume we have an intensity of 1 (arbitrary number) for each wave. The ideal hologram will be a 1:1 object to reference.

We will now look at a single spot on the film that receives a crest from the object and a crest from the reference. At that spot we get constructive interference and thus and intensity of 2. Now we make a second hologram on the same film of a second point on the object and this second point is exactly one wavelength away from the spot on the film. Thus we again get constructive interference and an intensity of 2. So at that point on the plate we get an total intensity of 4 with a 1:1 object to reference.

Now let’s do the same thing but instead of making two holograms of the two points we will expose them at the same time. We have one point at in intensity of 1, we have the second point at and intensity of 1 and we have a reference beam at an intensity of 1. They all constructively interfere at that point on the plate and we get a total intensity of 3 with a 1:1 object to reference for each object point.

At 10 object points all an exact wavelength from the spot on the film, with individual exposures we get an intensity of 20 but in doing all the points at one time we get an intensity of 11. This really starts to get very large when the number of points increases.

The concept here is what I have been unsuccessful in trying to explain. In looking at the light as waves, and giving the fact that waves interfere, with two exposures we must add the light of 4 wave. With a single exposure we must add the light of only three waves.

Now in working backwards, as we increase the number of individual exposures for the individual points we see that light struck fringe receives a higher intensity of light with the same 1:1 ratio, or reversed we see a higher object to ref ratio (1/2:1) if we lower the object light to keep the intensity the same as the single hologram of the two points.

So, if I wanted to make 500 holograms of small points on the same plate I would have to use very weak object light and rely on the fact that some points would reinforce one another on the plate when the were a wavelength apart. This indicates for this to be successful, we should force the individual points on the plate to be 1 wavelength or a multiple of 1 whole wavelength apart. We could then lower the object intensity by 500. With the points random, I imagine some points on plate would be optimal and some not so an intensity decrease of 500 would be too much. But surely a 1:1 would not work for each hologram would eventually turn the whole plate homogeneously exposed.
