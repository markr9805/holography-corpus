Same person, same handle, from Discord Holo Hub.

Once the initial thrill of creating a basic Denisyuk hologram dissipates, a desire to get some control over lighting and z-axis image location is often the next step. One overlooked item for the next level after Denisyuks is the “beam splitter”. “Split beam holography”, which is where stability starts to become a significant issue, allows the holographer to gain control over image/scene lighting and z-axis scene location relative to the plane of the hologram. Sources of inexpensive spatial filters were already mentioned in Discord HoloHub but overlooked was the mechanism for actually splitting the laser beam into object and reference beams. There are fixed beam splitters and variable beam splitters. Fixed beam splitters can be as simple as a piece of glass typically giving two beams at about a 90/10 ratio where the transmitted beam is 90% of the light and the reflected beam is 10%.

That piece of glass should be sufficiently thick so that the secondary reflection is physically separate from the primary reflection so the secondary and primary reflections won’t overlap and interfere with each other. The secondary reflection can then be blocked - this is usually done with a variable aperture iris but it can be blocked with a black card if the laser is about 50mw or less.

There are also non-polarizing beam splitter cubes that give a fixed automatic beam splitting ratio and are available in various fixed ratios.

The next step is a simple mirrorized piece of glass where the mirrorization is from a low optical density to a higher optical density. This allows the Holographer to gain ratio control over the split beams which provides much greater control over light intensity in different parts of the scene and over the ratio between object and reference beams recorded at the hologram plane. The variably mirrorized glass beam splitter can be in a rectangular or circular form depending on level of sophistication of mechanical control. The mirrorization itself can be a metallic or dichroic coating.

The next level of beam splitting control is use of two half wave plates and a polarizing beam splitter cube. These are the best and most sophisticated option at the moment providing the best stability, ratio control and polarization control of the two beams. This approach is not necessary at all for creating high quality finished holograms but is the best approach for more advanced holographers.

All of these variations assume a continuous wave laser is being used and each has its own”best use” case.

Regardless of the mechanics of holography, it would be a good idea to identify your market more precisely. Both security and display Holography are moving rapidly to an entirely hands off digital realm where the imagery to be recorded is generated in a computer and the digital file is then loaded into fully automated “printer”. The only hands on parts are loading the plate into the “printer” and then processing the exposed plate. With the advent of inexpensive instant self-developing photopolymers, even that final processing step is eliminated.

It’s also true that there is a new generation of deliberately hands on analog holographers coming along in the footsteps of the much older, soon to be gone, artists and professional analog and hybrid analog/digital holographers.

Fundamentally, hands on holography is now largely in the realm of hobbyists, artists, a few remaining professional commercial holographers, students in physics classes at the secondary school and university levels, and scientific researchers.

> jrburns47wrote:↑Tue Jul 14, 2026 9:31 am: Fundamentally, hands on holography is now largely in the realm of hobbyists, artists, a few remaining professional commercial holographers, students in physics classes at the secondary school and university levels, and scientific researchers.

This is true of display holography, but not of technical holography. As for scientific research, there's actually very little of it in academia; mostly research topics in holography are focused in the engineering aspects of holography, as can be seen in the papers at the SPIE photonics conferences. Apropos of engineering applications (what I call 'technical holography'), there are still a large number of of research organisations in larger corporate structures that use analogue, albeit technical, holography. We - Triple Take Holography - were involved with technical holography for larger corporations, including working with Microsoft Research. Even now, holography is used for a number of engineering applications in a number of high end 'high-tech' organisations; for example, Fiber Bragg gratings in optical communication systems, are holographic.

The difference between display holography and engineering applications of holography are mostly dependent on the level of accuracy in the process. Display holography needs fairly complicated geometries, as you point out, but without (very) high accuracy, as the final result is judged by the human visual system which can tolerate a high degree of aberration. On the other hand, engineering applications tend to have simple geometries, but need a (very) high degree of accuracy. A small variation of wavefront curvature will not be noticed by the human visual system, but will cause disruption in a highly accurate engineering system.
