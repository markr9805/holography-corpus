I took some already processed non-keeper holograms and placed them in a solution of AmDi (same concentration as making original film) and water for 1 minute. I then leaned them against a wall and let them dry for 15 minutes. I then exposed them to a quartz halogen light wich measured about 10mJ/cm^2 for 1 minute. Then fixed, water washed and dried with alcohol as normal. It seems both were red shifted in replay wavelength. I have always been able to blue shift a hologram with reprocessing but never red shifting.

My theory, which I stated somewhere before, is that when the hologram is placed in the resensitiser of course it becomes wet and swelled thus the fringes are swelled. If the hologram is not allowed to dry too much then when it is placed in the white light the fringes are still swollen slightly and the exposure to light at that time hardenes the fringes in a more expaned state. Thus after reprocessing the fringes are wider thus the red shift. I also believe that the amount of time after resensitising may give control to the amount of red shift. But again this is only a theory based on observation.

<Guys;

Alum (Aluminum Sulfate) hardens by two methods. Sulfate ion in solution decreases swell of gelatin. Aluminum, being trivalent, forms ionic bonds with amino acids. As a side note here, alum is less effective if your coating is very swollen to start with. The aluminum bonding has a limited 'reach' and becomes more effective if the gelatin swell is reduced. An acid stop bath helps the alum harden, but a water rinse after the developer does not.

These two 'reactions' are reversed by alkali and by the wash due to the copious water removing the bulk of both the aluminum and the sulfate.

Traditional true hardeners such as formaldehyde etc. form covalent (permanent) bonds with the amino acids and irreversably harden the film. They can also reduce swell by chemically pulling the amino acids closer together by means of this true bond. Washing will not remove this bond.

Strong alkali will reverse or remove any hardening in a gelatin as long as it is strong enough, treatment long enough, or hot enough. Hardening works by means of raising the melting point of gelatin through bonding (ionic or covalent).

Gelatin swelling is minimized at the isoelectric (neutral) point of the gelatin. For normal gelatin, this is about pH 4.5. Therefore, the sweet spot is at that pH for minimizing swell and scratching. But, there is a tradeoff with swell and fix or wash efficiency. As swell goes down, the 'pores' in gelatin become smaller, and restrict the diffusion of fix chemistry into and out of the gelatin thereby requiring increased fix and wash times.

The best compromise, as I have been trying to explain in other posts, is a fix with a pH of about 6.5. It is a compromise between the extremes of acid and base fixes for speed of fixing, swell, and wash times.

Gelatin swell is measured with a swellometer in microns, and hardness is measured with an abradometer which measures scratchability in terms of grams of pressure. The pH of gelatin in a coating is measured by means of a surface pH electrode. All of this is very expensive specialized equipment used by EK and other mfgrs to test films for hardness.

I have simplified this a lot. Photographic chemistry and engineering is a very large and complicated field. We only touch the surface in these discussions. There are a lot of proprietary methods and a lot of art used as well as what I mention here.

Ron Mowrey>

On the subject of red-shifting DCG, I found an interesting paper called "Wavelength shifting and bandwidth broadening in DCG" by Corlatan/Schafer/Anders in SPIE 1507, pages 354-64 (1991, in Holographic Optics III: Principles and Applications).

For red-shifting of reflection DCG holograms, the temperature of the IPA had a pronounced effect.

They had two graphs on page 358 showing how the IPA temperature affected 1) the central reconstruction wavelength, and 2) the bandwidth.

The first graph shows a large red-shift when the IPA temperature is raised from 20 to 35C. At 20C, the reconstruction centers at about 565nm, but at 35C it moves up to about 680nm. So it looks like a shift of over 100nm. However, there seems to be an increase in bandwidth at this temperature (about double) compared to the 20C IPA sample.

When the IPA temperature is raised only 5C higher to 40C, the bandwidth becomes very large, maybe 5 times as much compared to the 20C sample, without any additional red-shifting as compared to the 35C sample. So 35C or lower may be the optimal temperature for red-shifting.

The paper seems to leave out some important details, like the recording wavelength and the exact processing procedures, but I found the results to be very interesting. Maybe this could be used in conjunction with John's method to produce even greater wavelength shifts.
