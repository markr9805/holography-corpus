Partly because lenses are refractive elements and partly because of the need for stability during reconstruction in a complex optical system. To make things more complicated, there are two different kinds of diffraction: Bragg and Raman Nath, and these have different properties.

Let's take a camera as an example. The basic arguments will probably carry over to any optical instrument, with some reservations. It could be possible, for example, to replace the lens of a refractive, Galilean telescope with a hologram, but not a reflective telescope (ironically, for the same reasons that Newton invented the refelecting telescope!). In a camera, the scene extends across the field of view and away from the camera lens, there is a considerable variation in x, y and z. All the light from these diverse directions will focus in a one-to-one relationship on the focal plane where (hopefully) the film rests. This is because all light refracts in a refractive system, no matter the direction or depth. There will be lens aberrations, but these can be countered with a suitable combination of lenses. The defocusing of a scene along the z direction can be countered by stopping down the aperture. If you replaced such a system with a hologram, all the light may not diffract. If the hologram is a "thick" hologram, ie the diffraction mechanism was Bragg, then only a very narrow range of angles of the incoming light would diffract. In addition, only a very narrow range of wavelengths would diffract. You'd effectively get a highly saturated, relatively dim, single colour image of the parts of the scene that encompassed an angular range of maybe 10 degrees. Also, the focusing properties of the hologram would have to be built into the hologram on recording by the divergence of the "object" beam. This means that there is almost no depth of view. Closing the aperture would have no effect on this. You could have a combnation of holograms, but each "sub-hologram" would have to have a very high photopic and scotopic efficiency else there will be significant light loss. Getting high photopic and scotopic ain't easy! On top of all this, your Bragg hologram would need to be extra-ordinarily thick (an emulsion depth of 100's of microns) in order to be Bragg transmissive. You could, of course, have a combination of transmissive and reflective elemsts, but besides the complexity, you'd have the same issues of light loss because it's not easy to get the hologram to be optically clear to all but the diffractive wavelengths. Also, the same problem occurs that only a narrow cone of light will diffract.

You could put in a transmissive element, but then you'd have severe dispersion giving rise to what Bob calls chromatic aberration. Dispersion is not actually chromatic aberration because, for one thing, the colours go the opposite way. In diffractive dispersion, the red end is deviated more than the blue, while in refractive chromatic dispersion, the blue end is more deviated. But, the effect is the same. Everything will have an coloured aura around it. You could have a very narrow band transmissive element, but then you'd only get a very narrow range of colours and angle.

On top of all these problems, aberrations are far worse for slight changes in the hologram than for a similar change for lenses. Let's say that the lens was off by 5 degrees or so, then a well corrected set of lenses would not show much aberrations. But, a hologram would, depending on the hologram. Unfortunately, not a lot seems to be written about holographic aberrations these days. I've been combing the literature for my ISDH paper. Also, these diffractive elements have to be calulated, there's no "holographic design software" such as Zemax or Code V. In fact, most of my day is spent in calculating these HOEs (or, to hew to the modern usage, DOEs) and then Joy sets up the table geometry as per my calculatons. Clients often ask me which software I use and seem amazed that I don't use software at all! I do about 20% of the initial calculations in my head and then the more detailed calculation on the back of any old scrap of paper.

Having said all that, there are areas where holograms have almost replaced conventional optics. Most spectrophotometers these days have a hologram as the dispersive element.

> PinkysBrain: How would you even record the equivalent of a compound lens in analogue even for a narrow bandwidth?Simply interfering a plane wave with a converging wave will give you a zone plate equivalent to a single lens easy enough ... but I don't quite see how to do it for a lens system (I'm not sure a thin hologram even has enough degrees of freedom to do it).

All you have to do is model, or recreate, the wavefronts. That is, treat the lens system as a black box with a specific transfer function, with a specific input. The transfer function applied the input will give you the wavefront for the output. You then create this output wavefront as the "object beam" and the input wavefront as the "reference beam". There are standar matrices for refraction and reflection, so for the real lens system - the black box - you construct the transformation matrices, multiply them and get a grand "system matrix". You apply this matrix to the input wavefront; so if the input is a spherical, diverging wave and the system matrix is M, the output waveform is:

|o> = M*exp(-ik.r)

Actually a single converging wave will not give a zone plate, but the output can be modelled as a zone plate. In a zone plate, you have a binary index system, with the nth ring being at a distance of n squared from the centre. That is, a zone plate is discontinuous. The Bragg planes (for a thick hologram) or the surface profile (for a thin hologram) have no such n-squared relationship. The coincidence/confusion arises because a zone plate can focus/the hologram focuses/therefore the hologram is a zone plate. However, this can also be said of a standard, glass thin lens: a glass thin lens focuses/a zone plate focuses/therefore a glass thin lens is a zone plate.

The whole idea that a "hologram" is a "zone plate" came about with the early theories of holography. At that time they needed a theoretical model for the hologram and decided to model it as a series of zone plates sitting on the plate surface with diffraction occurring purely in the Raman Nath regime. Thus, if a given wavefront impinged on the hologram (ie the reconstruction beam), the "zone plates" all across the plate, would "sample" different parts of the (reconstruction) wavefront and, depending on the size and density of the zone plates, would create a new (image) wavefront. The series of zone plates was a sort of theoretical black box. Since lens theory was fairly advanced and zone plates were well understood, a whole bunch of theoretical constructs could be drawn up. Thus, the earliest theories of holograms referred to the efficiency as a function of the MTF of a zone plate. However, a zone plate is not a lens, it doesn't refract light. A zone plate works by virtue of Fresnel diffraction and, by it's nature, Fresnel diffraction is a near-field diffraction phenomenon.

However, if the hologram consisted of real zone plates, there would have to be a one-to-one correspondance between the points on the objects and the zone plates on the hologram. This would defeat the meaning of the phrase "hologram", since then every point of the hologram would not contain the data pertinent to the entire object. Thus for a given a specific point on the object, there would correspond a specific zone plate at a speciic position on the hologram. In this situation, if you could "block off" a given zone plate (say by applying a black dot over a part of the plate) then the reconstructed image would be missing specific points.

1. What is a thin hologram?

1. Thin Hologram

First of all, a thin hologram doesn't necessarily have to be physically thin. Holograms with an emulsion thickness of 100's of microns can be thin while emulsions of 10's of microns can be classed as thick (more on this later). Basically, a thin hologram is defined such that (1) the diffraction is a surface phenomenon and therefore only two dimensions are required to describe it and (2) the diffraction is in the Raman Nath regime.

Taking the first of the above 2 definitions, the hologram, ie the diffractive structure, can be classified by a spatially varying complex amplitude function,

t(x,y) = |t(x,y)|exp(-iphi(x,y))

There are now two possibilities, extremes, if you will: (1) phi(x,y) is constant across the hologram and (2) |t(x,y)| = 1, ie transmissivity is uniform across the hologram. In a real hologram, of course, phi(x,y) will not be completely constant and transmissivity will not be completely uniform, there will be both some absorption some variations in density.

Taking the first condition, assume that the amplitude transmission is linearly proportional to the intensity of the interference pattern:

|t(x,y)| = t_0 + t_1 cos(kx)

where t_0 is the average transmission amplitude of the unrecorded film and k is the (recorded) angular spatial frequency (k= (2pi)/d) ). Since the transmissivity is limited to values between zero and one, the maximum diffraction occurs when

|t(x,y) = (1/2) + (1/2)cos(kx) = (1/2) + (1/4)exp(ikx) = (1/4)exp(-ikx)

As you can see, that maximum diffraction in each order is one quarter of the incident amplitude, and so, the maximum diffraction efficiency is, epsilon = (1/4)^2 = 1/16 = 0.0625, or about 6%. A real hologram is far below this because the maximum transmissivity is rarely one. Notice that there is only direction for the diffracted beam for a given spatial frequency.

Taking the second condition, assume that |t(x,y) = 1 and the hologram is completely encoded in the phase relationship:

t(x,y) = exp(-iphi(x,y))

Now, again, assume that the phase shift is proportional to the incident phase, phi(x,y) = phi_0 + phi_1cos(kx)

then

t(x,y) = exp(-iphi(x,y)exp[-i-iphi_1cos(kx)] (ignoring the phi_0)

In this case, the variability of the phase, the second factor, can now be expanded as a Fourier series to give a summation of Bessel functions of the nth order, J_n(phi_1). This kind of thin hologram will diffract into a large number of orders n, with the diffracted ampltude of the nth order being proportional to J_n(phi_1). Since the first order Bessel function has the highest value, then the diffraction efficiency is now proportional to the first order Bessel function and epsilon = (J_1)^2 = 0.339 or about 30%. This, again is a theoretical maximum, rarely achieved. Notice, there are many directions of diffracted beam for a given spatial frequency, each direction being dermined by the appropriate Bessel function.

The criteria for whether or not a hologram is actually thin comes from the Q parameter

Q = (2(pi)lambda_0d)/(nk)

where lambda_0 is the recording wavelength, d is the emulsion thickness, n is the refractive index and k is the spatial frequency. As you can see, by appropriate choices of lambda_0,n and k, you have a lot of versatility on the value of d. In other words, the actual physical thickness of the emulsion is but one factor in the definition of "thin".

Also, a thin hologram diffracts in the Raman Nath regime. In this kind of diffraction, diffraction occurs regardless of the direction of the input beam

2. Thin lens

A lens system is a refractive system, wherein the amount of refraction is dependant on the height above the axis (for an axially symmetric system). In other words, the phase delay is a function of the height above the axis. This is not always true, and is only true for a lens with spherical or conic profile - the phase delay of a cylindrical lens, for example, is in a plane perpendicur to the meridional plane. A lens system is defined by what are called the cardinal points of the system. These are: the principal planes, the nodal planes and the two focal lenghs. In general, there are two principal planes, two nodal planes and two focal lengths, making six cardinal points in all. The principal points are where the actual refraction occurs, the nodal planes are generally on ether side of the lens and are defines as those planes where the magnification of the Gaussian pairs is unity.

A thin lens is defined such that the principal and nodal planes are coincident. For a biconvex lens, theses planes are perpendicular to the axis and go through the centre of the lens, ie they cut the lens in two symmetrically. The focal points of the thin lens is given by the so-called Lens Makers Formula,

1/f = (n-1)(1/R_1 - 1/R_2)

where R_1 and R_2 are the radii of the faces of the lens. In addition, The sign convention is such that the face of first refraction (the left hand face) has it's centre of curvature in the image space and the face of the second refraction is negative when the centre of curvature is in the object space. This sign convention gives R_1 positive abnd R_2 negative for a biconvex lens and the other way around for a biconcave lens. There is also a relationship between the object's distance the lens (strictly the 1st principal plane) and the image's distance to the lens (strictly, the second principal plane), known as the thin lens formula,

1/v + 1/u = 1/f

Again, a sign convention applies such that anything to the left of the lens is positive and anything to the right is negative. In this way, it can be seen that a biconvex lens will take a narrow aperture collimated beam and expand it to a diverging beam (not a larger aperture collimated beam, no single lens will do this)

3a. What is/are the limitation of recording an HOE on a thin hologram?

It depends on whether it's an amplitude hologram or a phase hologram (see above). In a purely transmission hologram, there will be one order and it'll be only a few percent efficient. In a phase hologram, there will be many orders, with each one falling in efficiency. A real hologram has both amplitude characteristics and phase characteristics, so there will be a set of orders falling in efficiency, but not always according to J_n. However, as I mentioned above, a thin hologram doesn't have to be physically thin. You camn record a thin hologram on a 1 cm cube of lithium niobate! It all depends on the Q factor.

In addition, it also depends on the spatial frequency response of the material. If the HOE function requires a large spatial frequency, you have to chose an appropriate material that can take this higher frequency. This requires a calculation of the required set of frequencies appropriate to the function. The basic equation of diffraction in a thin hologram is the grating equation,

lambda = d[sin(theta) + sin(phi)]

> PinkysBrain: The USAF used one to compensate for the spherical aberration of an acrylic fresnel lens to achieve diffraction limited magnification .... but I don't quite see why the fresnel lens is necessary in the first place.

They're not specifically trying to correct a fresnel, per se. They give the fresnel as an example of the application of the technique at the end of the paper. They're trying to correct for the spherical aberration (SA) of a large-aperture lens system as in a microscope. In spherical aberration, the Gaussian image is formed when the input angle, the divergence angle of the light going into the lens (or the entrance pupil) is limited to about 0.5 degrees; more specifically, a Gaussian image is formed when Snell's Law {nsin(theta_1) = n'sin(theta_2)} is replaced by the approximation sin(theta)~theta. Now Snell's law becomes ntheta_1 = n'theta_2. As the angle going into the entrance pupil increases the Gaussian condition no longer holds and so there is a phase mismatch. The Gaussian condition is predicated on Fermat's Principle and is caused by the fact the lens is spherical (it needs to be a conic section as per Fermat's Principle), which causes SA. The system in this paper eliminates SA because in a diffractive system Fermat's Principle doesn't really apply. In diffraction, light is not propagated point-to-point through various media, but propagates via a mechanism of absorption and re-emission. Thus, Fermat's Principle, and so Gaussian optics, no longer holds and you can get a wide-field lens with no SA - in theory. Of course, the hologram itself must have no aberrations!
