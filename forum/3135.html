There are a number of ezperiments you can do.

You can try interferometry. I'm assuming you're aware of the basic holographic techniqure (set up, shoot, develop) and being a physics lab, I'm assuming you're aware of the need for high density plates since the interference fringes are about 2000 lp/mm. So, take an object that's uniformly diffuse and shoot it, but don't develop. Then place a weight on the object or heat it slightly, and reshoot on the same plate. The relative motion between the two states creates a "beat frequency" of lines that is characteristic of the translation. Most books on holography have a section on holographic interferometry.

You can try polarisation holography. Choose an object that you know depolarises to some extent (this is important). Then shoot the object from one direction with one particular state of polarisation of the reference beam and, simultaneously, from another direction with a reference polarised at right angles to the other reference's polarisation. Hence you have two references, say A and B. A and B are polarised at pi/2 relative to each other. Then reconstructing with A will show that part of the object that polarises one way and B will pick up that part of the object that polarises the other way. Some parts will record on both A and B but the relative brightnesses of these parts when reconstructed with A or B will show the ratio of depolarisations.

You can use the above experiment and incorporate a crystal into it. This will give the students an idea on crystal birefringence. Perhaps place the crystal in the reference beam and orientate the crytsal parallel to and perpendicul to the c axis.

As I've said before, you cannot make a hologram of a cloth doll or a paper mache object by simply leaning the plate on it and shooting it on a cutting board on your kitchen table. Experience and knowledge may give you an idea of how much stability and, more important, vibration isolation, is relevant to any particular object, but most newcomers don't yet have this experience. I feel that by giving the impression that you don't need stability and vibration isolation it's a bit of a "hobby killer" idea. If you're shooting in the wilds of a remote countryside with no traffic, it's very different from shooting in a busy city with lots of heavy vehicles constantly going by. The inner tubes etc is more for vibration isolation than stability and some environments need more vibration isolation than other environments. It's always a good idea to overemphasis the vibration isolation aspect in the beginning and let the budding holographer "feel" his/her way to knowing how much is enough with more experience.
