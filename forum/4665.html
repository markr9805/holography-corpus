[quote:5616801574="eric343"]Slits, hehe...

What is the difference between the pattern generated by 2- and 3-slits, other than the sharper definition of the maxima and minima? Assume nothing changes other than the number of slits.[/quote:5616801574]

Well I tried the intuitive approach. You have three ways of selecting two things from three if order doesn't matter. So if the slits are A, B, C then you have AB, AC, BC. Thus each pair of slits produces it's own set of sinusoidal fringes. However, these sinusoidal fringes also interfere with each other, so you have three sets of cross terms and three "DC" terms". However, so long as the amlitudes are the same (all the slits produces the same intensity of light) then the sum of sines is always a sin. Therefore I expect a sinusoidal variation modulated by a sinusoidal variation. You'l have a very bright background "dc" level, and a set of fringes whose intensity itself varies sinusoidally.

Mathematically, it's not that complicated. A two slit experiment gives two sources A and B. The sum is therefore

pattern = (A + B)(A + B)" = A^2 + B^2 + AB(cos<phase difference>)

where (A + B)" is the complex conjugate.

With three slits

pattern = (A + B + C)(A + B + C)" = A^2 + B^2 + C^2 +AB(cos<phase difference A,B>)

+AC(cos<phase difference A,C>)

+ BC(cos<phase difference B,C>)

Have I missed anything?

So the square terms will give the very strong background dc term and the sum of the three cos terms will give sinusoidal waves modulated by other sinusoidal terms.

Here's another stumper. Tilt the slits with respect to the observation screen

The laser output is not polairised. There's a Brewster window that polarises the output. A beam can be split simply by passing it through a partial mirror where the silvering is carefully controlled. Thus a 20% coating will reflect 20% of light and poass 80%. Polarising beamsplitters work on a different principle. They use the fact that some crytsals have an optic axis. This creates two indices of refraction. Thus in some directions, there's one index for one state of polarisation and another for the other polarisation state. Polarising beamsplitters are built as cubes from such crystals and arranged that, once split due to the difference in indices for the two states of polarisation, one polarisation goes one way and the other goes at right angles to it. Now you have two beams at right angles to each other with 90 degree shifts in polarisation. Some beamsplitters (non-polarising cubes) rely on what's called Frustrated Total Internal Reflection (FTIR) in which a beam of light travels along a surface has a component of the E field slightly above the surface. If another surface is brought into close contact with it, this E-field "leaks" into the other medium.

Dinesh Thu Mar 31, 2005 11:09 pm #22
[quote:fb47c39be7="JohnFlux"]So why not use a beam splitter then?

I have a quarterwave plate in front of it, so I can turn that to produce whatever ratio needed. I have another quarter wave plate on one of the beams followed by a polariser. The two together allows me to get whatever energy I want out of the two beams.[/quote:fb47c39be7]

Is this a solution to convert an unpolarised laser to a polarised laser, or a solution to control beam ratios at the splitter, ie a variable splitter? If the laser is unpolarised to begin with, a waveplate in front of the cube will not help. You'll simply get an unpolarised beam with the e-vector dancing about as much but lagging by an angle depending on the seting of the waveplate. The splitting ratio will also dance all over the place, following the variation of the input beam (since the splitting ratio is determined by the polariastion angle of the input which is constantly varying). If you now have a waveplate at the output of one beam, the intensity of that particular output wave will also dance all over the shop, but it's polarisation state will be constant and twisted by an angle determined by the waveplate.. You'll effectively have a beam splitter whose splittling ratio is constantly varying but whose output beams will be plane polarised. So, it'll certainly convert an unpolarised laser to a polarised one, but the beam ratio will vary quite fast, depending on the variation of polarisation of the input. To ensure the input polarisation, you'll need a polariser at the input. Of course, there's light loss due to the attenuation of the polariser.

If the laser is polarised to begin with, then this is a standard solution (but an expensive one!) to beam splitting. Varying the input waveplate varies the polarisation state and gives two output waves at right angles whose ratio is determined by the input polarisation and whose polarisation is at right angles to each other. As you say, you'll need a wave plate at one output so that you can re-orientate the polarisation.
