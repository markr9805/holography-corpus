Dinesh Fri Jun 10, 2011 10:45 am #2
Make an 'L' shape with two mirrors. If you suspect green sensitivity, cut a small piece of the film in a dark environment and place it on the base of the L. Then place a small coin over the film and zap the film with a green laser so the laser covers both arms of the L. If it is green sensitive, then you should get a grating everywhere except where the coin was. If it was not green sensitive, you won't get a grating at all. A small piece of film and a relatively heavy coin should provide enough stability for a weak grating.

Yes, I believe you're right, which is why I mentioned the difference between a "carrier" of sorts that acted as a "host" for actinic reactions, as opposed to actually being actinic in and of itself. Photoresist, for example, is actinic in and of itself, while gelatin is not.

> PinkysBrain: As far as the wet development, I think the PVA solution needs wet development ... that's why I asked what the advantage is. UV development ala Sergio's solution seems so much easier.

I may have misunderstood the question. If you're asking why dry development has an advantage over wet development, it's primarily because of swelling. Wet development changes the physical structure of the emulsion, whereas dry development just changes the phase relationships in the emulsion, not the physical structure. So, in wet development, swelling causes a shift in reconstruction angles for transmission holograms and a colour shift in reflection. Also, it creates bandwidth. Dry development causes no shift in wavelength and has very low bandwidths. In effect, dry development is better in one way for colour work, where swelling is an important factor. However, the lack of bandwidth may be a problem for colour because the human visual system reacts to bandwidth almost as much as it reacts to wavelength. Your eye cannot see very low bandwidths and interprets colour by bandwidth as much as central frequency.

Another effect may be brightness, since, as I said above, bandwidth is an important factor in the total luminosity of a hologram. So, the efficiency of a dry-developed hologram may be high, but still be visually dim because the bandwidth is too low. In this case, the material is very good for HOEs. Of course, another advantage of high efficiency/low bandwidth is in a master-copy setup. In such a system, high efficiency/low bandwidth is very advantageous, so long as the copy medium has a high bandwidth.

Dinesh Tue Jul 19, 2011 11:28 am #21
> Martin: The key seems to be to apply them greatly diluted compared to "photographic" use.

In my ongoing (sometimes ongoing!) mission to find a 'natural' red-sensitive dichromate, ie replacing the dichromate with something that is red sensitive and has a higher oxidation state that can be reduced actinicely (no dyes), I tried permanganate. Since the stuff is purple, I reasoned it must be red sensitive and it certainly has higher oxidation states. But, I didn't know if the higher states could be reduced actinicely. So, not knowing the proportions, I added the same amount of permanganate as I would have added dichromate. When the film dried, I saw specks of permanganate coming out of solution on the plate! Clearly I'd added too much! Anyway, I repeated the experiment adding a tiny fraction of the original amount. The result was that I did get a photo-reduction with permanganate, but not at 633. It gave a not-so-great, so-so image at 532, but dcg is already sensitive at 532 so I didn't think I'd achieved anything. But, yes, the amount of permanganate is small compared to the amount of dichromate.

> dannybee: being that the permanganate is purple Or vilot i would think that would put it into the iR range

No. At, least, not in terms of observed colour.The colour of an object is what's "thrown out" after it's absorbed some of the spectrum. Permanganate is purple, so I assumed that "not purple" would be red. I forgot that purple is a mix of red and blue, so it may have been absorbing green and "throwing out" red and blue. That's not to say that it doesn't absorb in the IR, but it's not valid to say that it must absorb in IR because it's coloured purple.

I had talked about permanganate as a possible for a red sensitive "dcg", since I thought it was absorbing red. I even thought of haemoglobin as a red sensitive additive, but that's another story; I haven't bled for holography yet! I was in India while Joy was still here. She had to coat some plates for a client and asked me if it was OK to try and coat a few plates with permanganate. She asked how much and I said try the usual amount of dichromate. Anyway, she told me that there was a purplish crust over the whole plate but she said she'd shoot them anyway. A few days later, while I was still in India, she emailed that there was nothing in red, but there was something - not much of something, but something - at 532. I asked her what made her try 532 and she said that when 633 didn't work out, she tried 457, 514 and 532! I would not have bothered. I'd have just said, "Oh well, it doesn't work at 633" and let it go at that. When I got back from India and saw the purple crust, I realised I'd have to go down to about 1/10th of the amount of permanganate she'd used. This time I got about 20% odd efficiency with 532.
