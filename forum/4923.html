It's been said that soft emulsions are needed for SHSG, and I'm wondering if this is really true.

The idea behind SHSG is that soft areas of gelatin need to be established in exposed areas, and hard areas in unexposed areas. Or, alternately, the reverse of this (with reversal bleaching).

As shown by Usanov and co-workers, the gelatin which is in contact with, and chemically adsorbed to, the silver halide (or silver) grains will resist the hardening effect of formaldehyde (or another hardening agent). So the emulsion will get hard except for those adsorbed areas. Then the plate can be processed like DCG. Say for example I make a reversal-bleached reflection hologram on Slavich material. Then I place it in formaldehyde for a period of time. The gelatin will get hard, except for those areas of the gelatin molecules which are in contact with (and adsorbed to) the silver halide grains. Then, the emulsion is fixed to remove the silver halide. At this point I will have an emulsion which is hard in areas that had no silver halide, and soft in areas that previously had silver halide. Then it can be dehydrated or "shock dried" just like DCG.

I was always under the impression that emulsions which were already hardened, like Agfa, BB, or the Gentet material, were unsuitable for SHSG, and I suppose this would be true if the gelatin were hardened prior to precipitation of the silver halide. But if the emulsion is hardened after precipitation, wouldn't the adsorbed gelatin resist the hardening agent? I've soaked Slavich material in formaldehyde for 16 hours, and the adsorbed portion remained soft. The adsorbed areas really do have a strong resistance to the hardening effect of Formalin.

The only areas of the emulsion that need to remain soft are those areas which are adsorbed to the silver halide grains. If these areas are soft, then the emulsion would be suitable for SHSG. I don't know the manufacturing methods for commercial material, but I wonder if they are hardened after precipitation? If so, then perhaps the gelatin which is adsorbed to the silver halide grains would remain soft through the hardening procedure, making the emulsion suitable for SHSG.

There are actually more than two mechanisms. The original mechanism was the chemical reduction of the CR2O7 to Cr3+ rather than the photolytic reduction in standard DCG. In this case:

6Ag + (Cr2 O7--) + (14H+) -> 6Ag+ + 2Cr3+ + 7H2O

The reduced chromic ion then enhances cross-linking in the standard way. In this way, the anti-nodes , as described by Jeff, would be harder. So, effectively, you develop, bleach with a tanning bleach and fix. Then you process as per DCG.

Hariharan studied the use of a non-tanning bleach and a non-tanning develper in order to test the theory that tanning could be accomplished by the developer (as Ed as stated). He also used an effect discovered by Tull, where a non-tanning developer also can tan to some extent. The idea being that the oxidised products of the development process attach at one end to the developed silver and to an oxidiser at the other end. This apparently worked and Hariharan succesfully made SHSG with both dev and bleach being non tanning. Usanov's methods also include bleaching before hardening.

Apparently you need a developer with a pretty high gamma, which is why efficencies tend to be on the low side. I've used AAC with dichromate bleach and got reasonably succesful results. Like Ed, I got holograms with eta's roughly on a par with silver. Also bear in mind that the delta n does not need to be very high. A delta n of roughly (10)(-1) will give pretty good results. Thus, the problem could be excess tanning, rather than not enough, causing non-linearities. Noise is also a big problem with these techniques.

In anyone's interested, here's the method I used for SHSG.

1. Expose PFG-03M (no pre-treatments)

2. Develop in D-20 for 1 minute (this is just D-19 with an extra 15 grams of potassium bromide per liter). I exposed enough so that the emulsion would "go dark" in 1 minute. The density wasn't measured.

3. Wash

4. Bleach until clear in: 0.6 gram potassium chromate, 7 grams sodium hydrogen sulfate, 1 liter water. This is a dilute (and slow) reversal bleach suggested by Martin. The idea is to avoid removing silver halide from the soft PFG-03.

5. Wash. Room lighting was used for all the other steps.

6. At this point I dried the holograms, because I wanted to see if I had a decent image before proceeding further.

7. Soak in cold Formalin overnight (at about 4 degrees C). I should stress the health hazards of formaldehyde gas: I did this soaking in a separate building in a small refrigerator. As a matter of fact, I recently had a shed built in my backyard so that the formaldehyde soaking step could be done in a building completely separated from my lab. Incidentally, the vapor from the Formalin quickly hardened the flexible gasket in the refrigerator.

8. Wash

9. Soak in 18% sodium thiosulphate for 3 minutes

10. Wash

11. Soak in 60% isopropyl alcohol for 2 minutes

12. Soak in 100% isopropyl alcohol for 2 minutes

13. Soak in 100% isopropyl alcohol at 75 degrees C for 1 minute

14. Blast dry with hot air from a hair dryer

15. Immediately place hologram in a warmer (oven or toaster oven) for at least an hour. As soon as the hologram is pulled from the warmer, cement a cover plate to the hologram using optical adhesive.

A few notes: this procedure worked, but the brightness of the final SHSG hologram will follow the brightness of the original silver halide hologram. In other words, you can't take a lackluster hologram and expect to transform it into a brilliant one. Still, the Russians said they were getting diffraction efficiencies in the 80 to 90 range using this method. My problem was that I was having trouble producing good holograms to begin with.

It wasn't necessary at the time (5 years ago) to use cold solutions on PFG-03M. Now that I'm trying to improve the first part of the process using PFG-03C, I've found that cold solutions are required. I've done a few split beam holograms, but haven't been able to produce any nice, bright ones yet with the above chemistry (following the procedure given in the Russian papers). At this point I'm wondering if I should try a different developer/bleach combination, since I have a feeling that the only real prerequisite is that a good, bright hologram be obtained in the first part of the process (and keeping the emulsion soft until it goes in the Formalin). This method (MC-3) is based on reversal bleaching, but the others (MC-1 and MC-2) involve different chemistries.

There are five papers in English that I know of by the Russsian group, the two mentioned previously in this thread in Optics and Spectroscopy, two in SPIE 1238, and one in SPIE 2043.

I've been doing some tests on PFG-03C trying to improve the quality of the holograms made during the first part of the Russian SHSG method.

Among the three methods described in the papers (MC-1, MC-2, and MC-3), the last one was reported as giving the highest diffraction efficiencies (80@633, 85@515, and 90@476). The thing that separates MC-3 from the others is that the hardening step is done upon gelatin with silver halide grains that were in the emulsion originally. That is, a standard reversal bleach procedure was used to make the hologram. In the others, the gelatin was adsorbed to newly-formed grains (developed-up silver in MC-1, or rehalogenated silver in MC-2). My assumption is that the process works better on grains which were established when the emulsion was originally made. In any event, I'm working on MC-3, to which the following comments apply.

The question is how to produce optimum quality holograms on PFG-03C using a reversal bleach. So far, I've made some fairly decent ones, but I'm sure they could be much better. It's possible that I'm removing silver halide somewhere in the process. The papers recommend "D-20" which is D-19 with an extra 15 grams of potassium bromide per liter. The reason for the addition of the bromide was to reduce the silver halide solvent effect of D-19, as was stated in Optics and Spectroscopy(1991), pages 377-9. I use diluted D-20 (4 parts water, 1 part D-20). The bleach I use is 0.6 gram potassium chromate, 7 grams sodium hydrogen sulfate, and 1 liter water. I expose, develop to a density of about 1 (in ~2 minutes), wash for ~5 minutes in a cup of distilled water with magnetic stirring, and bleach for ~8 minutes until clear. Then wash under running tap water, rinse in distilled, and dry.

I think that the D-19 type of developer and subsequent reversal bleach might be a questionable way of producing bright holograms on PFG-03. There is an interesting chart on page 378 of the above paper. For a hologram processed with D-19 (not D-20) and bleached (apparently with rehalogenating bleaches, however), the reported diffraction efficiencies are only 22 and 25. And, Slavich says that PFG-03C can produce >45@633 (processing method not specified). Saying "greater than 45" seems to imply that much greater diffraction efficiencies should not be expected.

Moisture sensitivity is an important issue with SHSG (and DCG), and I would like to make some comments. (This subject was also extensively discussed in another thread.)

Concurrently with any SHSG efforts, including the various processing methods, etc., it is really a necessity that the problem of moisture sensitivity be studied, and that some (at least partial) conclusions be reached.

One of the main reasons for the interest in SHSG is the lack of "printout" which is part of the pemanence issue. However, all of the effort on SHSG will be wasted unless the holograms are protected from water vapor, which will cause them to vanish (as was quickly evident in my experiments). There are some possible exceptions to this rule, none of which I have experienced myself. For example, Jeff Blyth once stated that by keeping a DCG hologram in an exremely dry state for a period of days, the hologram will be stable unless it makes contact with liquid water. Also, methods have been introduced to make DCG holograms more resistant to water vapor.

All of these exceptions make me uneasy as a holographer, and for my own work, I'm going to insist that my holograms be sealed from the ambient environment, using the best technique I can find.

The only SHSG hologram that I saved from my tests five years ago has half-vanished due to a poor sealing technique (I simply poured optical adhesive on top, then applied a glass cover plate). At the time, however, I wasn't trying to produce "finished" holograms, just trying to find out if I could get the SHSG process to work.

So, if anyone is interested in SHSG, I would recommend that the sealing process be part of the plan from the start. As for myself, I've been doing substantial work on this. I sealed a "humidity test strip" between two pieces of glass, in the same way (and with the same amount of sealant) that I plan to use on the holograms. After six months in a bucket of water, the humidity card was unchanged, in the 20 to 30% range. Then it underwent numerous freeze/thaw cycles in a refrigerator that was hooked up to a timer (which shut off/turned on the refrigerator at the appropriate times). The seal still held, and continues to hold, with the indicator showing the same reading. I've never heard of this sealant being used for holograms, but it's a natural material that I chose due to its unique properties. I'm not in a position to disclose it at the present time, but I mention it because it shows how seriously I take the issue. Perhaps others can find materials/techniques which are better (either generally or for their specific purposes).

Before someone says that I'm going overboard, and that their DCG sealing method works perfectly well for their purposes, I will say this: if it works well for you, and you're happy with it, then there is no reason to consider anything else.

Perhaps my needs are unique to myself, but I've experienced too many failures when optical adhesive was simply poured on top, and a cover plate pressed on. Now, if the gelatin was scraped away from say 5mm from each of the four edges of the glass, then the adhesive applied and the cover plate pressed on top, then this would be much, much better, and the hologram may have a long life. Of course, you can take practically any hologram, control ambient conditions accordingly, and have the hologram last a long time, perhaps indefinitely. Likewise, if (as chance would have it) the hologram is exposed to the right kind of conditions during its lifetime (in this case, mainly low humidity) the hologram may survive, possibly for a long time. But, in all probability, conditions will change, perhaps rapidly.

Basically, what I want to say is this: Sealing is a crucial issue with SHSG. Whatever sealing technique is used, if is expected to protect the hologram for a long period of time, then it should be investigated, tested, and refined. Perhaps the greatest amount of emphasis should be placed on this part of the process.

Of course, the reported diffraction efficiencies are impressive: 96.3@458, 96.5@532, and 96.8@647.

By comparison, the highest DE's in the Russian work were: 80@633, 85@515, and 90@476.

Taking a look at the Bjelkhagen processing schedule, shown earlier in this this thread, it's clear that it's considerably more complicated than the Russian one, and more difficult to understand. In the Bjelkhagen work, the hardening of the gelatin appears to be done on no less than three occasions, while in the Russian MC-3, the only hardening occurs during the Formalin soak.

For me, the confusion rests with what Bjelkhagen means by "hardening of the emulsion surface."

I will quote the entire paragraph for clarity.

"However, most important was the application of a process introduced by Usanov and Shevtsov. After the two-step bleach and selective hardening process is completed, additional surface emulsion hardening is required. This can be performed by drying the emulsion in ethanol and then exposing the dry emulsion to formaldehyde in a heated, sealed container (a technique introduced by Usanov). In this case the hardening process can be performed in 15-30 min. What is important here is the extensive hardening of the emulsion surface that occurs during the dry-hardening step. The hard surface allows a high vapor pressure to build up inside the microcavities during the final dehydration step. Under this pressure the microvoids expand and isopropanol is replaced by air, thus providing the high-refractive-index variations in the emulsion."

I am going to assert that this statement simply refers to a "uniform hardening" agent applied to the emulsion, and that it could be either in the gas or liquid form. This uniform hardening agent was the key factor in the Russian method, described years earlier. The "surface" simply being the entire volume of the emulsion, except for areas adsorbed to silver/silver halide grains. So in other words, I don't think Bjelkhagen is referring to the first couple microns or whatever. Consider the following paragraph, published 12 years earlier by Usanov and Shevtsov in Optics and Spectroscopy (1990):

"The isophase surfaces of the SHG holograms have a discrete structure. In order to achieve the same variation in the refractive index per unit volume as in the DCG holograms it is necessary to produce micropores of rather significant size without permitting such micropores to form in the unexposed regions of the interference grating. This can be achieved by using isopropyl alcohol heated over 60 C to dehydrate the gelatin in the photosensitive layer of the hologram. We know that the boiling point of an aqueous alcohol solution at alcohol concentrations exceeding 50% is below that of pure isopropanol. When submerged into the alcohol the surface of the gelatin layer that has swollen in the aqueous alcohol solution is dewatered and exchange between the layer and the alcohol ceases. An elevated alcohol-water vapor pressure is produced in the layer, which serves to increase the size of the swelled micropores. When the treated photographic material is removed from the hot alcohol, the latter evaporates and the gelatin rapidly dehydrates, conserving to the maximum possible degree the inhomogeneous structure of the swelled layer of the SHG hologram."

Basically, I think Bjelkhagen is simply re-stating the Russian microcavity theory. I don't think so-called "surface hardening" is exclusively due to the formaldehyde vapor, as opposed the liquid bath. However, apparently there is some advantage to using a vapor, but the reason is still unclear to me.
