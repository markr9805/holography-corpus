I have been trying to replace an argon laser with a semiconductor laser for my two beam (one direct and one bounced from a mirror) interference system which is used to generate gratings. The exposure time increases drastically due to the reduced laser power, but the interference pattern looks ok. The only problem is that some periodical intensity modulation on top of the grating can be observed. I tried to eliminate any possible reflection surfaces that might be in the pathway to reduce this modulation, but no help. I am starting to think if it might be something inherited from the semiconductor laser itself or maybe it is the polarization orientation of the laser the cause of this problem?

I would appreciate if someone can share any thought or experience on it.

Thanks,
Ed Wesly Mon Mar 11, 2013 10:48 am #2
Some quick thoughts, without seeing the set up and results.

How are you seeing the fringes? Looking at them with a beam combiner of some sort, like one used in a fringe stabilizer set up? Then a separate set of fringes could be generated by the combiner, and not necessarily in the hologram.

Or are you looking at the fringes in the hologram under a microscope? Fringes could be generated by internal reflections in the holographic plate; are you using an anti-halation backing of some sort, like black paint on the back to eliminate internal back reflections? Or is it possible that light is coming into the plate from its edge, and internally ringing?

Pictures of the phenomena would be very helpful!

MilanKarakas Wed Mar 13, 2013 4:54 am #7
> Feng: The fringes are seen on the recording media. It is very much like the curved modulation at the edge of the holo in Milan's photo in his link. I didn't see those parallel lines though. so you think it is from the reflection of the back surface of the mirror?

Right now I inspected my grating and I am pretty sure that both lines ("modulated" and narrow lines) are due to lack of antihalation backing. I used primary beam at almost incidence angle, while reflected beam is close to 45 degrees. First one is responsible for narrow lines, while second ones (reflected from the mirror at 45 deg.) is responsible for almost sinusoidal modulation. And I believe that two sets of back reflections combines and produce constructive and destructive interferences on emulsion on the first surface.

Next time I will consider to use one of two methods to suppress back reflection from the plate; matching fluid (it can be done by mixing vegetable and mineral oil in some proportions to match index of refraction of the glass substrate), and black clothing just secured close to the back of the plate. Before process, it will be good to clean this oil, so that it does not contaminate water and alcohol baths (for DCG). Second option is to apply additional layer of the gelatin with some absorptive dye, which will be removed after exposure and before water/alc. bath processing. Or, in the worst case (that dye is hard to wash out), just removing whole emulsion from the back after exposure by warm water and dilute NaOH.

I don't know whether is possible to use some black coat (paint) which may be removed by acetone before water/alc. bath processing? Or some water soluble paint which does not reflect recording wavelength, and removed by water before processing?

Ed Wesly Wed Mar 13, 2013 7:01 am #8
When I worked at CFC/Northern Banknote we used to use a brand of spray paint called Krylon, and they had acrylic based enamels, which we could remove with a water soaked paper towel. When I went to get some more not too long ago, I found out that it is no longer made.

So I tried, and was successful with, was black tempera paint. It stuck to the glass, and after soaking with a wet paper towel it came off quite easily! I would recommend this over all the other methods mentioned above.
