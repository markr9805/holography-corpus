Has anyone thought of using the photographic "Liquid Light" product to make hologram plates?

I know that grain size and emulsion speed are inversely related. I believe I've read (in various photography forums) that standard 100 ASA film can record around 200 lines per mm. "Liquid Light" is an emulsion that is applied to various surfaces for artistic purposes. It is exposed using an enlarger and then developed the same way as normal paper. It has an effective ASA of around 5, so I would think it would be significantly higher in resolution than regular film. If the relationship between ASA and resolution were linear (unknown if this is the case), this emulsion might have a resolution of thousands of lines per mm. The emulsion does have some sensitivity to red light but I believe peak sensitivity is to green light. So if the resolution was suitable a green laser pointer might be used. Probably only transmission holograms could be made since the emulsion is not transparent. "Liquid Light" is available for about $2 an ounce. I would think an ounce could coat quite a few plates.

Anyway, I was curious if anyone had tried this or has any technical comments. I may be totally "all wet" here but it was just a thought by someone familiar (somewhat) with photographic processes but new to holography.

No Frank you cannot do it like that!

With holo recording material you are dealing with grains at least a thousand times smaller than what you would have produced for B/W photographic emulsions. There is NO intrinsic sensitivity to 532nm green.

The intrinsic sensitivity is well into the UV. [I have managed to make UNdyed good reflection gratings with a frequency tripled YAG at 355nm and then replayed them underwater to give a green replay. ]

Also the dyes I recommended using eg the pinacyanol for 633nm are wonderful graingrowth inhibitors . Unlike your photoemulsion experience

where you want grain growth to some degree in a "ripening process" to get your photosensitivity, we need to STOP that happening. So in the Ag diffusion system the dye needs to go in at the start of the birth of the grain. If you want to make Lippmann emulsion by the "conventional" method (eg H. Thiry paper) then again you need to add pinacyanol to the

liquified gelatin as a grain growth inhibitor.

A number of alternative dyes used as good sensitizers usually contain unsaturated sulfur atoms (usually they have a "thia" in their name. This

can change them from being grain growth inhibitors to grain growth promoters .... unfortunately.

At this level, you need to take the QM approach which kind of gets a little counter-intuitive (as does everything when you take QM into account!). The macroscopic exposure of an emulsion is determined by wave theory - which is not statistical in nature - while the microscopic mechanism by which the light "does it's work" is determined by a photon model - which is statistical in nature. In this case, since the interaction energy is probabilistically quantised, only some photons with certain energy levels will cause absorption and most photons with other energies will simply result in non-radiative oscillations - phonons, rather than photons. However, you also have Rayleigh scattering, which is a (non-statistical) wave phenomenon and also depends on the wavelength. Hence you have the situation where the AgX is most susceptible to actinic reaction by a (some) "blue" photon(s) while simultaneously the "blue" light is most susceptible to scattering by the AgX due to the size of the molecule and the 4th power law of Rayleigh scattering.

You can sort of model it like shooting food pellets at an ant. The food has to be about the size of the ant or smaller. However as the food pellets are shooting past the ant, the ones that are a little too big simply hit the ant and cause it to shudder, the ones that are a little too too small also hit it and cause the ant to shudder a little less while the ones that are "just right" are nabbed by the ant and eaten. However, the ant can't grab all the food pellets of the right dimension because they're rushing past too quickly and so it misses some of them, which then hit it and cause it to shudder. If, by accident, you shoot a ginormous food pellet which actually strikes the ant with full force, the ant disintegrates! However, there's a twist. The food pellets, after being ejected by the "food gun", don't go in a straight line. Their path "wiggles" spontaneously.
