Ok, take me out back and beat me, but I have to state what I feel is going on with DCG. This is not something I have read but is a concept derived from many readings, many experiments, contributions from this forum and many batches of DCG. Most of what is written may be incorrect but it is just a description of facts. Some times conclusions based on facts are wrong but…..

Please feel free to correct any mis-use of words.

Gelatin contains triple helix spirals made from polypeptide chains. When the gelatin is heated in water the some of the triple helix breaks into loose polypeptide chains, optimally most break into these chains. The plate is then coated. As the gelatin cools different things can happen. The chains can remain loose in their singularity state, the chains can remain loose but attach to other chains at just points or the chains can recombine to reform the triple helixes. I believe this is where the drying (curing) parameters of humidity, temperature and thus time, play a most important role.

If the emulsion is exposed to too high an ambient humidity when curing then the gelatin does not set up quick enough (does not dry fast enough) and more chains form into multiple attachments and triple helices while curling and moving. This leaves fewer chains ready to be attached by the exposure process. The amount of humidity and the size of the plate and the thickness of the emulsion all contribute to areas that can have more bound chains (near the inside of the plate where drying is slower, more overall bonding – denser concentration of cross-linked chains at swelling – more constriction at drying – shorter wavelength – less efficiency) and areas that have less bound chains (near the outside of the plate where drying is faster and chains locked into ready state). I believe this is where one avenue of greenies comes from. I have clearly seen this in multiple trials in high humidity environment emulsion curing with the outside of the plate exhibiting no greening and all the greenies are concentrated in the centers of the plate.

If the emulsion is cured in a low humidity environment then the emulsion cures faster thus locking more chains in the unbound, “straightened” and ready to be bound (cross-linked) state. I also believe this would be a good way of storing plates, in a very dry environment.

If the emulsion is kept in too warm an environment then the water molecules (thus the fluid state) moves faster, narrowing gaps between chains, unbound free chains or areas of chains curling causing bonding and desensitizing the plates. This could be backed up with the knowledge that DCG plates keep longer in refrigeration.

Now, if the plate is exposed in this dry cool condition the chains are less able to cross-link as the chains are being held very rigidity. The chains are only about 1 nm in width and a couple hundred nm long and separations can be close enough or too far away for bonding (cross-linking). But if the emulsion is brought into a warmer and higher humidity environment just before exposure but enough time to allow settling (temperature and humidity equalized with ambient temperature and humidity) of the plate, I believe the plate will be at maximum sensitivity. The emulsion can be more fluid, the straight chains curl or move toward the bounding areas which brings more chains into close enough proximity so that more bonding in those areas can happen.

This leads to the conclusion that the emulsion is always in a “fluid” state. That is, even if the plates are not exposed to light the chains move and bond with one another. The higher the humidity and temperature the faster the bonding of these chains.

My next set of experiments is to make a batch of DCG and coat plates. One set of coated plates will be cured in a dry and cool environment and another set of plates will be cured and dried in a humid and warm environment. Then for the plates cured in the dry and cool environment, expose some dry and cool and expose some humid and warm.

Actually John I don't think you can get anything meaningful out of measuring the pH of distilled water,

It can swing about wildly with tiniest amounts of CO2 from the air or traces of alkalinity from freshly washed glassware and if you are using a pH meter stick it will depend on whatever the last seriously acid or alkaline solution it was dipped in. One needs to have a level of ions present to act as a buffer to override the ambient “noise”.

Incidentally I have no idea what an “alkalinity of 40” means..

Your “well water” you describe as having a pH ~6 suggests if it’s right that you are in a soft water region is that right? In which case it may be worth getting a bit of blackboard chalk or lime to test the effect of Ca++ . It can be useful in the silver halide/gelatin system to have the gelatin coating toughened up a little with a small amount of Ca++ from a “weak” acid salt such as carbonate or bicarbonate as if you are living in a "hard" water area as I am. The calcium salt of a strong acid may not be so effective and on that point I am not clear what subtleties are going on with Colin’s use of gypsum CaSO4 or Epsom salt MgSO4 , in his brewing business , it may be to do with the biology of yeasts. Perhaps my microbiologist colleague here Colin Davidson could put in a brief note

next week.

The Ca ++ acts by combining ionically with 2 adjacent carboxylic acid groups along the strand of amino acids and causes kinking up at that point. (The slight alkalinity enables the amino groups to be displaced easily by the Ca++ to form NH2). This is a readily reversible effect that can be reversed by plenty of water rinsing or just a small acid bath.and is nothing like as strong as the virtually irreversible Cr+++ bond to carboxylic groups from the dichromate system or from chrome alum hardening.. So the kink effect causes the contraction and possible green shift in the diffraction I don't think it is to do with a plain loss of "bulk" of material as the levels we are talking about are so low.
