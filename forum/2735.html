Actually, it is a photograph. It's a photograph of the interference pattern. What's different is not the imaging properties, but the way light interacts with the photograph. In the case of a photograph, light simply reflects off the medium and the reflected light carries with it the intensity variations on the photograph. If the photo is in colour and the "reconstruction light" has colour components, the reflected light carries colour information too. If you see a colour photo in laser light,you see it in only one colour. In a hologram, the light interacts with the photographically encoded fringes by diffraction. You can still "see" the "photograph" with a microscope, but without magnifying optics, the diffraction effect overwhelms the reflective effect of seeing the fringes.

All this may sem a bit pedantic, but it explains some of the aspects of recording a hologram that's simple expected to be accepted when the subject is taught. The books, and the teachers, simply say that you need exceptional stability. They never say why you need this. Students simply accept that you need exceptional stability, they never seem to ask why ("the book said so"!). Basically. like any photograph, the subject of the photograph cannot move or you'll get blur. The faster a subject is expected to move the faster the film required to capture it. You'd never try going to Le Mans or Dayton with an 800 ASA film. In the case of fringes, any blurring of the fringes causes divergence from the sinusoidal requirement of the fringe profile and you get less light diffracting where you want it to diffract.
