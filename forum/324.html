Dinesh Thu Sep 22, 2011 11:40 am #34
Why the ground glass? Point 4 on p6 explains - to spread it out over the plate. In other words, they seem to be using the ground glass as a slow lens to spread it out even more. This may not be the best way to do it, but this was written probably 60's or early 70's and it was written for the complete beginners, so they may well have sacrificed technique for simplicity or availability. In the diagram on p 7, notice that the lens L1 does not expand the beam very much and the mirror M3 is also very small. Newport may well have tried to keep costs down for the beginner (especially in this time period these components were very expensive for the times) and so used small mirrors and weak lenses. In this case, using a ground glass screen to slightly expand the beam to fill the plate was a matter of convenience - a cheap and simple solution to the problem of filling the plate.

Why focus the beam? Again, they probably did it to be cheap and cheerful. I suspect they never actually did this, simply worked out how it should be done. In this case, the object beam should carry the image data onto the plate. What better way than to code the beam - modulate it - by sending the object beam right through the image source? This would certainly have amplitude-modulated the object beam and they may have thought that there was no point in phase modulating, since the transparency had no thickness. It was probably a thought experiment, "modulated" by the need for low cost and simplicity. What they may not have realised (or thought about) was that a clear area would have allowed the beam to pass right through with no modulation at all. In this case, you'd get just a simple grating. For the beginner, seeing a spot of colour spread in some parts of the plate might not have been a great-ting (sorry!)

> holorefugee: If your object beam is randomly polarized then randomizing your reference beam seems to make sense.

If you mean that ground glass randomly polarises a polarised beam, then it's not so, depending on the ground glass. Ground glass scatters the light, throwing it in different directions, and so alters thephase randomly, but not the polarisation. If the light bounces around inside the ground glass, hits the back surface and is emitted out of the front, then the polarisation will be altered. However, the reflectivity of back-scattered light is pretty low, so the proportion of light that's been depolarised is small, unless the ground glass has an appreciable thickness. Of course, you could have a non-linear ground glass! (Anyone writing a patent for non-linear ground glass owes me a beer!)

Ed Wesly Fri Sep 30, 2011 12:48 pm #67
As far as where to focus the image plane in a stereogram, I will use the above-mentioned CFC/Northern Banknote stereogram set as an example.

The goal of the equipment was to produce a three-registered color white light transmission image plane hologram on photo-resist for embossing. It was bullet proof engineered to do that on a daily basis.

The master transmission hologram was not parallel to the screen, but saw it tipped at the alpha angle as Benton called it, or the Benton angle as others may call it, or the tip angle. This was critical, and it was well calculated and implemented.

The reference beam was not only collimated as perfectly as can be, with the shallowest possible angle bouncing off telescope mirrors of the proper diameter to fill the 8 by 10 inch master, but normal to the plane of the plate. Some of the Benton and Suzanne St. Cyr papers mention the reference angle should be symmetric to the tip angle to make the fringes perpendicular to the hologram plane, so that image distortion is minimized by any emulsion shrinkage.

But by utilizing processing that does not shrink the emulsion you can get away with the normal reference beam. This has many advantages, like lowering the spatial frequency requirement, more efficient power to the plate, (no bloody cosine attenuation, you non-believers!), more compact geometry, and you can always find the reconstruction angle by the back reflection.

This master was exposed in three vertical strips for color separation, with 50 horizontal strips of image frames for a total of 150 images. When the real image was flushed out in the transfer step, the H2 was placed the exact distance from the H1 as the screen had been in the recording. This was critical, as the colors from the 3 slits would not come to the same focus as any other distance.

The whole H1 was illuminated at the transfer step, but masked with a piece of cardboard with slits of the appropriate height and position as determined by the "Benton math".

You could see the RGB pixels with a magnifying glass overlap each other. This was where the set up was focused; image depth was totally controlled by the stereopsis of the imagery. Trying to hedge the focus of the image would only lead to disaster, since the colors were out of register.

So this is the long answer to John's question. With your dual viewpoint stereogram described above and done in single color reflection I am guessing you could probably play with the real holographic image focus, but if your reference beams are not collimated, you might find that there is a significant difference between horizontal and vertical focus, a problem we faced in the 16mm film based stereogram at SAIC. But that's another story.

My bottom line would be find the sharpest possible image of the screen, and transfer to that, and take care of the 3D effects in the software.
