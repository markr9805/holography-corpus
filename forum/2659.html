Kaveh Mon Aug 09, 2004 6:43 am #4
As Bob says, we are not poo-pooing Brewster. But here is my take:

You want to reduce reflections between surfaces, so as not to get woodgrain. So you should make sure your reference beams are p-polarized. In this way, you will always get less reflection than s-polarized light.

Now, if you want to be sure you get no reflection, then you should use p, and come in at the Brewster angle.

But holography is a compromise, and always having to use Brewster is limiting. So, we can use other angles, knowing that at least we are getting less than we would with s polarization.

Look at the graph showing the reflectivity of the two states:

![Image](http://www.bazargan.org/Brewster.gif)

At zero incidence, around 4% of all light is reflected. As we increase the angle of incidence, the reflected portion of the s light, dashed curve, steadily increases. But p decreases first, going to zero at Brewster's angle, and then increases.

Now take 45 degrees. With s, you get around, say, 8% of the light reflected. With p, only around 1%. So you have dramatically reduced the unwanted reflection beams, and not used Brewster.
Dinesh Mon Aug 09, 2004 1:11 pm #5
Firstly, I agree with Bob and Kaveh. There's no absolute necessity to use brewster's angle. Some books (and well-known holographers!) tell you that you have to use Brewster's angle which they say is 60 degrees, without telling you why. This just adds to some concept that the whole thing is somehow formulaic. Newcomers are made to feel that there's some dogmatic ritual that's absolutely necessary to get a good hologram. What these books don't tell you is firstly you need p otherwise Breswter's angle doesn't "work" (I almost got fired from my first holography job because I kept insisting that <famous holographer> was wrong about using Brewster's without p. He felt I was upstaging him!) and secondly Brewster's angle isn't 60. It's given by (inv tan)(n), which for glass (n=1,5) is 56.5 degrees. OK, so that's pedantic, but some holographers religiously set their references at a "Brewster's angle" of 60 just to "do it right". As Bob says, there's also an assumption that the reference is perfectly collimated (which it never is), the plane of incidence of the hologram is perfectly flat throughout the setup (which is never measured) and all the indices of all the materials (glass-emulsion-plastic film?-glass-air) are known and every interface reflection has been calculated. Woodgrain can come from several internal interfaces, not just one as is shown in most books. In practice none of this is ever done. Even the fact that the film is facing the reference or away from it is important, since the emulsion has a different n and so internal reflections will vary according to the interface indices. Also, as kaveh has shown, there is a gradual fall-off of the reflectivity for p, it doesn't mysteriously vanish at 60 only to re-appear at 60.1. You can reference at 45 for p and still get negligible woodgrain.

Another factor is that at 60 degrees and assuming n=1.5, the light enters the glass at (inv sin)((sin(60)/1.5)) = 35 degrees. Now, there's a reduction of efficency proportional to the cos of the angle between polarisation vectors (Fresnel Arago laws) which means that there's a (1-cos(35 )~0.2, or 20% loss. When I shoot with film I sandwich film between two glass plates. Unless I'm really, really careful, the film is usually slightly twisted which means that the plane of incidence is no longer absolutely horizonatal. So even if I get perfect Brewster's I'll still get woodgrain on film. For me, this isn't important since I use film to test and make the real shot on glass.

Another factor may be that for an H1, woodgrain may not matter too much. Woodgrain forms on the surface of the H1, which is usually several inches away from the H2. If the H1-H2 distance is 8 to 10 times as far away as the H2 depth, the woodgrain will not be seen in white-light reconstruction. It'll disperse too much to be seen even as a color blur. This will reduce the intensity of the H1 and so will affect you ratio, but that can be compensated for by altering the beamsplitter.

Of course, none of this implies that you should not use Brewster's angle, or any other angle at any polarisation (OK, any linear polarisation), but the reference angle needs to be chosen with a whole set of conditions and repercussions and woodgrain is only one such consideration. Even if woodgrain is a major consideration, there are many other factors affecting it, not just the angle of the reference and the right polarisation.

I'd like to share a technique I've discovered which prevents "wood grain" fringes, and increases both the brightness and "viewing zone" of image-planed reflection holograms. It's based on the material Polaroid used to make to reduce glare off computer screens, which is a circular polarizer laminated to a linear polarizer (I believe the product number was HNCP-36).

I was using p-polarized light in the collimated reconstruction beam for my 30x40cm H1s, and p-polarized light in the collimated reference beam (at approximately Brewster's angle) for the H2. The system was laid out flat on the table, so that all incidence angles were parallel to the table. The problem was that the light comming from anywhere on the H1 that was out of the plane of incidence was no longer p-polarized when it hit the H2 recording plate! This effectively decreased the efficiency of the gratings formed by those rays (the skew rays) and the collimated reference beam.

My solution was to use the HNCP-36 as follows:

A) the HNCP-36 was cut so that the linear side was parallel to the edges of the piece (as opposed to being at 45 degrees as supplied),

B) the HNCP-36 was index-matched to the emulsion side of the recording plate with the linear side toward the emulsion and oriented to pass s-polarized light,

C) the reconstruction beam for the H1 was made to be circularly polarized, and

D) the reference beam for the H2 was made to be s-polarized.

The use of a circularly polarized H1 reconstruction beam meant that the skew rays diffracted by the H1 were also circularly polarized. This light would first encounter the circular polarizer at the recording plate, which would make them linear again. The resulting linearly polarized skew rays would then make fringes of maximum visibility with the linearly polarized reference beam, and this increased the brightness of the H2 when viewed off to the sides.

The use of s-polarization at the H2 recording plate provided maximum fringe visibility regardless of the angles of incidence, therefore increasing the overall efficiency of the hologram.

"Wood grain" fringes were avoided because:

A) the linearly polarized reference beam passes through the linear polarizer,

B) it's made to be (let's say right-handed) circular,

C) reflects off the polarizer-to-air interface as (in this case left-handed) circular,

D) becomes linear again after passing back through the circular polarizer (but now its polarization has been rotated by 90 degrees), and

E) is absorbed by the linear polarizer before hitting the emulsion again.

The downside of this technique is that it is wasteful of light. The resulting holograms, however, are brighter and stay bright all the way to the edges of the viewing zone. Also, the use of Brewster's angle is not necessary for the H2 reference beam. As Keveh pointed out, a little "wood grain" on the H1 is tolerable and the small amount that results from this technique is generally not noticed in the final image.
