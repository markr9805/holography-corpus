Joe Farina Thu Jan 18, 2007 6:05 pm #1
While on the subject of shutters, I would like to raise a question. I have a paper describing passive fringe stabilization for holography (i.e., doing everything you can to improve fringe contrast without fringe-locking). Among other things, it states the following:

"Placing the shutters just before the hologram recording fixture, and warming up the beam-forming optical components with the laser beam before the exposure can almost entirely eliminate thermal expansion of optical components due to the heat generated by the laser beam."

In other words, the paper suggest placing all of your shutters (at least two in a split-beam arrangement, one for reference and one for object) as far "downstream" as possible, i.e., right before the hologram being recorded.

The implication is, if you don't do this, some of your optics (mirrors or whatever) will warm up and expand while the exposure is being made. This sounds like a real concern to me, and not often addressed (in fact, this is the only mention I've ever seen of this). It definitely complicates the use of shuttering, since expanded beams will require a larger amount of shuttering material, and they need to be included for each object beam as well as the reference.

In the old days, people tended to use low-power HeNe's and the heating effect may have been inconsequential. But with so many 315's being used, I wonder if the situation is different. (I remember when I checked the power of my 315 for the first time, I had to be careful not to let the beam hit the black plastic case of my Lasercheck, else it would make little pock-marks in it.)

Are "downstream" shutters really helpful for long-exposure split-beam holography (using moderately powerful lasers)? Or is this just an over-refinement?
JohnFP Thu Jan 18, 2007 6:26 pm #2
That's interesting Joe.

I just did an 8x10 split beam tranmisison and jacked the power up to 300mW so I could cut the exposure down to 10 seconds. My beam runs to two mirrors first which rotate the polarization. Then a beam splitter. Then off to more mirrors and spatial filters with that cheap attenuator in line for fine adjustment of the object beam intensity. So at 300mW for 10 seconds I saw no problems.
