To ellaborate somewhat. You can make a holographic lens or mirror from a mirror and you can make a holographic lens or mirror from a lens. The dependant factor is which side of the plate your reference and object come in from. That is, as long as your reference beam and object beams come in from opposited sides of the plate you get a reflection hologram (mirror) and as long as your reference beam and object beam come in from the same side of the plate you get a transmission hologram (lens) no matter what you are using in your object beam - mirror or lens.

Also, to get a classical mirror, as Colin touched on, you need to get the hologram to be very efficient and reflect all wavelengths (very broadband). But if you want to make a mirror that works as a mirror for just a single wavelength for use in your table geometry then that is much more obtainable.

> Bird of Prey: This started me to wondering, assuming that the above is true, if the following might also be true. If you made a hologram of a parabolic relfector, like the kind you would see in a telescope, wouldn't this have a focal point? Just like a regular telescope mirror?

Sort of. You could also get a holographic parabolic reflector simply from using two diverging beams entering the plate from opposite sides with no original parabolic reflector in your set up at all. Example. Lets say you make a hologram with a diverging beam 24 inches from the plate on one side and another diverging beam 24 inches from the plate entering from the other side and off center by 30 degrees. After you process the hologram you put it back on the table. You illuminate the plate with a diverging beam 24 inches on center then you will get a reflection that focuses that beam at 24 inches and off axis by 30 degrees. Of course the efficiency of the reflection will vary with material used and processing parameters and the 30 degree angle and 24 inch focus will depend on processing also. Now if you took the same hologram and illuminated it with a collimated beam your focus spot would be at 12 inches. This is calculated by the simple lens equation 1/o + 1/i = 1/f (o=Object distance i=Image distance). In the above in the creation of our hologram our object distance (o) is 24 inches and our image distance (i) is 24 inches which yields a focal length (f) of 12 inches. Then using the 12 focal length and a object distance of infinity (collimated) for the replay with a collimated beam we get a focus spot size at 12 inches.

Ed Wesly Tue Nov 30, 2004 2:40 pm #5
> You illuminate the plate with a diverging beam 24 inches on center then you will get a reflection that focuses that beam at 24 inches and off axis by 30 degrees.

Unfortunately the light coming out of the HOE will look like it is coming from a source 24 inches behind the HOE, where the original source was. In other words, a virtual focus.

Flipping the plate around in this instance to generate the pseudoscopic image, keeping the on-axis replay beam 24" from the HOE, will generate a real off-axis focus, or rather, foci, as there will be some serious astigmatism.

Check this out with the Benton Math from the 1982 and 1985 LFC conferences or the MIT site.

> Then using the 12 focal length and a object distance of infinity (collimated) for the replay with a collimated beam we get a focus spot size at 12 inches.

Yes, there will be a focus in one plane at 12 inches, but the focus in the other plane will be quite a bit different, making two stripes instead of a single point focus.

This astigmatism business is what drives one nuts when designing HOE's, especially those used at wavelengths other than recording, or source and focal points nearer or further than those used in recording.

Dinesh Tue Nov 30, 2004 5:21 pm #8
A couple of points that may cause confusion. A hologram of a mirror is not a mirror and, similarly, a hologram of a lens is not a lens. If you simply took a mirror and placed it either in front or behind a plate as if it were a prcelain cat, then the original reference beam would allow you to see a mirror, just like you'd see a porcelain cat. This hologram would not satisfy any lens laws. In order to make a hologram that behaves as a lens you need to make a hologram of pure light fields such that a random incident light field would cause a diffracted light field which would simulate the refracted or refllected light field of a real optic. So John's geometry would create light fields because the hologram is made from light fields. Unfortunately, the example sort of falls down because, as Ed Wesley pointed out, the diffracted field from his example would be virtual. Also, the lens equation does not apply here because the fringes in this geometry have no power. A collimated beam would simply result in another collimated beam. The reftractive lens idea is sound, though I haven't checked the figures. One thing to be careful about in case of HOE's is aberrations. This is the fly in the ointment of all prospective HOEs! Anyone who really tries to make these HOEs quickly realises that the practice is not as simple as it sounds. A while back there was a lot of talk of replacing camera optics with HOEs which never got anywhere because of the practical problem of minimising aberrations.

You need to be careful applying the lens equation willy nilly to HOEs. The lens equation deals with Geometrical Optics where lambda is assumed to be zero. No consideration is taken of dispersion and other lambda-related functions. Benton, in "Benton Math", attempted to simulate a sort of "Holographic lens eqaution" by including dispersion into the lens equation. However, he did it at a time when few collimated the beam. The equation assumes a diverging beam which then converges, the point of convergence being dependant on lambda due to dispersive effects. Dispersion of collimated beams due to diffraction is a more interesting problem. There's loads of references to the mathematical treatment of HOE's. Hariharan is a good source. So is the so-called "Bible of Holography" , Optical Holography by Collier, Lin and Burchardt. At least, we used to call it "The Bible"!

[quote:a1a1449acb="JohnFP"]

If I introduce a difference in illuminating the HOE by lengthening the reference beam point source I will see the abberration effect of the inner rays and the outter rays relative to the plate will refract differently with respect to one another.[/quote:a1a1449acb]

You've got to be careful of the optical model you're using. This is a diffractive model not a refractive one. The usual lens laws of ray models assume a) paraxial rays and b) zero wavelength. These can be used as an approximation but diffraction is primarily a diffractive procedure which relies on wavelength, or physical optical, models. By using the 1/(lambda)z term you specifically use Fourier optics to manipulate a new lens equation into the diffractive realm, but you lose the justification of the paraxial approximation.

The transfer function of an input function f(x_1, y_1) onto an input plane at x_2, y_2 is given by using the Helmoholtz, Kirchoff integral and introducing a Green's function. If you start with the Helmholtz equations:

(nabla^2) + k^2)G = 0

and

(nabla^2 + k^2)U=0

where G is a Green's function, using Green's Theorem, you end up with an integral describing the form of light due to an expanding, spherical light field.If you restrict yourself to plane waves, this becomes:

S[f(x_1, y_1)exp{k*i(z^2 + (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}^0.5]dx_1dy_1.

Where I've used S as an integral sign. You can sort of manipulate this, the way Benton did, and simplfy it for a specific expanding beam to create a "holographic lens equation" by substituting a Green's function that simulates a lens. That's the basis of "Benton math". However, Benton math does not take into effect thick, volume holograms where Bragg diffraction donimates. There are no Bragg selectivity effects a la Kogelnik (I've often pondered a "updated Benton Math" by incorporating Kogelnik into Benton. I played with this back in 87 or 88 using a perfectly generalised coordinate system and, to my surprise, found that an elliptical system using elliptical integrals dropped out as incredibly simple.Steve Hart of Voxel urged me to publish, but I don't like publishing. If anyone is interested, reformulate the Helmoholtz function using generalised coordinates with a generlaised metric. Carry out the integration, also in generalised coords and then input an elliptical metric.) Anyway, if you make a thick hologram, and you almost always do with modern materials, then you need to take into account off Bragg deviation. This cannot be simulated by refractive models.

In your example, the "outer rays" would be off Bragg and so would cause aberrations that have nothing to do with refraction.The beam profile would skew, depending on how off Bragg you were. Now, if you were perfectly symmetric and right on-axis,it's possible that the off Bragg effects would also be symmetric, but the moment you deviated the beam in any way, the skew would get worse.

Depnds on the angle of deviation, the Bragg structure, the emulsion thickness, the index variation etc etc etc...
