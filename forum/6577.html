Joe Farina Thu Dec 06, 2007 1:38 pm #5
If baffles are helpful in your setup, then you most likely have a thermal problem as well, because there's a good chance that those currents are not of uniform temperature. When you say "wiggle" to you mean somewhat jerky? That sounds like air currents rather than slow creep of the steel table surface. If you must use a metal table top, steel is the best choice (short of cast iron or Invar), but the reason fringe-lockers are typically used is to correct slow thermal changes in steel table-tops. In my experience, I would not call this "wiggle" but very slow fringe drift. I wouldn't worry about fringe-locking at this stage.

If you really want to do long split-beam exposures, then I would suggest a redesigned table, even though I know it's a major undertaking. But fringe-locking is full of frustrations, and should be used only when there is no other alternative, in my opinion.

I would strongly recommend that you use a granite or marble tabletop, with gravity mounts. For a support structure, I'm afraid that wood doesn't sound so good. I suggest you think of something else. The table does not have to be large, in fact it should be as small as possible for a variety of reasons. The shorter you can make all the beam paths, the better. This will result in less drift. Then, enclose the table, or table area, totally. Of course, the laser should be completely off the table.

As a sidenote, according to data that I have, oak is an extremely stable material. But for various reasons, I would stay away from wood in general.
JohnFP Thu Dec 06, 2007 4:41 pm #6
Hans, believe me you don't want to dabble in fringe locking for what you are doing. Fringe locking can introduce movement into your table as easily as it takes it out. The best time to fringe lock is when you are doing extreemely long exposures (+10 min), need to compensate for small (a wavelength or two) slow movements, and the number of optic on your table is at least 4X the number of additional optics needed for Michaelson for fringe locker (this gives you a 80% chance of fixing problem but a 20% chance of creating one, which nets a 60% chance of a more stable set up). I will explain.

To set up a fringe locker you need to steal some object light and some reference light. Obviously the object light should come from the object illumination source because most objects reflect way too little light to make a sucessful locking fringe for the locker. Where the two beams are stolen from is up to the individual geometry but usually is it not convenient. Thus additional mirors, beam splitter, beam expander and photo cell (fringe locker detector) all need to be added. But these additions are all AFTER the hologram or "film" if you will. Basically you set up your split beam geometry for the hologram then steal both object and ref light and make another holographic geometry (a Michaelson) with that light to get expanded fringes to lock in on. The problem is if any of the stability problems happen in this secondary outside Michaelson then the fringe locker tries to compensate for that movement by adjusting the piezo mirror in the holographic set up which did not move. Thus the fringe locker actually introduces movement in the set up where there was none.

I hope this is clear. So until you can get a 5 or 10 minute exposure without a finge locker, don't even try to use one. Sorry, no easy way out of this one. But you can surely set up the additional mirrors, bs, and expander just as if you had a fringe locker in place and see "real time" during your exposure what is happening. If you need any help with the placement of these, let me know.

> Joe Farina: Thanks, Paulos. Your method is causing me to completely re-think the idea of fringe-locking.I noticed that you had 4 fringes on the TV. Is that typical? In the literature for commercial fringe-lockers, the standard amount of "locking" is 1/20th of a fringe over the length of the exposure. By watching the fringes, and manually adjusting the potentiometer, do you think that kind of control is feasible? Do you think a very large fringe would help to provide better resolution/control?

The fringes are rarely so beautiful, regular and straight as in the graph.

To achieve good control, the fringe contrast should result in really dark regions.

I did not measure the accuracy, but I roughly estimate that the resolution is better than 5 mm,

1/10th of a fringe if you have managed the fringe distance on the screen to be about 5 cm.

A proper piezo-potentiometer pair allows for a much finer control, of say 1/100th.

The limiting factor here is your eye, the sensor.

The fringes should be neither too close together, nor too large.

A very large fringe would enlarge the dark as well the bright regions, making them more faint.

I cannot say which is the optimum distance, it depends on the particular setup.
Joe Farina Fri Jan 25, 2008 8:10 pm #33
Thanks, Paulos. Could you please elaborate a bit on the "piezo-potentiometer pair"?
Paulos Sat Jan 26, 2008 12:03 pm #34
> Joe Farina: Thanks, Paulos. Could you please elaborate a bit on the "piezo-potentiometer pair"?

Provided that you have a proper piezoelement and assuming that this moves 1 fringe every 10 Volt,

you must be in the position to fine regulate the voltage to <100 mV (that is 10V/100),

to achieve a resolution of 1/100th fringe.

This is not possible if you just use a 1 turn pot, you should choose another alternative

as a multi-turn pot, or two pots in series e.g. 100K and 5K, the second one for fine tuning.

There is always the hysteresis effect of piezoelements, but this will not cause any noticeable trouble to this setup.
