semb Mon Feb 26, 2007 6:07 am #1
With many thanks to BobH I solved the problems. The main issue was stability in my setup. Now that I'm shooting on a marble tile, and with the plate suspended in a 3-point arrangement, I'm getting nice images. Here is the first decent one:

![Image](http://superduper.net/~simon/first_dcg.jpg)

Thanks to Jeff-Blyth as well for the timely warning a few days ago about glycerine getting used up - I made up fresh sensitizing solution for this shot.

Note - at the center the plate had 100mJ/cm2, dropping to about 20mJ/cm2 at the bottom. This means that I'm not quite seeing the claimed sensitivity of G307 yet - the brightness has dropped off significantly by the time the exposure is down to 30mJ/cm2.

This plate was coated with veil method, plate and gelatin at 125degF. Knox brand gelatin - 12%, with 0.5% per weight of gelatin KDi added, and glycerin. Plate was cured for 18 hrs, exposed to bright light for 30mins, sensitized - then dried under a cold air fan for 10 mins. Exposed for 200 seconds (intensity measured in the center as about 0.5mW/cm2) to 532nm. The plate was placed on a heated plate (225degF) for 3 mins, then cooled under the cold air fan again for 2 mins. 1 min in 22degC water wash, then rinse in cold, then 30% IPA - 30 secs, 90% IPA - 30 secs, 100% IPA 1 minute. Finally dried by hot air.

Hello Semb, great work!!!

I have to remark that regular DCG needs about 100mJ/cm^2 for 488 (blue) ...BUT... about 250mJ/cm^2 for 514. A slight bit more even for 532. At 100mJ/cm^2 of 532 with regular DCG you will probably see less then half the brightness.

So to get a nice hologram with 100mJ/cm^2 and a pretty good image at 20mJ/cm^2 is in fact a nice improvement in sensitivity.

As a real good comparison test, you could try to make a couple of regular DCG plates and expose the same way with the same energy and normal processing.

Congratulations, well done. I'm a bit curious about the improvement due to the marble tile: what kind of surface did you use before? For long exposures, I feel that stone, ceramic, and glass are the best support materials, due to low thermal expansion and contraction. This may be very important for simple Denisyuk holograms also, because the distance between the emulsion and the object must remain "locked" in place (I assume), and the supports for the hologram plate often rest on top of the support surface, whatever it is.

Your nice hologram, and the ones by Cristiano Perrucci (whose holograms were done on a ceramic tile, as I recall) seem to confirm my "prejudice" in favor of stone or ceramic.

It can be difficult to find thermal expansion coefficients for stone such as marble or granite, or for cermaics, but I believe they are very much below that of steel. Glass would also be a good choice from the standpoint of thermal stability.

Joe Farina Mon Feb 26, 2007 5:48 pm #8
Thanks for the link, Colin. A long time ago, I did a lot of searching on the web, looking for information on thermal expansion of materials. What I was hoping to find was a large list, with a wide range of materials like metals, natural stones, ceramics, glasses, etc., with the same unit of measurement for linear expansion so that it would be easy to see the differences. I couldn't find a list like that, but maybe one is out there now.

One thing I forgot to mention above is the importance of the support posts. Obviously, if one is supposed to have a benefit from the table or base plate being of a low-expansion material, then that benefit would be obviated if a high-expansion material was used to support the plate.

With regards to natural stones, they can be a lot easier to work with than some may think. I have a block of soapstone (steatite) which measures 6 X 6 X 3 inches which was pretty cheap, about $20. This material machines like a dream, any bandsaw will go through it with almost no effort. Almost the same thing can be said about alabaster or stones of similar hardness. (Marble would be too hard, however.) Unfortunately, I don't know the linear expansion of soapstone or alabaster, but I would guess they are very low.

Glass would also be an excellent material to make posts from, since they can be found in the rod form, and the ends could be flame-polished to form a rounded edge, so that they could support a plate nicely in the three-point manner.

Joe Farina Mon Feb 26, 2007 7:41 pm #14
Jeff Blyth's paper states:

"A clean very flat and preferably thick metal plate needs to be arranged in an internally unlit oven at 100C. Another clean flat metal plate needs to be positioned next to the oven under safelighting. After the laser exposure we placed our plate face up on the 100C metal plate. And left it there for a bake time which had been found by trial and error tests. The bake time is quickly brought to an end by removing the plate and placing it face down on the cool flat metal plate outside the oven. On 2mm thick glass the optimum bake time could be 2 minutes and on 3mm glass the optimum bake time might be ~4 minutes."

When I first read this, I thought the reason for the cold plate was to "quickly" stop emulsion heating in the sense that the "optimum" heating time was controlled (i.e., it wasn't heated too much during the gradual cool-down period). But in another recent thread, he stated that the reason for the cold plate was to be certain the emulsion had cooled down completely before going into the water bath. In fact, he said it could just cool down naturally before going into the water. Apparently, the cold plate is there to simply speed up the process.
