BobH Sat Aug 07, 2004 4:43 am #3
Sounds like too much leg and too little table! I recommend using only three legs. Three points make the most stable support. The carpet between the blocks in the legs only makes them less stable. Be certain to allow the air trapped in the middle of the inner tubes to escape. I'd also remove the carpet from under the concrete and use something like Fix-All (from the hardware store) instead.

in any case, an interferometer built on a Newport table on Newport isolation legs will show fronge movement if the system is mounted on a wood floor, and you walk around close to it. Don't move during the exposure! 8^)
Tom B. Sat Aug 07, 2004 4:56 am #4
What kind of floor do you have and how is it supported? The inner tubes etc. can filter out some vibrations, but if your floor sags when you step on it, the change in floor shape will ultimately end up tilting and bending the concrete slab. If you knew how the floor was supported, you might be able to find a better position for the table legs over the joists, or better yet, if you are on a ground floor, you could try cutting holes in the floor to sink posts for table support right into the ground, keeping the table isolated from the floor.

I'm blessed with a concrete floored basement in a quiet neigborhood - the floor is very stable and no isolation is required.

MichaelH Sat Aug 07, 2004 2:42 pm #6
Also make sure that the inner tubes are not over-inflated. Once the table is resting on them you should be able to push a finger into the side of the tube about 1/2" without causing the table to shake. The tubes should be pliable and not drum-head tight.

Hopefully you'll find that your mounts are also causing problem. Why hopefully? Because that's pretty easily solved. Using 11ga steel and magnetic mounts will help quite a lot but also pay attention to how your lenses and mirrors are stuck to the mounts such that they aren't loose.
Bruce Sun Aug 08, 2004 1:20 am #7
You may have some degree of vibration isolation with your table but without some sort ofrigidity you will always have a "bendable" table. It's all about frequency or wavelength of the incoming distubance, with about 5 hz. or less being the most difficult to deal with. If you can make the system above your innertubes very rigid and heavy then it will resist these long wavelengths with all energy dissipating at the innertube/ concrete (or steel) interface. In my experience, a concrete plate at least 3' or more thick will do wonders and then top it off with a steel plate.

If its thick, its probably rigid. (concrete)

If its heavy, it will take more energy to set in motion. (concrete, steel and sand).

It seems to be a common idea that your optics mounts should be vibration resistant also. I can't disagree more! If you are still trying to deal with vibrations at your optics then either you live above a subway or you didn't get your table right. Magnetic bases are not resistant to vibration they just stay in place when you perform fine adjustments. A table that small should be easy to get right.

Concrete = cheap

Steel = not cheap

I would not go thicker than 11 guage because of cost.

B.
