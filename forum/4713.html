4. Water in air.

Anyway, I believe (of course I still don't understand DCG... LOL!), the softer gelatin (higher bloom) holds moisture better (more, longer etc.) then a lower bloom and thus dries (sets) more slowly, too slowly with my drying technique. This is what caused the milkyness. The next time I make plates with this gel type I will insure there is more air flow over the plates after coating and spinning and get them to dry faster. What tipped me off on this is one time I coated plates and put them in a light tight, air tight box too soon. There was still a lot of moisture in them. The came out milky. Of course I can be waaaaay off here but only trying with the next batch of plates will tell.

I did take one of the milky plates and soak it in water for about 20 seconds and then blew off the water with canned air. The milkyness was completely gone even after 3 hours but we will see tonight if it is still gone. I am not crazy about this technique as I am sure some of the AmDi dissolved out (although I could not see a difference by eye).

a_k Mon Aug 11, 2008 8:35 pm #35
> My question; Has anyone treaded a milky DCG plate to remove the milkyness?

My answer is not specific to DCG so maybe it is not applicable. With some gelatines i noticed that the solution turned somewhat milky as soon as guanidine carbonate was added.

The remedy was to wash the gelatine with ice cold DI water several times until the gelatine looked clear.

During this procedure the gelatine swells somewhat. If you can't remove as much water as you would like after the last washing, you can use a thick glass tube, fix a piece of cloth at one end and suck the water out.
