Ed Wesly Tue Jun 05, 2007 4:24 pm #5
Polarization preserving fiber is definitely a good idea for single beam holograms. But I am not a big fan of fiber delivery for holography for a variety of reasons that we had hashed out here before, but may have been demolished by the "Forum Wrecker."

The big problem with fibers are that they never stop moving. This is a major issue with split beam fiber delivery. An airplane pilot name Bill Durrell built a contraption to split a beam into 3 fiber outputs, and when we tried to shoot some simple gratings, nothing was happening. He of course blamed me, but I made an interferometer out of the grating set up by putting a 50/50 beamsplitter at right angles to the plate holder and the fringes just wouldn't stop moving. Fixturing the fibers in plastic jackets attached to the tabletop helped, but it was still less stable than conventional optics.

For single beam work, to deliver a beam from a remote location there is not a big problem, as if the fiber is moving, the hologram still sees the same phase changes in the reference/object interference pattern. But the divergence out of the fiber is quite wide, kind of like a 40X or 60X microscope objective, so you end up close to the set up, which may be a not very good choice of geometry. The light comes out of the fiber like it's spatially filtered, nice and clean, but if you add lenses after the fiber to control its divergence, then you have the age old problem of bullseyes, etc.

If you blasting 100 mW of green into a fiber, it might start heating it up and lose tune after a while. Plus you might not get more than 85% coupling efficiency.

If I haven't dissuaded you, you should look for core sizes of fiber of 4 to 6 microns. If you start getting into what I call chunky fibers, like 10's of microns, you'll get some speckly looking modes out of the fiber. I found the best thing to do was gather up some samples and see what gives a decent mode and preserves polarization, regardless of the manufacturer's spec's.

TJ was trying to sell everyone that fibers were the wave of the future in holography in the early '90's, but it didn't turn out that way. Sorry to be such a downer, but I haven't found them worth the effort in dabbling with them.

In reading the following, I assume that a long coherence length laser is needed for multimode fibers (greater then 10um in diamter). Whereas if John's set up was done with a short coherent length laser, problems may arrise.

Also, it seems in graded-index fiber the movement of the fiber and thus the change in path length may not be as critical.

It also seem intuitive that to get more like to get down the "pipe" one would like a lens (or launcher) that converges the beam ever so slightly (long focal length) so that most of the light is past the critical internal reflection angle.

John, is your fiber Multimode, Graded-index?

[http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_Fiber](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_Fiber)

> Multimode fiberFiber with large (greater than 10 μm) core diameter may be analyzed by geometric optics. Such fiber is called multimode fiber, from the electromagnetic analysis (see below). In a step-index multimode fiber, rays of light are guided along the fiber core by total internal reflection. Rays that meet the core-cladding boundary at a high angle (measured relative to a line normal to the boundary), greater than the critical angle for this boundary, are completely reflected. The critical angle (minimum angle for total internal reflection) is determined by the difference in index of refraction between the core and cladding materials. Rays that meet the boundary at a low angle are refracted from the core into the cladding, and do not convey light and hence information along the fiber. The critical angle determines the acceptance angle of the fiber, often reported as a numerical aperture. A high numerical aperture allows light to propagate down the fiber in rays both close to the axis and at various angles, allowing efficient coupling of light into the fiber. However, this high numerical aperture increases the amount of dispersion as rays at different angles have different path lengths and therefore take different times to traverse the fiber. A low numerical aperture may therefore be desirable.In graded-index fiber, the index of refraction in the core decreases continuously between the axis and the cladding. This causes light rays to bend smoothly as they approach the cladding, rather than reflecting abruptly from the core-cladding boundary. The resulting curved paths reduce multi-path dispersion because high angle rays pass more through the lower-index periphery of the core, rather than the high-index center. The index profile is chosen to minimize the difference in axial propagation speeds of the various rays in the fiber. This ideal index profile is very close to a parabolic relationship between the index and the distance from the axis.

I guess that your fiber was a gradient index type and that could explain it

in terms of John FP 's helpful post which must surely hit the nail on the head as follows . If the the fibre curvature is changed during exposure then the outer part of the curve is stretched and the innerpart is compressed.

So with an initially uniform straight fibre with a uniform refractive index

across the inner section's diameter that means bending would have caused a continuous R.I. change across the diameter with a consequent small but critical change in path length across the diameter compared to the starting conditions when the laser shutter first opened. But if it is already a variable gradient type fiber across the diameter then this would

much reduce the critical effect of bending because the path length changes between the inner and outer sections along the diameter would be less compared to an initially uniform high R.I. fibre.
