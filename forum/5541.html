Now that I finally have success making constantly relatively good Ddenisyuks with PFG-01, I decided to try coating a few plates with MBDCG. I was familiar with processing the plates because of our mini PCG in Holland some weeks ago ([http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4379](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4379)).

I mixed the famous Blyth recipy and used Dr. Oetker's culinary gelatin for the mixture. Here are the results:

- First plate was coated with John's spinning technique on an old 75rpm record player. This created more mess than I anticipated for in my garage. I decided I need much more practice with this process with clear gelatin in daylight. The plate looked very uneven and messy.

- Next six plates I coated with Jean Dufrasne's mold coating methods. I had lots of practice with this from trying also Jeff Blyth's other famous silver halide loading method.

I left all plates in a dark place for about 20 hours. I exposed the spin coated plate first. Water 28C, first alcohol 45%, second 99%. In the second alcohol bath, the hologram already became visible, but after I took the hologram out and dried it with a hair drier, it quickly shifted towards the blue and disappeared forever. I tried a slightly warmer bath wich gave more fogging, but due to the fog's milky density, now only the real image is visible from the back side of the hologram.

I tried various exposure times on the next six plates (wich have a much thinner coating). Only on the final plate (with the shortest exposure time of only one minute) an actual dim hologram was visible after the second re-processing at 30C.

Because I processed the plates the same way Jean showed to Kris and me, I believe that some other variable must be detrimental to the success of yesterday. Perhaps my gelatin is not suitable, or perhaps the coating is too thin because of my type of gelatin. If my coatings are thin, perhaps this might also explain the low success rate I had with the diffusion loading of AgBr.

Conclusions:

Any idea's anyone?

I have an unused Meyer bar that can coat layers of 8um, but I think I also made an error when I bought that rather expensive piece of equipment. I never took into account that the actual gelatin layer will be much thinner when it has dried out. So I should have bought one that leaves thicker layers behind. Does anyone know how much percent gelatin shrinks after it is dry?

*Tip to other starters in MBDCG. When using a hot hair drier to dry the plate after the last alcohol bath, wait for the plate to cool down again before trying to re-process in a slightly warmer bath. I very effectively distroyed three experiments with this stupid hasty error.
JohnFP Sat May 13, 2006 12:53 pm #2
Hans, if you want to use the Myer bar you bought, why not just put two strips of thin tape down the sides thus additionally lifting the bar off the plate by the thickness of the tape. It's how I have recently been doing it. I belive a general rule of thumb is, if you have 12% of gelatin in your formula the final dry thickness will only be 12% of the coated thickness.

I coating with tape (about 25um) and a Myer bar of either .04" (1.02mm) widing which yield about a 91um wet coating or .025" (.61mm) winding wich yields about a 55um wet coating.

Thus

25 + 91 = 116um wet ....and dry (11%gel form) = 12 - 13um

25 + 55 = 80um wet .... and dry (11%gel form) = 8 - 9um

which is a little thick but I like the way the tape keeps the edges clean. I originally bought the bars to use without tape and it would have made 91um wet = 10um dry

55um wet = 6um dry

If your hologram is visible in the last alcohol and then dries to violet or blue then the fringes are collapsing. Now we need to find out why.

Hans Tue May 23, 2006 11:44 am #38
One more note:

> It sounds from you description that it is not simply evaporation that is causing the concentrated blotches. If TMG only was evaporating the MB would remain constantly homogeneous. It sounds as if some type of coagulation is occurring. That is, there is some kind of attraction going on in the plate.Perhaps the MB migrates to the spots where there is the most TMG left as it evaporates, and then crystallizes. At first the spots are blue, about 0.5mm in size, and divided over the coating homogenously. Then, after some time (about a day), the MB starts to turn a light purple colour.

On mold coated plates (coated between a sandwich of glass plates) the blotches are very small. On veil coated plates, it looks more like as if there are cracks in the MB, where the gelatin is completely transparent. I think the difference comes from the fact that the top most surface of a veil coated plate is much more smooth than a mold coated plate. I have one veil coated plate that just faded to transparency without blotches. I think that the TMG evaporates the quickest on the area's where the coating is more 'rough'. If one would look at the surface of a mold coated plate through a microscope it looks like a mountainous terrain. I think the TMG evaporates most quickly at the tips of these mountains. The MB then migrates to these mountains. If the surface is super smooth, like on one of the veil coated plates, TMG evaporates evenly and the MB just stays in place or moves to the surface in an even distribution. When the MB on these plates eventually reaches the surface and dries, it gets it's light purple colour, but because it is so evenly distributed, it looks transparent.

But this one that Hans is having is more of a minor skirmish in an entirely different country !However your post has had the good effect of making me concentrate on putting the problem in a nutshell for non-chemists..

Whenever we dissolve mixed salts in water the ions will always try to arrange themselves to make insoluble pairings so that they can precipitate out of solution.

As purchased methylene blue is a chloride salt (MB) Cl

So if we mix a solution of potassium dichromate K Di with a solution of this (MB)Cl it unfortunately manages to make the insoluble salt methylene blue dichromate (MB) Di

K Di + (MB)Cl -- KCl + (MB)Di precipitate

So all our dye is lost and it is not an actual true fading reaction involving the dye getting chemically decolorized----- it is just a precipitation problem.

We can fortunately solve the problem by taking advantage of the fact that

(MB) Cl is a salt made of a weak base with a strong acid . All we have to do is to make sure that there is always a stronger free base than MB around at all times before exposure is made, that will then force the MB to remain in solution in its free base form (MB)OH. (We are lucky here that this stuff is still water-soluble as so many similar organic free bases are not,) and although the gelatin film is not water the ions in it behave as if it is.

So as long as there is an excess of stronger free base around than MB, then MB will be permanently prevented from forming (MB)Di precipitate because the stronger base is always going to form the salt in preference to MB forming it.

Under alkaline conditions dichromate itself splits up to form chromate ions but that doesn’t change the rather simplified argument above.

So our problem here is that the very strong organic base TMG is unfortunately able to slowly evaporate . That then leads to the point

where the free base is no longer in excess enough to stop (MB)OH starting to form an insoluble chromate/dichromate salt.

Some gelatins help to prevent this by being slightly alkaline themselves to start off with..( They are the types that have been treated with lime).

So we need to prevent the gradual loss of TMG base by making glass sandwiches.

In Holland the culinary gelatin is made from pork and manufactured with acid. This gives it a low acidity and a very high isoelectric point. This is called the type A gelatin.

Photographic gelatin is made from cow (preferably the bones) and is made manufactured with an alkali. This gives it a higher PH and a very low isoelectric point. This is called a type B gelatin.

[http://www.gelatin-gmia.com/html/rawmaterials_app.html](http://www.gelatin-gmia.com/html/rawmaterials_app.html)

It turns out that at a PH that is lower than the isoelectric point, gelatin likes to bind ONLY with POSITIVE ions. At a PH that is higher than the isoelectric point, gelatin likes to bind ONLY with NEGATIVE ions. At a PH that is equal to the isoelectric point gelatin binds to nothing.

[http://www.jgp.org/cgi/content/abstract/1/2/237](http://www.jgp.org/cgi/content/abstract/1/2/237)

Now, the PH of type A and type B gelatins don't differ much (Type A: 3.8-5.5, Type B: 5.0-7.5), but the isoelectric points differ wildly. (Type A: 7-9, TypeB: 4.7-5.4)
