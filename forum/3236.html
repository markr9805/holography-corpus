Kaveh Thu Mar 17, 2005 5:00 pm #4
A rainbow is a transmission hologram so you should treat it as such.

I never measured beam ratios when transferring H1's to H2. As you are focusing an image, the beam ratio will depend on which area you look at. My rule was to increase the object beam level till it was just visible on top of the reference beam. I would block the source of the object beam and unblock it in quick succession, moving my hand in and out of the beam path. The difference in total brightness had to be just visible at the brightest parts of the image beam. Any brighter, and there was the danger of burn-in.

Dinesh Thu Mar 17, 2005 5:37 pm #6
Beam ratios are like exposures. There's no "golden rule". Each object needs a different beam ratio depending on such factors as polarisation, dynamic range of the object, angle subtended by the object at the plate, emulsion etc, etc. Kaveh's method is pretty much what most holographers use, however, this needs a certain amount of experience - the fly in the ointment is the phrase,"'just' visible". "just" is different to each person. With us, "just" is different for Joy than for me (also depends on the laser wavelength).

Beam rtios such as 1:1 and 4:1 were historical for a certain fringe visibility in the old Kodak emulsion. Back then in the PK (pre-Kogelnik) days, holographic diffraction efficency was calculated using an MTF and thin diffraction (Raman Nath) model. There was no theory of thick or Bragg diffraction in holography. Also the diffraction was assumed to be by amplitude modulation alone, which is a linear process, and was measured by using gratings with pretty narrow angles. Thus the 4:1 came about by simply looking at the H&D curve - usually for Kodak although there was a paper by Nick Phillips stating that an ideal holographic emulsion needed a gamma of >=2 - and drawing the largest triangle you could over the linear portion a la communications theories.. This large triangle showed that a variation of 4:1 on the horizontal (exposure) axis and so the myth/procedure of 4:1 was born. It's instructive to read the ld papers and see this stuff grow.

When new methods and theories (Kogelnik, thick emulsions) came into being, the old 4:1 and 1: 1 still persisted. However today a bleached hologram is non-linear and cannot have an H&D curve. Gamma in these circumstances is a meaningless term since tha ratio of D/log E is 0 if D=0! . Diffraction is nowadays by Bragg phase modulation and delta n is a more meaningful term. So even straight gratings no longer obey the old equations anymore. With display holography the situation changes yet more. Display holography seeks to have the brightest image and that depends more on the colour of the hologram so you have to consider the fringes in the post development phase rather than the recording phase. Display holography also has a set of fourier terms over and above the fourier terms in the gratings themselves, these being the standard fourier breakdown of any image. Each of these fourier terms has it's own delta n causing a variation in angle as well as dynamic range (The Zebra people have my undying appreciation!)

Most display holographers, including me, simply twiddle their fingers in the reference beam and sort of have an intuitive sense of, "Good. Shoot it!"

BobH Thu Mar 17, 2005 7:16 pm #9
I always measured the "hot spot" of the object beam at the recording plane, and the reference beam at the same location (by a consistant method), and defined that as a beam ratio. I'd then expose optimally for that total energy. The rest of the scene would be what it would be.

For any image-planed recording, management of the scene's intensity variation is key to success. The places where the image goes through the plane of the recording and/or the hot spots/specular reflections off the objects generally determine how low the beam ratio can be set (and therefore the maximum brightness of the final hologram). Proper image design begins with a mind's eye pre-visualization of the effects of "image-plane burn". This allows minimum beam ratios to be used, but the actual beam ratio will always be scene dependant. There is no "golden" number.

The H1 plateholder and slit are being imaged several inches in front of the H2 when you look at it normally. They're also being expanded somewhat because of the optics you're using.

I suspect that if you sit far back from the hologram in an otherwise darkened room, you might get hints of the H1 slit and plateholder. I'm going to try this myself tonight.

If you think about the fringe structure of a hologram and the fact that all points are generating their own fringes all across the film, you may begin to see why the H1 holder and slit are causing the image to become dimmer than it otherwise would be. All the fringes are essentially competing/interfering with each each other. Anything not contributing to the image you care about is likely to be taking away brightness. This effect is why you'll get a brighter copy when you decrease the height of the master (less competition for the same fringe space) and is why I typically make my H2's with a master illuminated by a slit of light rather than lighting the entire master (this also increases depth somewhat).
Dinesh Fri Mar 18, 2005 9:49 pm #30
It's essentially because of the slit. The thing you have to be aware of in a rainbow is that the image is the slit, it's not the "object". The "object" just gets carried along with the slit because it happens to be there.

In an open aperture (no slit) laser transmission hologram, the object is usually many cm behind the plate. When illuminated by white light, or any light with a wavelength spread, each component of the reconstruction source produces it's own image. However, due to the angle sensitivity of the reconstruction, the image is displaced. Thus, if you have a point object, let's say a small ball bearing, and you shoot it at 633 with the reference coming in at 60, then the fringes are created such that

lambda = dsin(theta)

where lambda is 633, d is the recorded fringe seperation and theta is 60 degrees. When you reconstruct with 633 at 60 degrees to the plate, the hologrtam already has fringes of seperation d and the angle of reconstruction is

sin(theta) = lambda/d = 633/d

Since lambda is 633 and d is already there caused by a laser at 633, then the angle of reconstruction is the same as the angle of recording. The object shows up in exactly the same position (assuming no change in d due to swelling in processing). Now, reconstruct in green at 550. In this case, the angle of reconstruction is

sin(theta) = 550/d

You see that the angle of reconstruction has shifted. The object seems to be displaced. You can see that at any other wavelength than 633, the object will seem displaced because the angle of reconstruction will shift. If you reconstruct with the entire white light spectrum, you'll get a continuum of colours (a colour spread) because each component of the white light will reconstruct at a different angle. If you reconstruct in a mercury arc lamp or a sodium lamp, you'll get two, maybe three images. Look at an H1 with a street lamp and only the yellow line of the sodium and maybe a gren sodium line will reconstruct. This is known as dispersion. It can be compensated for within certain limits (Take it away, Kaveh! )

Now you can see that since the angle of reconstruction is what causes dispersion and the effective change in object position, the nearer the image plane the image is, ie the nearer the plate, the smaller will be the effective shift in image position for the same angle of diffraction. If the image is shifted by, say, a degree, then the lateral shift at 1" behind the image plane is 1tan(1), while iff it's 5" behind the plane,the lateral shift is 5*tan(1). So, the nearer the image plane an object is, the smaller the dispersion. Can we make this better? Well, one way is to restrict any light to the inside of a small band centered around the image, so that if the angle of reconstruction causes a large shift the image will disappear. But if the angle of reconstruction is shifted by only a small amount and the resulting shift of the image is inside the band, the image will be (relatively) clear and not too dispersed. The band will restrict dispersion to a very small range, depending on the position of the image and the width of the slit. So essentially, you make a hologram of the slit (and whatever happens to be behind the slit. Oh, look, it's a porcelain cat!)) and you arrange for the slit to be imaged right at your eye. Now the slit itself is dispersed so you get several sliits, one for each component of the reconstruction source, and if an image happens to be behind the slit, that too shows up in the colour of the slit. Try looking at your rainbow in a street lamp. Only the predoniinant sodium lines will reconstruct and you'll get two, maybe three slits.
