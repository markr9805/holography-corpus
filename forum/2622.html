I did some experiments today with some laser diodes and making holograms. When I powered up my LNCQ05PS/D&S driver combo, I noticed that the output (visually ) didn't appear to be perfectly stable. After it warmed up, (or so I thought) I shot a reflection hologram, and got a sliced bread hologram. I took my photodiode/amplifier setup and did some listening. The output seemed quite noisy, so I reduced the power, and found a "zone" where it was quieter. I let it stablize a bit, checked to make sure I was in the center of that quiet zone, let it stablize again, and shot a good hologram.

I have a "constant current" setup as well, but the output from that setup seems noisier. Has anyone else checked for amplitude noise from their diode setups? It could be because I'm using a different diode in there (ml1016r) but I shall have to do a shot with it to see if it's any good.

I'm running the diode at an optical output of around 21mw.

tpark Wed Jul 14, 2004 12:09 pm #4
> Colin Kaminski: Can you measure the coherence length at 21mw for me?

I measure a coherence length of 88 cm - this diode seems not as good as the ML1016R, which has a coherence length of around 1.5m at 21mw.

Each device got 10 minutes of warmup before measurements were made.

I had my photodiode/amplifier setup looking into the fringes after the diverging lens, and it seems the "hop zones" are pretty easy to find with such a setup - there's an increase in noise/cracking as the hop zones are approached. You can hear it if the sensor is in the beam on the holography table too - this seems like a handy way to detect potential trouble, instead of wrecking yet another plate.

I made some holograms after these tests, and they were free of banding. I had it set up for fairly long exposures (22 seconds) so I could hopefully catch any drifting if it was there.

Colin Kaminski Thu Aug 12, 2004 2:14 pm #7
> John Klayer: Just about any cheap laser pointer seems to work to make holograms.

There are some that don't. Make sure when you expand the beam it is a single bright spot. Some are multimode and make three or more spots. Also some of the bullet types have internal reflections that make cleaning up the beam impossible.

A good rule of thumb is if you expand the beam with a lens and it looks like a uniform oval or stripe then it will produce a good hologram.

I'm using an ML1016R in an LDM3756 kit, driven by a constant current driver which I obtained from ebay. THe whole thing is powered by 4 "D" batteries. Measured power output was set to around 21mw - I adjusted the power so that I was not in a hop zone. With this setup, I've had no sliced bread holograms. I let the thing warm up for half an hour, but really it stabilizes in less than 10 minutes.

The other setup which I have which does good holgrams is an LNCQ05PS driven by a modified D&S laser driver. I added in a potentiometer in series with the current control resistor so I could adjust the power. Initially I got sliced bread holograms with this (and with the ML1016R) but with some tweaking so that the diode wasn't sitting in a hop zone, it produced good holograms. This is powered by 8 "D" batteries, but I'm sure

it would work with 6 "D" batteries - just have to get enough voltage for the

5 volt regulator. Power ending up being around 25mw, but I'm sure there are hop free zones close to max power.

The wavelength of the LNCQ05PS is slightly longer than that of the ML1016R (not just specifications, actually viewed with a spectroscope) but it doesn't appear to make any difference.

The LNCQ05PS is mounted in an LDM1100 kit, and silicone free heat sink compound is used between the diode and the case.

The LNCQ05PS also works well with a constant current source.

Hope zone detection is done with a silicon photodetector and a small Radio Shack amplifier - it's noisier in the hop zones.

tpark Fri Aug 20, 2004 5:33 am #12
Hi,

I can't remember what specific amplifier it was, but it was a small white box powered by a 9v battery with a 1/8 inch input jack. The initial tests were done with a michelson interferometer, but later I got lazy and just put the thing in the beam of the laser, and turned the pot while monitoring the output power so I didn't cook the diode. Colin is quite correct here - I think some discussion was done in the old forum. The whole exercise of determining if the diode is in a mode hopping zone is a worthy one, since if the diode is stable you won't get the frustration of sliced bread holograms.

Basically, I set up the system as though making an exposure of a hologram, checked the noise from the system, then measured the power, let the system settle, then did an exposure. Once the setting has been determined, I have been lazy and don't check for mode hopping each time. I'm fortunate in having an exposure area that is at constant temperature, so I don't have to adjust the system each time.

It's worth checking the archives here, as the discussions that Colin mentions explain how to do this in depth, and are very helpful.
