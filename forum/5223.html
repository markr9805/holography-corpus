I thought I posted this somewhere but could not find it so I thought I would start another thread on it.

I have always noticed when I add just a little extra light to the object when making a Denisyuk (I know it's not really a Denisyuk if I am adding additional object lighting) the hologram comes out much brighter. By accident I found an interesting technique.

Usually I try to squeeze in the extra light from the side but I always have a problem that the object is too close to the plate in a Denisyuk set up or I have to back the object away from the plate to get extra illumination to the front of the object which I really don't want to do most of the time. Plus it is from the side and for even additional illumination both sides would need to be done.

So I was making a Denisyuk and I tried to make a transmission grating at the same time. I did this by setting up a standard Denisyuk and then adding a large flat mirror on the reference side of the plate at the bottom and lying such that spill light that missed the plate at the bottom would be reflected up to the plate from the bottom. What I found is that the transmission grating was not noticeably visible when viewing the reflection hologram but the hologram what much brighter when turned upside down and still viewing a virtual image but with the reference from the bottom mirror. My conclusion is this reflected reference beam was dimmer the main reference that went directly to the plate but the light reflected off the object was the combination of both reference beams, the one from the top and the one from the bottom. I just never thought about adding additional lighting to the object right through the plate.

So if anyone is trying single beam reflections give this a try and let me know what you find. I will try it again soon myself. This would be a very nice way of making brighter Denisyuks without additional beam splitters, mirrors and spatial filters (lenses). Only one larger flat first surface mirror is needed which are very cheap and can be found in old overhead projectors.

Tony Sat Dec 03, 2005 11:38 am #16
I tried this in silver last night.

It just so happens I was doing some SBR experiments at the time this thread started. My control hologram, using standard SBR techniques, was made on 8E75 with a red Hene laser. As usual, it was not too bright, and fairly noisy. The film was developed in D-19, bleached with FE EDTA bleach, dried and spray painted flat black on the emulsion side.

Then I tried JohnFP's setup. It was fairly easy, just adding a flate front surface mirror at the bottom edge of the plate. I did not carefully set the angle of the extra mirror as there were some limitations of my setup, so it came out about 80 degrees to the film instead of 90.

The extra mirror reflected some of the beam back up onto the film and provided some additional light on the object ( a flat white porcelain figure). The light from the extra mirror covered a large area in front of the subject, but it did not completely overlap the area having just the original beam. This was useful for comparisons later.

Film was exposed and developed the same way.

Because this was a top lit reflection hologram, normal reconstruction would be with the illumination from above. I'll define some angles as follows: from the top edge of the film is 0 degrees. Normal to the film is +90 degrees. From the bottom edge of the film is +180 degrees.

On reconstruction, here is what I observed:

1. When viewing the original image in the normal way, illumination at around 34 degrees, viewing angle 90 degrees, the image was not as bright as the control hologram. I was able to see that the image was dimmer in the area of the second reference beam. It was brighter where there was no second reference.

2. When viewing the second image, illumination was from +146, viewing angle +90. This image was also dimmer than the control holgram, and dimmer than #1 above as well. This brightness of this image was proportional to the brightness of the second reference, and got dimmer as the second reference fell off due to the gaussian beam shape.

3. diffraction gratings were visible when viewing angle = illumination angle at +34 and +136 degrees.

4. There is a third image, brighter than the #1 or #2 above, but still a bit dimmer than the control hologram. It was visible when illumination was +90 degrees and viewing angle = + 170 degees (very oblique angle). This image only appeared where both beams overlapped.

My Conclusions:

In this experiment consisting of a single data point, I was unable to find any improvement in a Denisyuk hologram on a silver material when adding a second reference beam. It's my opinion that if the limitations of the dynamic range of the medium are considered, adding a separate beam does not produce a brighter Denisyuk, as the total energy of the illumination light is split into multiple output images, none of which are brighter than the original SBR image.

Tony Wed Dec 07, 2005 1:47 pm #21
I'm not sure how to explain the example of multi channel holograms. (I've never tried to make one). If each channel is truly as bright as a single channel hologram, then it seems somehow we're getting 'something for nothing'.

Maybe in the case of multichannel holograms, the trick is that the exposures are not made with both reference beams at the same time, so they cannot interfere to form a grating.

As for the case of the beams 'stealing' light from each other, if each of the two beams is 56 degrees from normal to the plate, then they form a grating consisting of Bragg planes which would parallel to the plate. Illumination from either of the original reference beam directions would not be perpendicular to the fringes, and some light would be diffracted along the direction of the other reference beam. (This is a simple HOE grating mirror). Conservation of energy considerations would demand that this light is not available for conversion into an image.

There's clearly two processes going on here, one for exposure/development and another for reconstruction. In the exposure portion, there is a limited dynamic range of transmission amplitude or phase modulation available - and if a large portion of this is used in the creation of the grating between the reference beams, it seems like there would be less left over for creation of image-producing fringes. I think this limitation on dynamic range would also apply to the mutlichannel hologram, except as noted above, no grating would be formed between reference beams.
BobH Wed Dec 07, 2005 4:35 pm #22
> wouldn't a 2 channel silver hologram only have the capacity to be only 1/2 as bright as a single channel hologram and a three channel only 1/3 as bright? Maybe it is, I am not sure.

The diffraction efficiency of an image is inversely related to the amount of information being recorded. More information, less DE. If you make a "2-channel" image-planed hologram where the total size of the combined master is the same as a "single channel" hologram, both should have similar DE.

An image-planed hologram made with a small master (ala John Kaufmann and Lon Moore) will generally be brighter than the same image shot with a larger master for this same reason.

If you have two reference beams going as JohnFP suggests, you're recording more information and the DE will decrease. However, if the two reference beams are P-polarized and at 90 degrees to each other (inside the emulsion), there will be no grating formed between them. In this case, the resulting decrease in beam ratio might bring the DE up enough to overcome the decrease caused by the "fogging light" represented by the second reference beam. I think that DCG probably has the dynamic range for this to occur, but not silver halide.

There are two possible explainations for Tony's results that I can think of because I cannot see shadows from the bottom reference beam where there should be shadows. First, the second reference beam was not nearly strong enough to make enough difference combined with exposure time and processing. It could have very well been that in his set up additional or less exposure time was needed for the hologram to be as bright as his base line hologram. I think a next test the following should be performed. That is, to cover half the plate with the a card and block off the bottom light. The cover the other half and open the bottom light. This gives a great base line with all parameters the same. Second, it could have been that the coherence length was exceeded for the bottom reference beam. Note that the beam has to travel further to the bottom mirror then the light directly to the plate. It then has to travel from the mirror to the plate, then the usual from plate to object and back again. This could add inches. This would also explain why a shadow was not observed on the object in the hologram from the bottom light (coherence exceeded) but the plate received more light and the interference between the two reference beams was preserved.
