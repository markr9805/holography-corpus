I know we touched breifly on this a while ago and Jeff B. informed me of the only necessary chemicals needed in the fix bath for DCG. As I purchased these a while ago, I thought it would be nice to open the discussion back up and get to it in the lab.

Here is what I have so far.

I plan to mix 2 grams of Aluminum Sulfate per 150ml water to be used as a hardener.

I plan to mix 2 grams of Sodium Metabisulfite per 150ml water to be used as the Dichromate reducer.

Alkali removes the hardening (of Kodak fixer or Aluminum Sulfate), make DCG less sensitive and increases storage time.

Acid increases hardening, make the DCG more sesitive and decreases storage time.

Alum decreases swell of gelatin through bonding but if emulsion is too swollen, no or less crosslinking is achieved.

Swelling is mimimized at 4.5 ph.

6.5ph good starting poing with nice swelling with addequate hardening and good sensitivity.

Now, separating these processes makes me think.

First I believe I will soak the plate in sodium metabisulfite to get rid of the unexposed Dichromate. I am not sure if I can adjust the ph of this bath but I know I can adjust temperature to change the swelling amount.

Then place the plate in the Aluminum Sulfate to harden the unexposed regions. Now since the unexposed regions have been swollen (the exposed regions do not swell due to heavy crosslinking), the croslinking in the unexposed regions will be much more spread out due to point number 3 above. This is what makes the "honeycomb" latice support network in the unexposed regions in which the water in between this latice network will be ultimately replace by air.

Then process in alcohol and dry.

I am really excited about trying these two chemical independently! I think I am really starting to understand the mechanics of what happens to the amino strand (gelatin) from cooking to drying. Of couse all of the above is only a starting point and concentrations, temps, ph, which bath to use first, etc will need to be worked out.

acidic gas sulfur dioxide(SO2). So you can smell SO2 a bit in solutions of

sodium metabisulfite which are automatically acid as you imply, so adding sodium carbonate will raise pH and take it back towards being sodium sulfite which in neutral or alkaline solution is not smelly. Sodium sulfite is a less active reducing agent than the metbisulfite, but even so it is a rapid

reactor with dichromate.

Aluminum Sulfate

A problem with Alum is that you cannot get its pH up to neutral level

with sodium carbonate without an aluminum carbonate precipitate forming so

you may have to settle for no higher than pH 5. But As a matter of interest What is the pH of your Kodak fix solution you normally have used please?

Joe Farina Thu Nov 01, 2007 6:03 pm #15
> Dutchelm05: During post exposure, there is a time when the DCG film is left in the dark for 5-10 minutes. What is that doing?

Exactly what happens is a pretty speculative question, I'd say. But I'll throw in my two cents worth.

I look at exposure as a way of speeding-up the normal dark-reaction. DCG has the normal tendency to get harder as time passes. Exposing it with light accelerates the hardening effect in those areas. An oddity of DCG is that, once the light is removed, those areas that previously "saw the light" remain pretty excited and stay that way for a long time.

In that excited state, the hardening rate is faster, compared to areas that did not see the light. The dark areas are also getting harder, but at a much slower rate (i.e., the "normal" dark-reaction).

The excited areas gradually get less and less excited as time passes (after the light has been removed). They are most excited immediately after exposure, then, ever so slowly, they get less and less excited. Seconds after the light is removed, the rate of hardening in the exposed areas FAR outstrips the normal dark-reaction hardening of the unexposed areas. So most of the benefits gained by leaving the DCG in the dark after exposure occur in (say) the first 5 minutes, then continually get weaker over the next hours, then even weaker over the next days, and still weaker over the next weeks. So after a month, the exposed areas may still be getting harder at a faster rate compared to the unexposed areas. But the difference may be quite small, perhaps negligible. Leaving it alone for the first 5 or 10 minutes is probably the most helpful from a practical standpoint, but I have a paper that says the longer, the better. They were even getting better diffraction efficiency after leaving them alone for a month before processing them.

A good question ! ----- It needs a chemist to do it justice because it is complicated.

Although it straightforwardly means it reacts slowly with gelatin without light being involved as Joe suggests it does need some explaining. If you are not interested in the finer chemistry detail then stop reading here .

Most oxidizing agents such as oxygen in the air, nitrates (saltpetre as in gunpowder etc) , and chlorates can be mixed with combustible materials and just sit there inactive virtually forever unless something such as a lighted match gives them that vital spark which triggers the chain reaction and rapid burn up. So these oxidants need what’s called “activation energy” as a kick start. However in the case of dichromates the chromium is a member of what is known in the Periodic Table as “transition metals” . These are inclined to have the peculiar ability to indulge in low activity with little activation energy which is why they are used universally as catalysts for low temperature reactions. They have variable valency which is why we have referred in this forum to CrVI going to Cr V going to Crlll The transition metal iron in our blood is doing this sort of low temperature oxidation work for us of course too. The transition metal effect is to do with atomic orbitals where the electrons have a large array of complicated empty orbitals to whizz about in some of the time and sort of get through barriers without having to be kicked to jump over the normal activation energy barrier that non- transition metal ions have to do.

A non- transition metal such as say Aluminum which is always Al 111 in

our water based alum chemistry here and cannot be reduced or oxidized to a different valency but it does make complexes with the gelatin and hardens it (but not quite as strongly as Cr111).

So back to dichromate which has 6 electrons missing from its uncombined metallic state. The electrons have been taken mainly by 3 oxygen atoms in a not very stable arrangement and these electrons are actively whizzing around the Cr atom’s empty orbitals as well as their main base around the oxygen atoms. Energy is gained for the system if they can return permanently to the Cr atom by being instrumental in getting the oxygens to swap them over by taking other electrons in organic groups in the gelatin to

give more stable arrangements producing partially oxidized gelatin.

So the dark reaction of dichromate is primarily a matter of oxidation of the gelatin without a kick start with light energy or extra heat and it can be slowed down in a ‘fridge but needs to be in a freezer to really slow it down.

Incidentally the less pure the dichromate the more it contains other transition

metals such as copper and the more it enables this catalysed oxidizing effect to occur in the dark. More acidity also increases it which is why the more acidic ammonium salt in unexposed gelatin film gives it a shorter shelf life than the potassium salt.

In any case there is no sharp pH point where dichromate is fully turned into chromate , it is gradual transition as you go up the scale. So at pH

9 say there is still some dichromate and if you remove it then more chromate will break down to replace it because it is in equilibrium at that pH. To understand this more, first we need to consider the importance that Chromic acid has as it comes into this chromate/dichromate business as follows.

Chromic acid (made from chromium trioxide and water: CrO3 + H20 ----> H2CrO4 note it now has 2 H's replaceable by a metal ion or ammonium ion)

It reacts with bases such as potassium hydroxide to give the potassium chromate salt :

H2CrO4 + 2KOH -----> K2 CrO4.

This chromate salt has the ability to combine loosely with another molecule of the acid to make dichromate salt like this apparently:-

K2CrO4 + H2CrO4 ------> K2Cr2O7 + H2O where the formula for dichromate is what you get when you evaporate the water off but

in water it is in reality K2CrO4.H2CrO4 so it has 2 acid hydrogens and it is an acid salt although the straight formula on the bottle K2Cr2O7 does not look like an acid with replaceable hydrogens.

pH reading close to 9. So what this means is that acetic acid is weaker than Chromic acid . ie. the stronger the acid the more the pH of its potassium or sodium salts tend to be neutral. So HCl gives neutral KCl and very weak carbonic acid gives alkaline potassium carbonate and ultimately

Hans Thu Nov 08, 2007 9:00 am #37
Great experiments John,

Perhaps there is some competition between swelling and hardening taking place in the plate after all:

- Plate submerged in Alum only: Alum hardens the plate all over, but washes some of the Cr(V) out of the emulsion before it can be hardened by the SM. => weak fringe structure and milkiness.

- Plate submerged in SM only: SM hardens the fringes, but at the same time there is some uneven swelling because of the water penetrating and swelling the gelatin at the same time. => random fringe spacing and more milkiness.

- Plate submerged in mix of SM+Alum: Alum very quickly hardens the plate throughout a little bit, allowing the original fringe structure to be preserved when the SM hardens the fringes.
