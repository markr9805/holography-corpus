Joe Farina Fri Apr 18, 2014 7:07 am #2
The exterior dimensions of my lab measure 14 feet (4.3 meters) by 22 feet (6.7 meters), which is 308 square feet. That would be 28.8 square meters. The interior space is less, especially since I have some baffle-walls of 2-inch styrofoam with an air gap of about 1-foot in the room with the isolation table (to insulate the table from the electric baseboard heaters which heat the building). The interior (usable) space is about 240 square feet. It was formerly a 1-car detached garage.

Ideally, and in my opinion of course, the ideal arrangement is a detached garage with a concrete slab. One-car is enough space (with careful planning of the interior layout). Two-car is the ideal. Basements can of course be forced into "unwilling compliance" but a detached building is better.

BobH Sat Apr 19, 2014 9:00 am #7
The size of the laser is generally insignificant to the size of the lab/studio needed for CW holography (except for those using a large framed ion laser, or a few of them). Going from a he-ne, he-cd, or small framed ion laser to a diode version of the same makes no difference.
Joe Farina Sat Apr 19, 2014 12:30 pm #8
> BobH: The size of the laser is generally insignificant to the size of the lab/studio needed for CW holography (except for those using a large framed ion laser, or a few of them). Going from a he-ne, he-cd, or small framed ion laser to a diode version of the same makes no difference.

Yes I would have to agree, in most cases. Diode-pumped solid state lasers do save some space, in comparison to their Argon counterparts, but this isn't what makes the difference. It's the cost and complexity. Generally, Argon lasers need water cooling, three-phase power, and an etalon. The green solid-state Coherent 315M doesn't need any of this, just a small power supply, small controller unit, small laser head, and an adequate heat sink for the head and controller. Typically about 100mW of single-frequency (no beam measuring needed) power is available for a surplus cost of roughly $2k. The same can be said for the Melles 457nm solid-state blue laser for about $6k. There are several "competing" sources of red light: HeNe's, diode systems, and (gasp) Krypton. The Krypton laser would be more suitable to a commercial enterprise making large holograms, a heavy price is paid for that quantity of red light. I've found that the largest HeNe's are suitable for my needs, they are reliable lasers.

There are many topics that could be discussed if you and Graham want feedback from practicing holographers, it might be worthwhile to post some topics here on the Holoforum.
