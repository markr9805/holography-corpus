[quote:3899aaf005]Instead of using a beam splitter in a transmission hologram, can you just use two lasers?[/quote:3899aaf005]

Light comes out of the laser as a set of waves, bits of wiggles, if you like. Imagine spaghetti noodles flying through space while wiggling. These sets of waves are known as "wavetrains". The length of a wavetrain is known as the "coherence length". The laser emits wavetrains all pretty much of the same length but the intervals between each wavetrain is random. When you make a split beam hologram, you make a copy of a wavetrain coming from the laser ("split the beam") which you send along a different path. So long as either the two paths, one for each wavetrain, are exactly the same , or the difference between them is within the length of a wavetrain, then the two wavetrains recombine. If the two paths are exactly the same, the two wavetrains exactly recombine, if the paths are not exactly the same but within the length of the wavetrain (the "coherence length" of your laser) parts of one wavetrain will combine with parts of the other. In this case, the two wavetrains will interfere. However, since the gap between wavetrains are not the same, if the two paths are differ by more than the coherence length, then one wavetrain may, or may not combine with another wavetrain behind it. One spaghetti string may or may not meet another spaghetti string running a little behind. Usually not so you'll get no interference. In the case of two lasers, it's impossible to tie the wavetrains of the two seperate lasers together. Each laser produces wavetrains at seperate intervals and so any wavetrain from one laser will not match a wavetrain from another. Well, it's actually not "impossible". As edelbrp pointed out, it's possible to "coherently couple" two laser so that each laser produces a set of wavetrains together. Effectively, each laser says to the other, "One, two, three launch!" and off go both wavetrains simultaneously! This is, however, not easy to do.
