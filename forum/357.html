> 142laser: OK jeff so what is the best safe light for this media? Incandescant with a good green filter? Mercury does have a red line even with a green filter and a UV absorber so that is no good.

Hello 142,

Johnfp beat me to the punch on this one. LED Christmas lights on a dimmer. That's my favorite. They come in red and green. Look for the kind that have some other similar colored plastic thing over the LED. So if the LED's themselves are red, look for something that also has red balls, or berry's or whatever over them.

That extra filter will cut out any stray lines you don't want. Green LED's have a little red light in them, the green plastic thing will get rid of that. The dimmer is important, just use a regular type designed to go into a wall switch box. Make sure to insulate the connections well. The LED's will probably be too bright without it.

The main reason I like the LED's is you can string them over the whole table. Back and forth in rows on the ceiling. If you use a standard safe light, one bright light in the middle of the room, you have a super bright spot and it falls off quickly so the other parts of the room/table are in the dark. Even worse, that single light makes such major shadows over everything its hard to see all the sides of your equipment.

With the LED string and dimmer combo you have a nice soft even glow everywhere. No shadows anymore.

Next is the classic holographers switch. Take a pull cord switch. The ball chain type that has a spring return. You can get them in any ace hardware for just a few bucks. Mount that over the side of the table you work mostly from. Mount it on the wall about 2 feet over your head. Now tie a string to the pull ball chain and connect it to the other side of the lab. On the opposing side the switch is mounted to. Make it so there's a little bit of tension. Just enough to keep the string pretty straight.

Hook the switch up to the white lights in the room. Not the safe light, you can leave that on all the time, the room's white light. Now you can turn the room light on and off from anywhere along the tables length by just reaching up and pulling on the string.

Much easier than going to the wall switch 50 times a day.

Jeffrey Weil Sat Oct 15, 2011 8:35 am #11
Hello TomB,

I like using the classic methods. What I do to check my safe lights is cut up a piece of film or plate into small pieces. I put a coin on top, leave it there under the safe lights for much longer than I would ever have to leave a real exposure under that light. I then develop it for much longer than I would ever develop a real exposure.

If I cannot see the outline of that coin, the light is safe. Even if you used math or another indirect method of figuring out if the light is safe you would still need to do a test like this to confirm the results. I find it easier just to go straight to the real world test.

There is a line of red in a green led but those plastic berry filters do the job nicely of cutting it out.

Jeff W

That's undoubtedly the safest way of using safe lights.

That said, we may be stressing far too much on the "safety" aspect of our safe lights.

There's an interesting 1992 SPIE paper, written by Valery Petrov, "Reflection and transmission silver-halide holograms recorded and processed in white light" (SPIE 1732).

In his conclusions, Petrov points out:

> Both reflection and transmission holograms may be recorded and processed in presence of rather intense incoherent light. But the same levels of white light illumination during recording and processing have different influence on resulting DE. Given high value of DE may be obtained in spite of very intense incoherent illumination during photographic processing. The influence of white light illumination during recording may be reduced significantly by applying much greater coherent light intensity in lesser time. (p. 734)

His experiments were based on PFG-03 emulsions.

Ed Wesly Thu Oct 20, 2011 9:46 am #17
Depends on which red. It was called Agfa 8E75 because its sensitivity extended to 750 nanometers wavelength. I had shot feasibility holograms on it with at first a 25 mW Spindler and Hoyer 780 nm SLM Diode, then shot big versions of the Holodar HOE with a 1 Watt Ti:Sapphire. As to be expected, it needed about 4 to 8 times as much light at 780 nm as it did at 633 nm. So there is some sensitivity to the near IR, as usual doing a safelight test or IR test as Jeff suggested above will prove the practicality.

The color of the appropriate safelight for the Agfa 8E- and 10E- 75 materials is more of a cyan than a true green if you look at the Spectral Sensitivity Curve on their brochure. If you don’t have this antique holographic incunabula, check out [http://nlutie.com/ewesly/AgfaBrochure.PDF](http://nlutie.com/ewesly/AgfaBrochure.PDF) . You will see the curve dips at approximately 500 – 510 nm, more like a cyan than a leaf green.

And if you look further to the right on the curve, you will observe that curiously these emulsions have about equal sensitivity at 633 and 532 nm! I shot some gratings on 10E75 film with both my He-Ne and Compass 315, and it needed about 20 microJoules/cm^2 at both lambdas! So Jem’s trick of using a 532 nm laser for a safelight revolves more around the intensity level being low enough not to fog rather than the color blindness of the emulsion.

I haven’t been using a laser for a safelight when using the Sphere-S GEO-3 emulsion you have heard me raving about, but a green LED, even though the emulsion is panchromatic! It is so slow, and our eyes see green the easiest, so I can get away with the intensity being feeble enough not to fog but strong enough for me to see my way around. As always, testing proves whether you are on the right track or not. For instance, these same green bulbs are OK for 8E75HD and Harman Red and PFG-01 and 03M, but not for 10E75!
