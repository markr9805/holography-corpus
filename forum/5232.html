Ed Wesly Fri Dec 02, 2005 10:31 pm #3
> What was "special" about Omnidex that set it apart from the other dry-film photopolymers?

What made it "special" was that it was a real pain to work with! DuPont would send the stuff in sheets that were composed of a base sheet, the polymer, and a cover sheet. To use, you would remove the cover sheet, and try to roller the exposed polymer onto a clean glass plate. This proved to be more difficult than it sounds, very rarely getting it down without bubbles.

Because it was an anaerobic reaction (oxygen inhibits the polymerization) you would have to leave the base sheet on top of the sandwich during exposure. Unfortunately this stuff was birefringent.

You would expose it in a typical SBR mode. The light sensitive coating was comprised of the monomer and a web-like structure, kind of like little balls in a pile of spaghetti would be the easiest way to describe it. During the exposure the monomers would combine into polymers in the bright fringes, and the monomers in the dim areas would migrate into the bright areas. When you took the plate out of the set up, the holographic image would be there.

Sometimes during the exposure a curious thing would happen; you'd be looking at the plate on the object, and the object would "disappear" due to destructive interference of the reconstructed half-done holographic image and the object's wavefront! Very real-time interferometry!

Then the hologram would be exposed to UV to polymerize any residual monomers. Conceivably you could just expose it holographically until all monomers were polymerized, but this was just to be on the safe side.

This image wasn't real bright; the next step was to bake the plate until something happened to the polymer so that it changed its refractive index and you got higher modulation. This unleashed a nasty odor.

I have the dubious distinction of being the first to make a true color hologram on their true color material. It was either 1989 or 1990 when DuPont revived their polymer line. (There was an earlier version that was a liquid that you could coat, available from Newport in the '70's) Although they had made the material, the only multi-colored exposures they had made were red, green and blue images on different parts of one plate. The object I used was a Matrushka doll that Denisyuk himself gave to my son. The holo was the size of a 35 mm slide, because the exposure doses were 20 to 40 milliJoules in the blue and green, but a whopping 160 milliJoules in the red, meaning a 1000X greater exposure than Agfa 8E75HD!!!

Unfortunately the management of DuPont's holgraphic group was a little out of touch with the holographic marketplace. My research grant at LFC was supported by Abbott Labs, the intent being to replace conventional optics in their analytical instruments with HOE's. Abbott would have liked to use the duPont stuff to manufacture the HOE's, but duPont wanted a piece of the action, not just supplying the materials! Ridiculous! It's like Kodak wanting a royalty off of photographs that were shot on Kodachrome!

They seem to keep that silly mindset, as now they are not supplying the material to anyone except qualified security vendors. Great, what are they going to sell, 100 m^2 a year?

Although I had some experience with the material, it never impressed me as being significantly brighter than silver halide. And it was kind of noisy, possibly because of the web like structure.

I have seen some brigher ones done by artists such as Patrick Boyd and Nancy Gorglione, although they were monochromatic. Rich Rallison, that little old HOE maker was also a proponent of the material.

At the moment I am not so sure what is going on with this stuff. They had made a mass replicator, details of which are in SPIE and LFC ISDH symposia, but whether is running or in mothballs, who knows.

Probably the most accessible example of it is on one of the Holography Marketplace books, maybe even the last one. I saw a stack of them at a place in town called American Science and Surplus, [http://www.sciplus.com/](http://www.sciplus.com/)

Enough for now!

JohnFP Mon Sep 11, 2006 11:26 am #14
I have never used pre-made resists. We used to coat our own. Unlike DCG, resist coatings are much thinner.

In resist the only fringes needed are the ones on the surface, the surface relief fringes. We used to spin our plates in excess of 1000rpms as a contrast to the 80rpm for DCG. Usually a thickness of only a micron or two was desired. A gallon would last years unless you were in full production. 5 gallons should last a lifetime.

We did expose with a HeCd because yes, resist is most sensitive to the UV and deeper blues. I don't remember the exact energy but the HeCd was about 20mW, we split the beam and the object beam was expanded to 18-20" to illuminate the 16"x3" (two slit channels) and the other reference beam was expanded to about 6-7" to illuminate the resist directly. Our exposures were about 1/2 hour.

[http://www.townetech.com/shipley.htm](http://www.townetech.com/shipley.htm)

Processing was very quick and it was important not to over process or you would start etching away parts that were supposed to remain. Usually as soon as the hologram would appear in the etching solution, then another 5 to 15 seconds by trial and error was the best (if I remember correctly). We used a dim white unfrosted bulb over the processing tray to view the hologram while developing.
Ed Wesly Mon Sep 11, 2006 2:39 pm #15
It would be of interest if you could remember the time of development for your resist. The last place I worked the rule of thumb was a devatatingly fast 8 seconds, yep, you read that right! It seems like tht was a relatively well-kept secret in photo-resist holography circles, as when I was investigating on my own before that job I started with tens of seconds of development.

As usual, whatever works to get the results you want is best. But the relation between exposure and development in resist is still a frontier for exploration for me. But when I get my source of blue photons!

Here's another mind-blower re: resist in holography, that kind of goes against the grain or the conventional wisdom espoused in this forum. And that is we were shooting with a 40:1 ratio in the transfer! You read that right, 40 times more reference beam than the highlight in the real image plane! And it wasn't just germane to this set up, as devised by Craig Newswanger; an addition to the staff also said that it was the house rule at his former employers!

So there is still much to be learned about the holographic process!
