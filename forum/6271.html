I was wondering if anyone has tried these for object lighting. They seem to offer two important benefits: more efficient use of light due to the limited divergence of the diffused beam, and some kind of polarization retention. I was reading on the Edmund Optics FAQ (which is quite useful by the way) that holographic diffusers "retard" polarization, but they didn't say how exactly. If it acted like a half-wave plate, that would be good. Even if it acted like a quarter-wave plate, I suppose one could insert another quarter-wave plate in front, so that both optics together would behave as a half-wave plate. Although these Edmund diffusers are expensive, each plastic optic could easily be cut into a multitude of smaller ones.
BobH Wed May 23, 2007 6:46 pm #2
I've used holograms of diffusers to make stereograms and for object lighting a few times. They were always homemade on silver halide material, so were polarization preserving.

Really helps for prevention of stray light going all over the place as with a normal diffuser. The downside is that they are generally custom made for a particular set-up, and really need to have high DE to not waste too much of that expensive laser light.

BobH Thu May 24, 2007 1:46 pm #6
Those diffusers are from Physical Optics Corporation (actually the spin-off company). I use them all the time. They are also referred to as "light shaping diffusers" and are made by a holographic method. The hologram diffusers I mentioned above are actually holograms of diffusers.

The POC diffusers' directional and/or shaped lobe of diffusion makes them more efficient than ground glass or other wide lobe diffusers in a hologram recording system. Their "high" cost will probably be waaaay lower than the cost of the photons they save, but only in a studio that's actually making holograms.

> Joe: I was reading on the Edmund Optics FAQ (which is quite useful by the way) that holographic diffusers "retard" polarization, but they didn't say how exactly. If it acted like a half-wave plate, that would be good. Even if it acted like a quarter-wave plate, I suppose one could insert another quarter-wave plate in front, so that both optics together would behave as a half-wave plate.

By retard they mean that it makes a 1/n plate and the value for n is likely to be anything between 2 and 4 depending on the wavelength, angle and optic. What this means to us is your linear polarization is going to be eliptical by a certain degree and you won't know how much till you measure it.

I guess you could rotate it in front of a polarizer to learn just how eliptical it is. It might be usefull for lowering the glare from a highlight to rotate the holographic diffuser, I have not tried. It works well with 1/2 wave plates.

Just a side note on specular reflections, Bright reflections on a H1 or H2 near the film plane overload the abilities of the film and cause "burn in". You can deal with them by adding a 1/2 wave plate to the object beam and rotating the polarization of the specular reflections so it does not form fringes as well or move the image plane front or backwards to get the reflection out of the film plane. I find even 1/8" can make a difference. (We have talked about this a lot. If anyone wants to learn more search for burn in.)
