My only suggestion, having not actually tried this, is to place a polarizer over the LCD screen. Then take another polarizer and look through it at the screen. When you find the brightest image of the screen the mark the orientation of the polarizer you are looking through. Then take that polarizer and put it in the reference beam keeping the same polarization. Remove the seconds polarizer, the one you looked through and is not in the reference beam and shoot the hologram, but leave the first polarizer in that is on the LCD screen.

Ed Wesly Tue May 27, 2008 9:03 pm #4
For the first time in my life I just got some prescription sunglasses with Polaroid filters, and it’s been amazing. The glasses have their polarizing picket fences (if you follow my drift) oriented vertically, as they are designed to block what would be the most common hazard for a driver, horizontally polarized light reflecting off water, pavement, windows and windshields, etc.

But I can still see laptops and other LCD displays with them, as their polarization vectors are oriented at something like 45 degrees from the vertical, a testimonial to the wisdom of the producers of such displays. So this can cause some confusion as to the holographic problem at hand.

The Liquid Crystals are switchable retardation plates, either ¼ or ½, depending on the application. If they are used in a transmission mode, then they are ½ wave, so they take the light polarized by the input polarizer and rotate the polarization so that it can pass through the second filter, oriented orthogonally to the first.

In the case of passive displays like on calculators, (no internal light source), there is a polarizing filter on top, then the LCD array acting as ¼ wave retardation plates, to circularly polarize the light, if turned on, then it reflects off a somewhat specular backing plate to preserve polarization and go through the ¼ LCD pixel a second time, so that light is either blocked or passed depending on the state of the LCD.

So how is the input laser light polarized? Does it match the polarizing vectors of the filters? This needs to be done, and of course a retardation plate would help rotate the vectors into compliance. Then there would be no need for an input polarizer for the LCD, except if the laser were not polarized.

But there would be a need for a polarizer on the output side of the LCD array. This is where the contrast will happen between a switched on and switched off pixel.

I am just guessing at this point about the set up, but from here the screen is imaged onto a screen via a lens? As has been noted before in this forum, ground glass does preserve polarization, while other milkier back projection screens don’t. Use a polarizing filter to check the status of your screen and the orientation of the output polarization. And of course match the same polarizing angle in the reference beam.

Taking a second look at holography4fun’s post, it looks like a diffuser comes before the LCD screen. The above info is just as important in this case, as the light needs to be polarized before hitting the LCD, so I would suggest ground glass instead of the milky white flashed opal.

So if I am reading into this correctly, you have been doing a basic one step rainbow with lens and backlighting a transparency with a diffuser of some sort. Make sure the diffuser preserves polarization. Make sure the backlighting provides the same polarization alignment as the filter that came with LCD screen. Make sure you have a polarizing filter on the output side of the LCD screen.

Have fun!

Ed Wesly Fri May 30, 2008 2:59 pm #7
Congratulations on your results! It's always hard to figure out what someone is doing from verbal descriptions. The best way to learn is by playing with the setup!

Good idea to peak the image contrast by rotating the polarizer, as mentioned above. Removing the polarizer would of course wipe out the image, as our eye cannot see different states of polarization without an aid.

> My assumption was that if i shoot it like that and then i develop it, there will be no image, and then if i place a polariser on my eye and i rotate it viewing the developed plate, i would see the image.
Not a good assumption! Holographic materials (with the exception of one, see another thread I will start later) do not preserve polarization! So this wouldn't have worked!

> To my surprise, on the developed plate, there was a clear image of the object. !
Actually this shouldn't have been so surprising! The 1/2 wave retarding liquid crystals rotate the polarization by 90 degrees when they are turned on, so there is no interference with the reference beam occuring with those pixels! So with the front and back polarizers eliminated, the image should be cleaner!

As a matter of curiousity, it might be interesting to try with your set up, if you can control the polarization of the reference beam separately from the LCD's path, to rotate the polarization of the reference beam by 90 degrees and see if you get the complementary pixels to make an image, kind of like a negative!

Please post your results and pictures of your set up if you can!
