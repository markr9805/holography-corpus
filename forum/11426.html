I think I was the first person to use a dot matrix machine at American Banknote Holographics in 1994; since that was 30 years ago, I haven't any idea how they've progressed since then. However, based on my knowldege from 1994:

If you make a hologram with just two beams - no object, just two beams - you get what's called a 'grating'. If you make such a hologram on material such as photoresist, then such a hologram will give you a colour based on the illumination angle - as you turn the hologram you'll see different colours. In a dot matrix machine, a single unexpanded beam is sent down a tube. At the end of the tube is a beam splitter, splitting the beam into two beams, one beam continuing down the tube, and the other coming off at right angles to a mirror and being redirected. Where the two beams meet, there is a photoresist plate (see diagram below). The tube can be rotated, so the plane of the two beams is also rotated. The rotation angle of the tube - the plane of the two beams - is determined by some digital image, back in 1994 it was Photoshop. What basically happens (happened?) is the angle was set by the digital pixel value, and the digital pixel value was set by the colour on the digital image. So, for example, the pixel value for orange in the RGB colour space is R = 107, G = 80, B = 0; this particular pixel value would rotate the tube to a specific angle and record a single 'dot/pixel' on the hologram. If this particular dot/pixel on the hologram is viewed by a light source at the specific angle, it would appear orange. If viewed by a light source at any other angle, it would change colour.

The photoresist plate is set on an X-Y translation stage. In the machine I worked on in 1994, the first dot/pixel recorded is the top left pixel on the digital image. The tube was rotated to whatever the value of the first pixel on the digital image was, a shutter opened, and the first dot/pixel was recorded on the resist plate. Then the plate was translated along the X direction (sideways), the tube was rotated according to the pixel value on the next pixel in the digital image, and the next dot was recorded. This process continued - shifting the plate along the X direction, rotating the tube, and exposing, until the last pixel on the first line in the digital image was recorded. Then the plate went back to the zero position in X, and incremented in the Y, and the machine would start on the next line of pixels on the digital image. As the machine scanned the digital image as a raster, the hologram dots were recorded as a raster, with different planes of the two beams. If you viewed the hologram from any specific light source, all the recorded pixels in the raster would show a different colour because the tube - and hence the plane of recording - was different for each recorded dot.

The reason you see rainbow colours is that the recording plane is rotated, so the direction of the reference is altered for each pixel - note the angle between ref and object is set by the side mirror (45 deg in the 1994 machine) but the direction is varied. Now, given any illumination source, the different directions of the recorded dots will show up in difrferent colours - each dot appears as a different colour; remember that all directions will diffract in a resist hologram. Not sure why you see a greyscale image, I can only speculate that you're reconstructing with a very diffuse source, which will give you a grey-ish image. Another possibility is that the "grey" image is not a diffracted image but burn-in, when the dot/grating was burnt into the material by over-developing.

I don't think there are any significant changes since 1994. It's possible the software can pick up any image from any imaging software, ie it's not limited to just Photoshop as it was back then.

> JayFritoeswrote:↑Wed Aug 06, 2025 1:13 pm: So, the tube rotating changes the azimuth angle of the beams, which affects the angle of the grooves on the photoresist? But what about the groove spacing (pitch)? As far as I understand, the groove spacing in a diffraction grating is what controls the color, and the groove angle is what controls the angle at which that color appears.

You're right that the groove spacing controls the colour. But, the groove spacing in a dot matrix is fixed by the mirror/beamsplitter combination, so the interbeam angle, which determines the spacing, is fixed at 45 deg. (for the one I used). However, in a dot matrix, the direction of the two beams controls the colour. As the azimuthal angle is varied, the interbeam angle is always 45 deg but the direction of the "off-normal" beam, the beam coming off the mirror, changes direction. So, if we arbitrarily assign directions of, say, North (N), East (E), South (S) and West (W), then, for example, a specific pixel value of the Photoshop image may twist the splitter/mirror (SM) combination to N, another pixel value may twist the SM combination to some angle between N and E, and still another pixel value may twist the SM combination to S. In all of these cases, the SM combination always impinges light at 45 deg but from different directions. This has the effect of causing a glittering, varying look to the image is rotated and as each dot, referenced at 45 deg but from a different direction, is referenced at the right direction. Some dots come in at certain directions, and, as the hologram is rotated, different dots come in. Since this is a thin/surface hologram, as the hologram is rotated, colours fade in and out as the hologram is rotated; they don't 'wink out' as the hologram is rotated.

You can see the effect on a You Tube video I found. We actually took a video of an actual dot matrix I made in 1996, but can't attach it. I'm assuming you can't attach a video.

> JayFritoeswrote:↑Thu Aug 07, 2025 6:29 pm: Since this was 30 years ago, how complex would it be to DIY a similar laser setup today?

The basic principle is pretty simple. You send a laser beam into a tube with a beamsplitter and attached mirror (the attached mirror is important because this fixes the groove spacing). In our case, the laser was sent horizontally to a mirror that diverted it downwards, ie the beam was diverted to be vertical. This vertical beam was sent into a rotating tube with a platform to hold the beamsplitter, a mirror attached to the platform diverting part of the beam so as to meet the straight through beam at 45 deg.and a hole in the platform to allow the through beam to access a plate. The tube was connected to a stepper motor which was controlled by software. The software "took in" a pixel value from Photoshop, and sent that information to the stepper motor. The stepper motor then rotated the tube through some angle determined by the software. The tricky part is beam alignment and the software. You'd have to access the colour buffer where the bit values for the colour of a specific bit was stored, then send this to the stepper motor controller, where the controller would have a look-up table for angle vs bit value. In our case, the software was written by a student from a local college. If memory serves, the software was written in Pascal. Today, probably writing in C++ may be easier. It may even be possible to have AI write it (Fair disclosure, I'm not a great fan of AI). Also, back then the process was slow, for a 4x4 in (~10cm x 10cm) image, I'd start it on Friday and let it run all weekend; sometimes, it was still running on Monday morning. Today, with more powerful lasers and better stepper motors, it could probably run a lot faster. However, modern imaging software today has up to 32-bit colour, making over a million colour values, which would be tricky for a maximum twist of 360 deg.

> JayFritoeswrote:↑Thu Aug 07, 2025 6:29 pm: I looked into it a bit. It seems like grayscale achromatic holograms require higher-level equipment like an electron beam. Does that sound right?

No, it's made in the conventional process. First, a hologram is made by exposing a plate by a laser to an object/transparency about 8 inches from the plate. This hologram is viewable only in laser light, and is traditionally called an "H1". This H1 is then set to project the image in space, and a second hologram plate is placed in the (projected) image area, this traditionally called the "H2" and is viewable in ordinary light. At this point, the direction and method of reference beam determines what kind of hologram it becomes. If the reference for the H2 comes in from the other side of the H1, the hologram is a reflection hologram. If the reference comes in from the same side as the H2 it's now a transmission H2. If you illuminate the H1 with a single slit of laser light, the transmission H2 reconstructs in bands of colour, called a "rainbow hologram". If the geometry is a transmission geometry, and there's no slit on the H1, you get an achromat.

Here's a good explanation from Alec Jeong of Integraf - my primary source for holographic materials: [https://www.integraf.com/resources/arti ... -holograms](https://www.integraf.com/resources/articles/a-simple-holography-easiest-way-to-make-holograms)

The hologram is a surface/thin hologram, so you get a large range of diffracted colours. The hologram is dispersive, therefore all wavelengths in the source light would disperse. The exact colours, and the exact range vs angle of viewing depends on the exact value of dispersion, which in turn, depends on the way the original was processed, and the surface quality of the bill. In the ABNH dot matrix, we used a He CD laser at a wavelength of 457nm, with an interbeam angle of 45 deg., which gave a spatial frequency (grooves per millimetre) of sin(45)/0.000457 ~ 1094 l/mm. If the bill couldn't take that frequency - which I suspect it can't, you'd lose some dispersion, and therefore some range of colours. For example, dvd's have a groove spacing of 0.74 microns, and so a spatial frequency of ~ 1351 l/mm. If you hold up a dvd to a light source and twist or turn it, you'll see the same (ish) effect as a dot matrix hologram - a range of colours that depend on the angle of viewing, despite a fixed spatial frequency. But, a dvd has a groove depth of 0.14 microns, whereas a resist hologram has, typically, a groove depth of about a micron - so the dispersion of the dot matrix is higher on the plate. But, when it's transferred to paper, you'll lose some spatial frequency and groove depth, which limits the dispersion.

For instance, a dot matrix hologram is composed of small "dots", size typically tens of micrometers. Each dot then contains parallell strips (a grating) of pitch that might be difficult to recognize using a cheap microscope (below 1 um). To your point "...if diffraction inherently splits light into spectral colours": this is not entirely correct. A diffraction grating of suitable pitch and depth causes color dispersion, that's right. But when the grating pitch is coarse, color dispersion may become unnoticeable. On the other hand, when the pitch is very fine (below wavelength), the grating does not make diffraction orders (simply said) and color dispersion does not appear as well. That microphotograph definitely looks like a conventional printing screen. I think there is no reason to assume that the grayscale portrait is treated on the diffractive structure level; it is more likely created using other technology.

One could indeed print that portrait using conventional technologies. If it was black ink, then I doubt you would see negative image in transmission, as black ink absorbs light in both reflection and transmission regime.

Another option might be to attack the reflective layer. To explain: the base of the window with the DOVID is transparent. If you apply a diffractive structure onto it, it would work mainly in transmission, its effect in reflection is very weak. To improve the behaviour in reflection, one applies a metallic layer on top on the structure, for instance a thin layer of aluminium. If you intentionally corrupt the aluminium layer, reflectivity is lost and the spot may look darker than the unaffected neighbourhood.

Yet another option would be to attack the transparent base itself, for instance using strong laser pulse, and burn it locally. That would make the base black throughout the volume. This approach, however, would unlikely lead to a negative image in transmission, I'm just noting that to show there are various techniques employed in security printing. (By the way, such laser heat treatment is used to personalise ID cards, driver licenses etc.)

If you want to learn more about methods used in security printing, check the book Optical Document Security by Robert L. van Renesse.

It's unlikely that a conventional holographic dot matrix would have some sort of diffusive structure overlaid on the gratings. This would imply that the diffusion would carry over to the next "dot", exposing it photographically. If a grating were recorded on top of an already (photographically) exposed area, the modulation would decrease. The result would be some sort of "dot matrix speckle", where the diferent dots would vary in efficiency, and the higher efficiency dots would have a high contrast against the lower efficiency dots. I doubt there would be the smooth transition that you see with dot matrix images.

As for exposing many gratings in a single dot, this is possible if more than one mirror is used (see my previous explanation as to where the single mirror is fixed). However, over and above the problems of alignment of several mirrors and one beamsplitter, you would have cross-talk in any one grating. If this cross-talk spilt over to the next grating, you would see several diffraction patterns.

As I've said (for the fourth? fifth? time now), given any visual effect, there are a variety of ways of accomplishing this; Petr has given several ways of producing the given effect. To find out how this particular effect was actually created, you'd have to examine one in your hands, under a variety of reconstruction geometries. Otherwise, you end up going through a plethora of speculation - finally ending up in a never-ending rabbit hole of speculation. Even if two or more people could actually examine one on hand, there may well be different speculations. It is vitally important to understand that a hologram, in and of itself, is not a hologram, it's a variation of spatial and phase properties on, or in, some structure, such as a thin grating or a Bragg structure. It becomes a hologram if - and only if - it is illuminated by some source with a specific spectral profile and direction. Displaying a "hologram" in a picture or video with no inormation as to the illuminant gives almost no information. Mostly the same applies to the term "holopixel". If you are in a niche of dot matrix, then perhaps it is a common term there, I don't know. I am used to talk about dots and hogels. By a dot I mean a spot with a simple grating within. By a hogel I mean a basic structural element of a certain type of hologram; a hogel is typically composed of several dots. But I don't think such terms are widely accepted, it is always necessary to define terms when starting discussion. I am not surprised Dinesh "does not know what a holopixel is" -- he has been in holography for a couple of decades and has seen how various terms emerge, disappear, change their meaning or are used differently in different applications.

As for how grayscale is done: I fully agree with Dinesh that it's just a rhetoric excersise without detailed examination of the hologram. If you are really interested, try asking someone with a decent optical microscope if you can look at $50 note (try the nearest high school). Observe the hologram both in small and big magnifications. In small ones, such as 20x, try observing the overall structure and try to spot regular properties. In big magnifications such as 100x, use a high numerical aperture lenses, such as 0.95, and observe the details of the microstructure. Moreover, as 100x lenses have very shallow depth of focus, try scanning through the volume of the film to see whether everything is located in a microstructure or if there are several layers or even volume features.

It is also very instructive to build a simple analysis device. Put the hologram on a flat surface such as a table. Take a sheet of translucent plastic or paper sheet, make a small hole in it and position it a few inches above the hologram, parallel to the table. Then take a laser pointer, position it above the hole in the sheet so that it sends the light beam downwards through the hole to the hologram. There it reflects from the hologram and diffracted light hits the translucent sheet. You can then observe from above where is the highest light concentration. Such a simple device helps you to analyse various places on the hologram. When there is a simple diffraction grating on the hologram, you should clearly see sharp diffraction orders on the translucent sheet; you can even reveal orientation and pitch of the grating from the analysis of the orders. When there is a diffractive white, you should see a large blurry spot on the translucent sheet. If you use a near-UV laser pointer (405 nm), it is sometimes possible to check whether high frequency gratings are present.
