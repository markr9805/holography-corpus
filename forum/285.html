Dinesh Mon Aug 01, 2011 12:33 pm #2
> kyodai: Obviously with this method the green beam would be coming from slightly different angel.

No, not necessarily. It depends on the kind of hologram you're making.

There are a large number of ways to get "colour" into a hologram. These ways can be used in both transmission and reflection geometries. However, in most of these ways (not all), you need a panchromatic film. Native silver halide is blue/UV sensitive; I believe, without looking it up, that it peaks at around 350nm. In order to capture different wavelengths, dyes are added to the silver halide. These dyes have a different response, so different films have different sensitivities and panchromatic film (film that has dyes that can capture red, green and blue) have different sensitivities for each colour. I believe that the film that comes with the kit is only red sensitive.

So, the simplest way (but the most complicated procedure!) to get "colour" into a hologram is the method known as "pseudocolour". This is a method used with reflection holograms (some holographers call multi-slit rainbows 'pseudo-colour'. I wouldn't worry about this technique at this stage, I mention this before someone comes back to me and quotes some article or paper where a transmission hologram is called 'pseudocolour'). The idea is that when you shoot a reflection hologram - either single beam Denisyuk or H1/H2 from a transmission master - the "data" is in the forms of planes within the emulsion called "Bragg planes". These are planes that are (mostly) parallel to the sides of the emulsion and occur in layers, like a cake with many layers. The plane separation is usually about half the wavelength of the laser. Thus, a laser shooting at 633 will create planes that are separated by 316.5nm. These planes act like an interference filter and reject any component that is not 2316.5, in other words if illuminated with white light, only light of wavelength 2316.5 = 633 will be diffracted and the rest is absorbed or goes right through the plate. This gives a red image. This is an idealisation, of course, and real emulsion won't have all the planes at exactly 316.5nm (or whatever half the wavelength of the laser is) but the planes are still all separated by roughly half the frequency of the laser.

But, if you swell the emulsion first, then shoot with say 633, then collapse it, the planes, which were at 315 when they were shot, will collapse to a shorter distance, say 270. In this case, the image will be a colour of 2270 = 540nm - green. So, here's the procedure (assuming you're using a HeNe, simply change all the colours if you're shooting at some other wavelength):

Shoot image number 1 - this is the "red" image"

Swell the plate - 2 minutes in a 2% TEA solution (there are other substances like sugar etc and people on the forum will all have their favourite swelling agent).

Dry the plate - there are a number of ways of doing this, but the emulsion should dry in a nice smooth finish. Make sure it's completely dry otherwise it will continue drying as you expose and you won't get an image.

Put the plate back, but don't move the laser.

Remove the subject for image number 1 and put the subject for image number 2 - the "green " image. Don't put the object in the same position as the "red" object, move it away a little.

Shoot image number 2

Develop

You should see image number 1 in red and image number 2 in green.

If you're shooting a reflection (single beam Denisyuk or H1/H2) with multiple lasers, it's very important not to twist or move the laser. Simply move the object, ie put the object for the other colour in a different position. The reason is that the white light that reconstructs the hologram can only come from one direction. This one direction must be both the direction of the red laser and the green laser. The red laser creates "red" planes and the green laser creates "green" planes, but both these planes will recreate from the same direction as the original laser was pointing. So, as long as both beams come from lasers in the same direction, the white light reconstruction will give you red off the red planes and green off the green planes and you get red and green together. If you move the laser, you must also move the hologram so that the recon light at one position only reconstructs red and from the other position only reconstruct green; you won't get both colours at the same time.

There are other techniques such as post swelling, multiple rainbow slits, very thick emulsions etc, but they're more complicated.

*You can have more than one reconstruction beams, and some people use this technique for special effects. But just to get colour from one hologram, one of theabove techniques is enough. I only mention this because I don't want someone to quote an "expert" who's used more than one reconstruction beam.

You may not get exactly red and exactly green. I haven't gone into the concept of bandwidth and emulsion shrinkage, both of will cause a shift in colour. Accurate colour rendition is a difficult task and I'm going through research trying to find ways of accurate colour rendition. My feeling, for what it's worth, is that colour holography will only succeed if we can record the more blended colours of real life like yellows, browns and pinks and not the harsh deep red and greens that a lot of colour holography shows.
