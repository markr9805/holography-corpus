[quote:8e8eec0714]I feel, and have always felt, and have expressed my concern that holography will not grow until holographers get together and share their technique and findings[/quote:8e8eec0714]

It would be nice, in an ideal world, if everyone revealed everytechnique in his or her own field and worked for free to "grow" their own profession. Perhaps programmers could reveal the code of an OS they're writing. Perhaps chemists could reveal the details of the emulsion they're developing. Perhaps drug companies can reveal the detailed chemistry and development methods they use. Everyone can then make their own holograms, write their own OS's, make their own medicines etc, etc. Personally, I feel that this, "Everyone should reveal all their techniques" is a little simplistic. I use to think like this when I was 16 years old!

In the real world in which we all live, in a country and a system that's run along capitalistic lines, this is simply not practical. Only those who are not affected by these attempts to "reveal all" want everything revealed. Mostly find that a programmer feels that profesional holographers should reveal all their secrets, but when asked to write a piece of code for free, they fall back on the fact that they make a living at it and can't give it away for free. The glaring hypocrisy doesn't seem to sink in. Sometimes they'll accept doing what they do for "free" in exchange for something considered valuable, but even this system of barter is a method of payment. I don't ask any programmer to program something for me for free using the argument that it'll make programming "grow" if they wrote a program for me for nothing.

Holography is as much of a profession as any other. Anyone coming to SPIE is well aware of it. There, professional holographers exchange information and techniques within limits, but no one, to my knowledge uses the argument "Tell me everything you know for the Greater Good and Progress of holography. This professionalism for certain techniques should be respected and the holographer should not be made to feel that somehow holography is in the doldrums because he/she did not tell everyone everthing! Holography will progress if and only if it's seen as a valid expression of some form of populism. As a "closet" subject carried out in dark basements and capable of only chess pieces, bottles, skulls and pretty colors, it'll get nowhere. The article in the BBC news about the Queen's portrait hit it right on the head - it's kitsch. It will remain kitsch unless the image gets better, not whether or not it's "bright" or "DCG" or "narrowband" or any other techno-babble. A bottle or green skull or a fan or pretty colors will not impress anybody just because it's "3D". I think this is more an expression of the limited imagination of the holographer than the inability of holography to "take off". We should accept it as a hobby and nothing more - not some Great Mission to get it "out there" and have it "grow". No one for instance building balsa wood airplanes complains balso wood airplane building is not growing for lack of information.

On the other hand, many hobby holographers don't seem to realise just how much it has grown over and above bottles and colored fans of light. It and it's cousin shearography, is a standard method of non-destructive testing. These contracts are worth many millions of dollars. I heard that someone (well, I know who, but I ain't saying!) got a ten million dollar contract for holographic non-destructive testing for the military. Holographic Memories are an exciting new frontier in memory densities. Holographic Bragg selectors are a standard part of long distance telecom and the internet backbone. There's a hologram in the IRAS satellite. There may even be one of my holograms in the space-based X-Ray telescope (I say 'may' because i was told that's what it'd be used for. I have no idea if it actually was!). Your laptop probably has one of POC's holographic diffusers in it. Digilens were working on a fascinating system of combining holography and electronics to make Optical Switching networks (Bob, where are you?) In medicine, defence, space in practically every field holography has become an integral and useful component. It's "grown" alright! It's just not "grown" into colored fans and bottles. There's ple-e-e=nty of information out there, if you have the education, knowledge and access to papers. There are thousands of papers on holography every year. We, on this forum are hobbyists. Let us accept this limitation and make the best we can of our hobby instead of constantly crying "What happened? Why isn't holography 'growing'? Why aren't I recognised by the mass media?" Let's build the best balsa wood airplane we are capable of with our limited tools and not wonder why Boeing isn't beating a path to our door!

I think the software industry is a good analogy. My company is software based. We use almost exclusively 'Free' software ([http://www.fsf.org](http://www.fsf.org)). It is usually free in that we don't have to pay for it. The code is open so we can modify it to our requirements, and we can do things that our competitors who have 'bought in' software can't do. Thousands of people have contributed to this software, and we contribute too. This is our give-away page:

[http://www.river-valley.com/index1.html](http://www.river-valley.com/index1.html)

And we know that literally thousands have downloaded and benefited from these programs.

Our model is to open and share most, but not all of our software. We are also active on newsgroups, dealing with people's problems, all free of charge. This means when we have a problem, we post a message and replies come within minutes. Much more efficient than any commercial support group.

In analogy with this, suppose Rudie gave away almost all of his techniques. People would immediately try out similar holograms. He would forever be known as the originator of these techniques, so any commercial contract would probably go to him anyway. Having kept back a few tricks, he would also have the edge on his particular technique, although others would come up with tricks he didn't think of. And because holography would benefit, he would benefit too.

This might seem an idealistic scenario, but at least in software, this has worked and is working for us. I was also very sceptical until my Indian colleagues educated me in Free and Open software. We are happy, we are ethical and moral, our staff are happy, our clients are happy, and we make money out of free software. Maybe this can work for holography.

It's true that if you have a succesful business you can give away stuff for free. However, if you gave away everything, or almost everything, for free and are neither independantly wealthynor have another source of income how do you live?

These days non-disclosures are commonplace. If you have an application for a holographic technique and need resources or staff you get them to sign a contract that imposes certain penalties. I think it's as true in holography as in any business that in order to give something away, you need to have another source of income that's protected by non-disclosures, contracts of one form or another etc. Personally, I think it's only fair to state this.

I wear a Triple Take hat which is a professional holographic company. Insofar as I feel that a particular technique can have a financial benefit for me, I state that I'm not going to reveal this in public. Tom B. Sat Jun 26, 2004 7:07 am #6
My old-fashioned bias is toward the totally open, ideal scientific model - to stake a firm claim on a discovery, all info needed to replicate the result must be freely provided. By retaining trade secrets, the discoverers should forfeit the right to formal credit, and they also lose any opportunity for correction from their peers, who in nearly all cases would (if the idea had been disclosed) have gleefully pointed out that the idea was either wrong or trivial, and in any case it had been done much better 10 years ago by X who modified the procedure to get better results. Could be helpful , unless X had died in the meantime and took the secret recipe with him.

Trade secrets may provide some short-term advantage, but since anything that can be engineered can be reverse engineered by a sufficiently motivated competitor, and there are many ways to skin a cat, (ouch) the real protection is minimal. Total reliance on trade secrets, uncorrected by peer review, is a recipe for doom - only your competitors know exactly what you are doing (perhaps better than you do) and nobody can inform you of possible improvements. I would say much the same for patents - a Bonanza for lawyers, but nowadays a complete waste of time. The real field of commercial competition should be engineering, manufacturing, and marketing.

Holographic art is another matter. Many artists are very secretive and superstitious about their processes, perhaps fearing that they would seem either too meticulous or too sloppy when described, and besides, they would rather make art than provide recipes. I can respect this, but I thank Rudie Berkhout for taking the time to describe some of his techniques in detail - rather than dispelling the mystery, it only deepened my awe.

I'd love to see some of his work in glorious 3D sometime.
