Tony Fri Oct 14, 2005 5:02 pm #3
Why wouldn't it be possible to do this with just one polarizer? The idea is, just add a polarizer to the reference beam. The object beam is unpolarized - i.e. contains light of many different polarizations. This illuminates the object, which further randomizes the polarizations, which has the effect of taking light that was correctly polarized and making it incorrect, as well as the effect of taking light that was incorrect and making it now correct. When the light from the object intereferes with the reference beam, any components that are correctly polarized will make fringes.

In other words, if the object is going to randomize the polarization anyway, why bother polarizing the object beam in the first place?

John Klayer Sat Oct 22, 2005 5:31 am #6
I made more than a few holograms several years ago using fiber optics. My thinking was that if the diode laser has a very long coherence length, then a little scrambling thru the fiber would still yield light with enough left over coherence to make holograms. The ones I made were not real great. There were a lot of splotches on them. I used several ways to launch the laser into the fiber. What worked about as well as anything else was a simple glass marble like kids play with used as a ball lens. I also tried fiber bundles. Maybe I shouldn't even mention this, as it is quite embarassing and was an asanine idea: I tried combining two laser diodes together by launching each into a fiber and then bringing the other ends of the fibers together to form a single source. Yeah, I got holograms - but did the two diodes work together or did one produce fringes and the other make noise?

You may want to buy the Frank DeFreitas book "Shoebox Holography". It has a chapter on fiber optic holography.

I remember Holodisk mentioning an idea a couple or so years ago about using fibers to bring two or more laser diodes into mutual coherence by drawing a little light from one and sending it into another with a fiber to "synchronize" them.
Colin Kaminski Sat Oct 22, 2005 3:07 pm #7
There are some papers on the net about this. When you see the photo of the labs it looks insane. $10,000 plus. However that does not mean it wouldn't work with a little optical epoxy and a good Temperature control assembly.

I would guess that the simplest thing to try would be to mount two diodes in the same cooling block and use two constant current power supplies. One would then link the diodes by bleading a little light back into the "driven" diode and then you would adjust the power of one of the diodes until the syncronized. If this did not work you would have to frequency tune the diode with temperature instead of power.

Power tunning is good for making small changes to frequency. But to make large changes you will need to change the temperature.
