This is sometimes known as an achromatic hologram, where there are no hues, the image (at least close to the film plane) appears to be black and white as you had predicted. This is the point of the exercise in TJ's Thomas Edison foundation book as discussed in [http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewt ... =17&t=6141.](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?f=17&t=6141.) Once again I invoke the name of Steve Provence, who said his contribution to the true color holography market was making black and white or silvery realistic objects, his most famous of the classic era a very convincing "silver" watch, multi-channelled so that the hands spin crazily along (not unlike Professor Provence!)

To make these transfers as sharp as possible, the H1 should not be parallel to the copy plate, but tilted so that the H1 "points" toward the replay light. (Aka the alpha angle, achromatic angle, or Benton angle.) I always hesitate to invoke the seminal Benton Papers like as if everyone knew what the heck I'm talking about, but I will always send you a pdf of them if you PM me c/o this forum. As mentioned in the TEA thread in Beginning, the colors in WLT holos come from geometry, not chemistry.

Usually multiple slit WLT transfers are used to give pseudo-color effects. A trio of slits at the proper spacing will give color separated images that can add up to true color. Try illuminating a multi-color embossed holo with a diverged beam, and you will see floating in space in front of the holo 2 or 3 and sometimes more slits. The topmost slit will give the (relative) blue parts of the image, the lowest red.

I say relative because any slit in a rainbow holo will conjure up all colors of the spectrum; it is when the R,G,and B slits are placed in the right positions to each other that you will simultaneously see a red, a green, and a blue image, and if you are really good at registration, C, M, and Y!

Why not give it a test shot and see what you get? Even trying to do the transfer without all the fancy tilts will give some multi-colors or B & W effects.
