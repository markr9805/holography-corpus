here's how I do it; other's may differ...

The H1 was (probably) made with the reference hitting the emulsion side. Therefore for the transfer you want the reconstruction beam on the opposite (non-emulsion) side to give a pseudo reconstruction, nominally positioned in the plane of the H2. The H2 reference is from the opposite side as the H1, and the H2 reference side is the emulsion side. Then when reconstructing the H2, the reconstruction beam is on the non-emulsion side to give a pseudo reconstruction of a pseudo image, which is an ortho.

When setting up plates, I chant silently to myself, 'emulsion always faces the reference beam'... (not sure if it's ALWAYS true, but so far, it works for me!)
Dinesh Wed Aug 10, 2005 3:38 pm #3
It depends on whether you used an overhead or side reference for the H1. Assuming you used a side reference, put the H1 on the table and illuminate it so as to see the image behind the plate, ie the plate is between your eyes and the image. Then turn the plate around the vertical axis, ie spin the plate around, if you used a side reference or flip the plate over so the bottom becomes the top, if you used an overhead reference. At this point, the image now floats in space between you and the plate. You may need to stand back a bit to see it. Take a white card and slowly bring it into the volume of the object. Care! The image is backwards - as you bring the white card towards the H1, you start at the back of the mage and work towards the front. At any rate, you'll see 2D slices of the image as you bring in the card. Thus, let's say you're shooting a cat. Furthest from the H1, you'll see the 2D profile of it's ears. As you bring the card towards te H1, the 2D of it's eyes comes into focus, then perhaps it's nose etc. Determine the "slice" you want on the image plane and set your H2 there. In the example above, you may want to have it's eyes on the image plane, so you set your H2 where you saw the eyes focused.

Now you have a choice: reflection, rainbow or achromat. For reflection, illuminate the H1 fully and bring in the H2 ref from the other side of the H1, ie the "back" of the H2. For achromat, fully illuminate the H1 and bring in the H2 ref from the same side as the H1, ie the "front". For rainbow, illuminate the H1 with a slit illumination, ie the H1 only has a narrow slit of light hitting it, and bring in the H2 ref from the front.

In any case, the final viewing position of the hologram is with the viewer's head in between the H1 and H2. The viewer's head will be between H1 and H2, looking away from the H1 and towards the H2. This determines where the emulsion should lie. Mostly it isn't that important unless you intend to black the hologram. In this case, you want the emulsion side away from the H1. Thus, imagine your head between H1 and H2, looking at the H2 plate (the back of your head is towards the H1). You want to be looking at glass (or plastic) so the emulsion needs to be on the other side.

Dinesh Fri Aug 12, 2005 2:19 pm #5
The blackening is both to enhance the visibility of the hologram and to protect the emulsion. When the paint is wet, it does get into the emulsion, I've seen green hologram red shift and red ones disappear, however once the paint is dry it comes back. There is much discussion on the "best" types of spray paint to maximise uniformity of coating without damage to the emulsion. Most people I know use a brand known as "Krylon".

BobH Fri Aug 12, 2005 4:29 pm #7
I'd recommend glossy black instead of flat, or matte black. Glossy black will look blacker because of the absence of scatter from the flattening components. Also, another good reason to paint the emulsion of holograms is that the paint index-matches away the effects of surface relief gratings, increasing the signal-to-noise ratio. 8)
