I've recently been filling up my table with a forest of mounts and several of my purchased (shop-type) and home-made bases were a bit wobbly, the enemy of stability.

For the shop bases I initially started leveling them with a method used for sharpening chisels. Get a piece of float glass or marble, attach a piece of sandpaper to it and sand the bottom of the base until it's flat. That worked well enough but took too long. I then thought that perhaps I could use jb-weld (which I have a few boxes of after building my new table) as a filler which would level the base.

I took a piece of marble and spread a sheet of wax paper over the top. After mixing the jb-weld I spread a thin bead along the "skids" of the base and then lightly spread it so that it covered the skid completely. I then put the base on the wax paper and pressed down to both even out and thin out the layer of epoxy.

Let it cure overnight and it's a snap to remove the mount from the wax paper.

You end up with a nice smooth and slightly waxy bottom that once in-place on the table, doesn't want to move and won't wobble as long as your table is reasonably flat.

I suppose it could be and might be more stable than my method. The epoxy won't stay perfectly smooth forever although I suppose I could remove it and re-apply.

For the bearings you could epoxy them to the underside off the skids so you could use slightly larger bearings as working with itty bitty bearings might be a major pita.

I'd be mildly concerned about leaving divots in the table top steel as mine is only 16ga steel and those mounts have very strong magnets. I can practically lift my table up using one that I have. For reference my table top weighs about 200lb.

BobH Sun Oct 17, 2004 4:20 pm #9
I use a large flat file to dress up the surfaces of all bases going onto the table after being stored in a drawer (or box) with other components. Certainly if they were handled by anyone else, or dropped. I often find very little dings (or burrs) on the corners of aluminum and mag bases, and the file (used across opposite corners) takes them off.

Another source of component rocking is the flatness of the table. Over-tightening clamping bolts into the table can pull the table's threads up a bit, causing a local "bump" there. Scratches will also raise a burr. Particles of glass, metal or dust can also make a base rock. My practice is to clear the table (as completely as practical) before each system assembly, and wipe it off to inspect for these problems.
