Joe Farina Thu Jul 06, 2006 9:55 pm #4
John, there is a huge amount of literature on silver halide emulsion preparation. As we know, holographic emulsions are in a very special class, because grain sizes need to be extremely small.

If I had to recommend a single book as an introduction to the subject, it would be:

Photographic Emulsion Technique, by T. Thorne Baker, Second Edition, 1948 (American Photographic Publishing Co., Boston). I would suggest requesting this book using the interlibrary loan service from your local library. It's on the shelf at the University of Notre Dame.

There are many books and articles on the subject (as both Martin and myself can attest) but I suggest this one as a starting point, because it is very clearly written (in English, which is what you want, I assume). Back in those days, the quality of published material was usually much better than what it is today. And the book is not so very old that it's difficult to read (in contrast to some of the early books and papers on the subject).

On pages 153-4 he gives a method for a Lippmann emulsion:

"In a method described by Lehmann, a solution of gelatin is first prepared as follows:

A. Hard gelatin............20g

Water....................390cc

This is dissolved and filtered though spun glass (glass wool) or cotton batting (wadding) and used at 35C.

To 80cc of the A solution add

Distilled water........10cc

Silver nitrate............4g

To the balance of solution A, add and dissolve 3.2g of potassium bromide. Taking care that there is no froth on this, it is slowly poured into the gelatin-silver solution at the same temperature, stirring fairly gently during the precipitation and for three and one-half minutes afterwards.

The following sensitizing dyes are then added immediately:

Pinacyanol (1:1000 alcoholic solution)........4cc

Orthochrom T (1:1000 alcoholic solution)....4cc

Acridin Orange (1:500 alcoholic solution)....4cc

This mixture of dyes must have been previously warmed to 30C. It is added slowly with gentle stirring, not more than forty-five seconds being taken. Under no circumstances must the emulsion be subjected to any further heating, the object being to avoid grain growth and fog formation.

A very thin coating is applied to glass which has been so perfectly cleaned that, if breathed upon, the condensation is even all over and clears quickly. The coated plates are laid on a cold slab to set, and are then washed in a trough with gently running water. Ten minutes should suffice to remove the potassium nitrate. The washed plates are best dried in a horizontal position. This is stated to give the most brilliant colors."

*

This is just one example of the preparation of a Lippmann-type emulsion, there seem to be an infinity of others.[/i]
