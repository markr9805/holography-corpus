Although I have'nt had the vast experience you have had John I do think you are on the right track on this humidity problem and want back your experiment by refining the "water absorbing polymer crystals?", On experience I had with

HeCd made DCG's years ago. I put a

"finished" DCG in a good desiccator on a mesh frame above a dish of

phosphorus pentoxide based desiccant for about a week.

The effect of that treatment was amazing , no backing glass was then used and the hologram just stayed unchanged for years until finally I wanted to see if it actually went if I actually put it in water ,(it did but it took several minutes to go).

I am sure that when we normally process we leave so much water content still there even with the driest IPA in the final bath and with strict absence of humectants such as traces of glycerol or TMG. This water then gradually messes up the void structure. But it seems pretty clear to me that if you can bring the water content way down to a small fraction of this value it somehow seems to NOT encourage more ambient humidity in. In other words if the intrinsic humidity of your gelatin is still high (RH~50% and above say), it just "breathes" in ambient moisture as Bob implies but I say again if you get it REALLY REALLY dry in the first place , it no longer absorbs ambient moisture (provided it is free of humectants.).

The desiccants used to do this job should be serious ones. You can use cheap silica gel (like that supplied in little bags with say new computers, but until you give the gel a proper treatment it is useless. You need to put it in a dish and heat it to the highest temperature you can get to in a domestic oven for an hour,

(minus the bag or it will scorch it of course). This treatment is also necessary for silica gel bought from chemical suppliers. You then let it cool a bit but don't forget it and put it in your desiccator as soon as you can. My desiccator had a flange which needed to be well greased with petroleum gelly. Some plastic food containers used to be well made enough to do a reasonable job as desiccators but cheap disposable ones are likely to be quite useless.

There is a further discussion that needs airing on the difference between

water content and Relative Humidity also known as "water activity" (aw) which is the same thing as RH used for gases but used for solids or liquids and uses decimal parts instead of %. So your "dry" gelatin granules in the packet have a typical water activity of 0.6 or 60% RH. if they are left around to equilibrate with the typical room. The actual water content though is surprisingly around 10% of the total weight of gelatin. (ie 100ml per kg gelatin.) Gelatin will not go mouldy in the bag unless its aw gets above 0.85 because it dehydrates moulds or bacteria when its aw is below 0.8 If you have a hydrophobic liquid like say kerosene in equilibrium with the room it too by definition

will have an aw of 0.6 but its actual water content may be only around 100 ppm or roughly 0.1g per litre.

BobH Mon Sep 12, 2005 1:02 pm #16
I've used decalin, mineral oil, and Cargille index-matching fluids. The decalin is very easy to clean up with methanol, but is not something you want to be absorbing through the skin (nor the methanol). Mineral oil is a real hassle to clean (acetone worked best), and is an oily mess. It's also too viscous to get the bubbles out easily. Probably cheapest and safest, though. Cargille index-matching fluid is the best. Cleans up nicely with acetone, no bad smell. Probably most expensive, though.

A note on solvents. I used methanol for a long time, thinking it was less toxic than acetone. Acetone's better, however, because it evaporates so fast that it doesn't get into the blood (through fingertips). I know, I should have been wearing latex gloves. I wear finger cots now. In any case, once you get used to index-matching your holograms will be cleaner and brighter and you'll never go without.

I use an alcohol filled squirt bottle to remove white spirit. Although alcohol is hardly miscible with it, it works well enough to enable the water to then

wet the gelatin without any hindrance.

Wet ipa does not affect plastics, even the drier IPA is not a solvent like for example acetone.

The point you raise about getting the layer to have an even water content

may be right but there is one subtlety I have come across that is not obvious, it is as follows:

The rate of your initial drying can affect the future behaviour of the gelatin. So if you blow dry in the centre of a large sheet , the edges that were dried more slowly may behave differently always. This is connected with the hydrogen bonding arrangements as the gelatin liquid gels. So faster drying can lead to a slightly weaker film than a slower dried film where the bonds have time to find the stronger arrangements. However

I doubt if this has much to do with the very localized effects you describe.

JohnFP Tue Feb 28, 2006 1:13 pm #45
Hummmmm... I am really surprised I did not receive any comments on this observation from some of the DCG producers out there. Is it that it works and they are busy making greenie free Holograms or was this a trade secret that no one was supposed to mention.

Ok, it's been about a half a dozen sessions using this technique and I will have to say I am very pleased with the results. My newly adapted process has the following.

Make up the emulsion and when it has cooked as per my usual modus operandi, take the beaker with a magnetic stirrer in it and place it in the refrigerator for at least a couple of days and up to three weeks so far (Obviously I disconnect the light in the fridge). As I have a dedicated refrigerator I have not covered the beaker yet but I do notice that the emulsion loses some moisture and the subsequent coatings are a little thicker, after about the third or forth reheating and coating, substantially thicker. I offset by heating my plates. Even if the beaker was sealed in the fridge I think it is the water vapor evaporating during the repeated cooking stages. Maybe a topped beaker would be the way to go. Or study the amount of water loss and add it back in prior to every heating. I believe the Cr transition to V accompanied with the freshly broken down amino chains accounts for help in eliminating differences of hardening throughout the emulsion.

Secondly, I fix as normal with little agitation the whole time. But now when I place it in the water after fixer I agitate nicely (not too vigorously) for at least a minute. I feel it is important to neutralize the fixer as close to all at once as possible. When it is simply put in the running water there is no guarantee that the water flow is adequate and even across the whole plate and it is possible that the fixer may stay in longer in some areas then in others. Thus the new procedure.

I have virtually eliminated greenies all together and this is very exciting for me. I guess the true test will be this summer when ambient humidity is high and my dehumidifier is working overtime.

I have also come to find out that the temperature of the emulsion at coating time, the temperature of the plate at coating time and the ambient temperature at coating time are also very important to keeping the greenies at bay. For those of you lucky enough to be in a year round controlled environment this may not be of concern, but for me where ambient temperature changes drastically, this is very important to identify.

Here is what I have found so far. This is using the veil and spin (78rpm) method with emulsion at 95:10:3 on double pane 5x7" glass.

Let's say the emulsion is always at 115 F. at coating time. Then

If the ambient temperature is 62 F the plate needs to be warmed to 95 F

If the ambient temperature is 67 F the plate needs to be warmed to 85 F

If the ambient temperature is 72 - 75 F the plate does not need to be warmed.

If the ambient temperature is above 75 - 80 then the emulsion needs to be made thicker by using less water (90:10:3)

I bet this could be all figured out using the viscosity of gel at 115F, the viscosity of this same gel vs. a time scale representing drying/cooling time on the spinner at a particular ambient temperature and the force the spinner exerts on the different radii of the emulsion on the glass plate. Then we could throw in humidity which probably affects drying/cooling time. I don't believe humidity in itself needs to be controlled other then realizing how it changes the drying of the emulsion while on the spinner. In other words higher humidity would require a thicker emulsion or the glass to be less warmed or a lower ambient temperature at coating time. In my case the humidity is high when the ambient temperature is higher thus the only variable I can change other then dehumidifying is a thicker emulsion (less water).

I am only observing my notes over the last couple of years and drawing conclusions. I have to believe that if the temperature of the glass is too high relative to the ambient temperature, then the emulsion thins too much in the center. This is clearly evident in the way the greenie is shaped, always in the center and points sticking outward. Maybe spin less or slower, not sure but I think trying to control a variable is better then trying to offset it with another variable.

Now if the glass temperature is too low with relation to the ambient temperature, the gel does not have enough time to evenly flow across the plate during the spin (remember, using veil to coat) and it is very evident that the greenies show up always on the thin or thick end (I haven't figured out why this is other then the thought that the processing, specifically the hardening in Fixer with hardener, is good for one thickness but not the other because of the thickness differential).

This is a completely different greenie control then previously in this post in which the processing causes the greenie. These conclusions were just drawn up last night and the true test will be with the results of following sessions.

If anyone wants to critique these hypotheses, feel free to do so.
