In November 2004 I did some tests using shellac, with the objective of sealing 2 pieces of glass together to make a water-vapor resistant seal. I took two 4" X 5" pieces of glass, and coated the outer areas of both pieces with brushed layers of alcohol/shellac. This was applied to about 1/4 inch to the outer areas of both pieces. This was dried completely, and a paper humidity indicator strip was placed between the glass plates. Then the two pieces were clamped together and placed in a low temperature oven so that the shellac melted and fused together.

This showed a lot of promise, as I immersed this under water for 3 months, the seal held (although there was damage to the shellac in the outer 1 or 2mm, which was acceptable for such a severe test), and the humidity test strip was unchanged. Then I placed it in a refrigerator with a timer which would keep turning the refrigerator on and off, so that it would do some rather extreme temperature cycling. After many cycles (I don't have my notes in front of me for the exact number) there was a very minimal effect, and the seal held completely. After that, I deemed the process a success, and put the sample in my damp basement for long-term storage.

This year I was browsing the internet and read that shellac continues to become more and more brittle as it ages. I thought that my earlier heating/fusing process had polymerized the shellac thoroughly, so I didn't think this would be much of a factor, but it appears I was wrong. The seal now has some unsual-looking cracks in a number of areas. The seal is still intact, and the humidity test strip is unchanged, but after viewing those cracks, I consider this method to be unacceptable.

So, the search continues.

Joe Farina Fri Apr 10, 2009 2:58 pm #5
Right now, I'm looking at epoxy again, specifically Hxtal. There is a interesting video about Hxtal at:

[http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i6Ysd04b05Y](http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i6Ysd04b05Y)

The only problem with epoxies (and probably all polymers like UV adhesives) is long-term exposure to water vapor. If, for example, a glass/epoxy bond is exposed to 80% humidity for a couple weeks, it might begin to fail. Some factors will increase the adhesion to glass however, based on the technical literature, such as 1) use of the appropriate silane solution, wiped on the glass prior to the application of epoxy (reported to increase adhesion dramatically), 2) roughening the surface of the glass beforehand (I may do this with a 600 grit silicon carbide blast, since I have the system), and 3) degassing the epoxy prior to application (as shown in the above video).

Even with these improvements, the edge of the epoxy will still be exposed to air in the final DCG sandwich, and polymers do absorb ambient moisture over time, as is well known. Once it absorbs a certain amount throughout the entire epoxy layer, any additional moisture can cause a "breaking point" so to speak, where the water begins to break the bond between the epoxy and glass. Moisture does have a strong affinity for glass, according to the literature, and "tries hard" to get at the surface of the glass and displace the epoxy. This is unfortunately a rather common sight in DCG holograms. They don't look like cracks per se, but rather like odd, bubbly-like fingers or branches. This must be an illustration of water becoming adsorbed to the surface of glass, displacing the epoxy or UV adhesive.

I've been giving some thought to a two-part approach, first using Hxtal in the usual manner. Then to remove the exposed edge of epoxy, I was thinking about grinding this area away, making a small channel in the glass about 2mm deep X 2mm wide. Then I want to try adding sodium silicate to this channel, and let it cure. The idea is that the hardened sodium silicate will act as a barrier, keeping water vapor away from the epoxy edge. I don't see this as a "total solution" to the problem, but is intended as an extra measure of protection.

I have been doing some testing of sodium silicate (water glass) and it has some very interesting properties. It has extremely strong adhesion to clean soda lime glass (window glass). Thin layers dry quickly (so quickly that I don't think the water present will adversely affect the epoxy while the water evaporates). When first dry, it's quite soft and extremely brittle, but as it ages, it continues to get stronger and harder, becoming more glass-like. According to the literature, it gets harder over a period of years, as it reacts chemically with the glass support.
