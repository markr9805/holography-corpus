About an hour ago I posted the following on Andy Lesniak's thread on "Thermal vs Chemical hardening"....

I wrote:-

........I am really amazed at your results with only 10 seconds exposure even though you have a powerful [100mw] 532 nm source. The science literature on the absorption spectrum of the dichromate ion is considered as utterly negligible. around 532nm .

I have run the dichromate spectrum myself this week and found the absorption indistinguishable from the background level. I am mystified how such photosensitivity is coming about. .. ........

Suddenly I had an idea....suppose some UV is coming out of his laser too!

So I took a dichroic mirror that was made specially to reflect 355nm light

rather than 532nm, ie. the frequency tripled rather than doubled 1064 fundamental and shone my 20mw laser pointer at it so that the beam

reflected onto a sheet of ordinary white copying paper. And sure enough

inspite of the large amount of 532 light reflected off the mirror , a comparatively dim fluorescing blue spot could be clearly seen!

(The ordinary paper is important because it fluoresces under 355nm whereas higher grade white gloss paper does not and you can't see 355nm unless you are pre-buscent or have had a new eye lens operation,

but that's another story....) So with this relatively small proportion

of 355nm coming out with its vastly increased absorbability by dichromate (and even more by any chromate around ) might this be the explanation

for DCG successes with "532nm" ?

Jeff

I understand most data from research on dichromates refer to absorption spectra measured in plain dichromate (water) solutions.

The explanation (for the success of 532nm recordings) may be along the one given by Solano, Lessard, Roberge (Red sensitivity of dichromated gelatin films, Applied Optics, Vol. 24, pp. 1189-1192, 1985):

> By analyzing the spectral characteristics of the dichromated gelatin, we found that the absorption spectrum of the dichromate ion is altered when it is introduced in a gelatin matrix. An absorption appears in the red part of the spectrum which allows hologram formation using 632.8nm radiation.


Of course that red sensitivity is tremendously low. The authors measured sensitivity levels around 15 – 150J/cm2. The graphs given by Solano et al. suggest an increased sensitivity towards longer wavelengths. And this may well imply enhanced speed at 532nm.

One might also wonder whether adding different colloids to gelatin would produce different results. I remember having made a DCG “quickie” at 532nm. Adding some paper glue to the gelatin obviously increased speed.

By the way [http://cslin.auto.fcu.edu.tw/oe/index1.htm](http://cslin.auto.fcu.edu.tw/oe/index1.htm) describes DCG gratings recorded at 532nm.
Joe Farina Sat Dec 10, 2005 2:23 pm #5
Martin has a good point about the gelatin possibly being a factor.

On page 1190 of that paper, they also make the comment: "...The insert in Fig. 3 suggests the contribution from a large band with a maximum around 550nm..."

At the start of the paragraph, they call this a "new absorption band in DCG." So this implies that it wasn't generally realized until then (1985). The graph they refer to above (Fig. 3) shows transmittance/absorption curves for a DCG film (10 microns) as well as for an aqueous volume of ammonium dichromate. They look similar, with an extremely sharp slope between 500 and 550nm.

Maybe the absorption of solid dichromates had been misleading in the past, since they mention: "...For various solid dichromates, very weak absorption around 530-560nm...has been noted over the last fifty years."

Looking at the graph in Shankoff's original paper, the absorption of dichromated gelatin (and he definitely says gelatin) looks extremely poor at 532nm, almost identical to 633nm). This does not seem to be in good agreement with the other paper.

However, I think I can say with certainty that straight DCG holography (without any dyes) is fairly commonplace these days. Having an affordable source of powerful (~100mW) 532nm light available (Coherent 315M) seems to have helped this along considerably.

dcgman Sat Dec 10, 2005 3:12 pm #7
I think the 355nm "recording" is a red herring. A hologram recorded at 355 and replayed at ~532 would yield a magnified image. Gabor was looking for a way of increasing the magnification of electron microscopes when he came up with the idea of the 'hologram': recording using very short wavelength "electron waves" and replaying with much longer "visible waves" to get magnification.

A tidbit of "history" for Bumblebees:

"...For the yellow-green light exposure, we prepared our own gelatin films with Baker Co. U.S.P. gelatin, using a method similar to Brandes et al. We sensitized with 0.7M ammonium dichromate, but without dyes. Exposures were made at 530 nm with a doubled Nd:YAG laser. Other laser lines were used from a pulsed xenon laser at 460, 495, 516, 526, 535, and 540nm. For all of these wavelengths, efficient Lippman reflection holograms were obtained with index modulation up to 0.03. Exposure energy at 540 nm was about 300mJ/cm2 to achieve 85% diffraction efficiency."

"Dye-Sensitized Dichromated Gelatin for Holographic Optical Element Fabrication" by Andrejs Graube, in Photographic Science and Engineering, Vol. 22, No. 1, Jan/Feb. 1978.

300mJ sounds quite "doable" with a 100mW 315M for small DCG holograms.
