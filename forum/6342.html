I'll post some facts as I find them. Perhaps they will turn out to be true.

CD:

The elongated bumps that make up the track are each 0.5 microns wide, a minimum of 0.83 microns long and 125 nanometers high.

The track is about 5 km long.

The track is encoded with eighth-fourteen modulation. This can be a tough one to fix as it is designed to ensure that there are enough ones to keep everything aligned. In old floppies it was hard coded into the hardware. I worked on a couple of disk protection schemes that exploited the failings of modulations schemes.

On a CD-R there is a dye layer on the data side of the disk in between the aluminum and the laser. This dye turns dark when burned by the laser. Until this happens the back is completely devoid of tracks or information. Good news.

All of the error correction and modulation will likely make the simple idea of just writing the "correct file" to the disk an unlikely scenario. They will likely have to be fixes at the hardware level. The blank CR-R disk remains a viable medium it is just that the writer needs to be fixed.

However, there is lots of precise positioning equipment available (old CD drives). Perhaps one can take 2 burners and set up the feeds in an x-y configuration and burn the hologram as a square instead of a spiral. This is the most hardware intensive approach but it simplifies the software considerably. The disk would be held still and the laser would simply scan x-y and burn one pixel at a time. Since there is 1.6 microns separating the spiral tracks we can assume that we can achieve at least that level of positioning accuracy.

This would then be a different dot matrix hologram all together. I guess it would need a different name.

Another approach would be to use the dedicated hardware to make a hand drawn hologram. Then the software would calculate where all of the spirals need to be for each point and then "scan" the image to make it writable with the x-y plotter.

Still, another approach would be to use an analog interference pattern and try to have the intensity so high that it burns the image. This would take a lot of power and would likely need an Ar+ laser or something as watts seem appropriate but I have not broken out the back of the envelop to make the calculations. And I am not sure how the dye would respond to blue light. The NA of the laser would be smaller so a smaller spot could be used.

> "The most "raw" type of organization is known as "mode 2." Mode 2 does away with many of the nice things about CDs like error correction, but it gives us the most control over the bits. In a mode 2 sector, the first 12 bytes contain "syncing" data and the next 4 contain specific information about the sector. These bytes cannot be changed at the software level. (Maybe it is possible to write a driver that could change these?) The next 2336 bytes are free to be anything though. If this were all that happened to the data, our job would be easy. Unfortunately, there's a lot more data manipulation before the data actually gets written to the CD."If I am not mistaken, most newer CD-R burners have a "RAW" mode that allows full control over the whole 2352 byte sector, and in some cases even the 96 byte subchannel code.
