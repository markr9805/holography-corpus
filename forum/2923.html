One way is to get a thick piece of glass. It needs to be thick so you can block off the back surface reflection, but you lose light this way due to the back surface reflection. The back surface reflection may not be too much, depending on the angle of the glass. This is pretty restricted since the beam ratio is set by the angle of the glass to the beam. If you want to change beam ratio you need to change the geometry. But, if you want to try this, get a thick piece of glass, about 1/4 or 1/2 in, and let the beam bounce off it at some angle. There will be two reflected beams - front surface and back surface. Block off the back surface with a card. The amount of light reflected will depend on the angle at which it bounces off and the polarisation. It's important to have s polarisation. This means if you're using a horizontal setup, the polarisation needs to be vertical.

You might try searching astronomy sites. Amateur astronomers make their own mirrors but you may need to alter the process to create a thin, partial mirror.

If you or the school has access to Silver Nitrate, Ammonium Nitrate and Sodium Hydroxide , You could use the mirror reaction on the surface of a piece of glass ( [http://chemmovies.unl.edu/chemistry/sma ... SS075.html](http://chemmovies.unl.edu/chemistry/smallscale/SS075.html) ). Again, use fewer ingrediants for a partial mirror.

But after reading your post, yet another possible method comes to mind.

A number of commercial beam splitters are made by vacuum vapor depositation of aluminum, or some other reflective substance. A vacuum chamber might not be necessary, however, if you used a reflective material with a lower melting point.

Solder joints, in electronic circuits, are shiny and reflective if done properly. And a small amount of vapor is formed, when you use the soldering iron to solder a circuit. And of course, this vapor exists at atmospheric pressure.

Perhaps if he heated up a small amount of solder over a bunsen burner, enough vapor could be released to coat a piece of glass. The drawback to this, is that this amount of vapor would be hazerdous if inhaled. So it might best be done in an enclosed box of some kind.

The bunsen burner of course, would need to be external. Not only would it need oxygen to burn, but you would want to protect the glass from being coated with carbon from the flame.

Simply allowing the vapor to deposit by itself, however, would not be very efficient. A better method might be to put a high voltage electric charge on the glass, and an opposite charge on the vapor. This would cause the vapor to be drawn to the glass, instead of coating the inside of the chamber.

A light beam of known intensity would shine on the glass, and when it's intensity is reduced by half, the glass should be partially reflective enough to form a beam splitter.

Once again, this idea is untested.

Here is an interesting article (If not way off topic) from:

[http://www.worldwidewords.org/qa/qa-mad2.htm](http://www.worldwidewords.org/qa/qa-mad2.htm)

These days we associate mad as a hatter with a bit of whimsy in Lewis Carroll’s famous children’s book Alice in Wonderland of 1865. Carroll didn’t invent the phrase, though. By the time he wrote the book it was already well known; the first example I can find is from a work by Thomas Chandler Haliburton (Judge Haliburton), of Nova Scotia, who was well-known in the 1830s for his comic writings about the character Sam Slick; in The Clockmaker; or the Sayings and Doings of Samuel Slick of Slickville of 1836, he wrote: “Father he larfed out like any thing; I thought he would never stop—and sister Sall got right up and walked out of the room, as mad as a hatter”. As the author felt no need to explain it, by then it was clearly well known in his part of North America. Whether it was invented there, I don’t know, but it seems likely. An early British reference is in Pendennis by William Makepeace Thackeray, serialised between 1848-50: “We were talking about it at mess, yesterday, and chaffing Derby Oaks—until he was as mad as a hatter”.

Note, by the way, that mad is being used in both these cases in the sense of being angry rather than insane, so these examples better fit the sense of phrases like mad as a wet hen, mad as a hornet, mad as a cut snake, mad as a meat axe, and other wonderful similes, of which the first two are American and the last two from Australia or New Zealand. But Thomas Hughes, in Tom Brown’s Schooldays, used it in the same way that Lewis Carroll was later to do: “He’s a very good fellow, but as mad as a hatter”.

Few people who use the phrase today realise that there’s a story of human suffering behind it; the term actually derives from an early industrial occupational disease. Felt hats were once very popular in North America and Europe; an example is the top hat. The best sorts were made from beaver fur, but cheaper ones used furs such as rabbit instead.

A complicated set of processes was needed to turn the fur into a finished hat. With the cheaper sorts of fur, an early step was to brush a solution of a mercury compound—usually mercurous nitrate—on to the fur to roughen the fibres and make them mat more easily, a process called carroting because it made the fur turn orange. Beaver fur had natural serrated edges that made this unnecessary, one reason why it was preferred, but the cost and scarcity of beaver meant that other furs had to be used.

Whatever the source of the fur, the fibres were then shaved off the skin and turned into felt; this was later immersed in a boiling acid solution to thicken and harden it. Finishing processes included steaming the hat to shape and ironing it. In all these steps, hatters working in poorly ventilated workshops would breathe in the mercury compounds and accumulate the metal in their bodies.

We now know that mercury is a cumulative poison that causes kidney and brain damage. Physical symptoms include trembling (known at the time as hatter’s shakes), loosening of teeth, loss of co-ordination, and slurred speech; mental ones include irritability, loss of memory, depression, anxiety, and other personality changes. This was called mad hatter syndrome.

It’s been a very long time since mercury was used in making hats, and now all that remains is a relic phrase that links to a nasty period in manufacturing history. But mad hatter syndrome remains common as a description of the symptoms of mercury poisoning.

[quote:8df2e10a7f="BobH"]I'd suggest making your first hologram a transmission grating, using spatial beamsplitting and mirrors. This is probably explained as a "single beam transmission" set-up. Make the hologram small, like 1"x1". [/quote:8df2e10a7f]

One way to do this is to set a mirror at some angle to the plate and hit the plate/mirror combination with a collimated beam at wide enough to cover both the plate and the mirror. This beam needs to come in from the opposite side of the plate from the mirror. The direct beam hits the plate while the beam refllected off the mirror reflects back and also hits the plate at a different angle. When you shine a laser onto the finished hologram, there'll be two beams - the direct, straight through zero order beam and the diffracted beam. The splitting ratio depends on the efficency of the hologram and the the angle of the diffracted beam depends on the angle of the original mirror.
