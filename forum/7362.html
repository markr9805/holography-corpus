Joe Farina Wed Jun 04, 2008 10:28 am #20
Based on the tests done by Dave and Tony, I would say that narrowband shifting can easily be done with succinic acid (SA). There are some cases where the results are more broadband, although Tony is the best person to ask about this, since photos really don't tell the story.

The idea is to control the color of DCG, with some degree of precision and reliability. The steps are as follows:

1) Produce a DCG hologram using SA in the system, so that the final result is "redder" than desired.

2) Thoroughly dry the hologram using either a microwave oven, or a desiccator. A dry gelatin layer is normal, good practice when doing DCG. If it's sealed while still containing moisture, it will be vulnerable to irreversible color-shifts in the future, when subjected to excessive warmth. This was noted by Rallison. If it contains moisture, and is not sealed, it will be very vulnerable to ambient humidity. This drying step is an important part of the process, since the color should not shift back towards blue at this stage. Tony has shown that either a microwave or desiccator will work. At this point, I would suggest a microwave, simply because it's much faster.

3) Heat the hologram in a regular convection oven at 100 to 150C until the color moves down towards the blue as desired. There's no need to worry about not enough baking, as the layer will already be thoroughly dry from Step 2 above. It's possible that 150C may be too high, and perhaps this was the cause of the slightly irregular result in Tony's last test. He also did it at 100C, but I don't recall how long it took, or if the layer was more uniform afterwards.

The main drawback of the system (at the present time) is that a blue-er than normal hologram needs to be produced first. I would really like to eliminate this need, and only do one wet processing. In DCG, it makes a huge difference if one processing can be done instead of two. So in other words, instead of producing a blue, finished hologram (then doing the SA and reprocessing), it would be ideal if the SA could be applied right after the fixing and rinsing. It would be necessary in this case to be confident that a "blue-er than normal" hologram would have been produced anyway. But when Tony gave this a try, there were blotches. He was limited by the amount of time possible in the SA, or the gelatin would start lifting off. I thought that perhaps if the time in the SA bath were longer, and there was more vigorous agitation, it might improve the result. But we were having problems keeping the gelatin attached to the glass. Tony tried some silane that I sent, but it may have been the wrong kind. Perhaps we should try the one that Jeff recommended. Or maybe we can do something to the edges of the plate, because the edges are the first to lift off.

Joe Farina Thu Jun 05, 2008 2:06 pm #29
Tony, are you running out of SA? If so, I will send more. (I sent some to Danny this morning via USPS priority, he should get it in a few days.) The "subbing" layer Martin referred to was a prelimary layer of gelatin and chrome alum, which is then hardened by heating. Then, on top of that, the regular DCG coating would be made. It would be kind of a "coupling layer" between the glass and the main DCG layer: the chrome-hardened layer of gelatin sticks really well to the glass, while the DCG sticks really well to the chrome-hardened gelatin. It's a very old, tried-and-true method of getting gelatin to stick to glass, often used industrially for silver halide emulsions. But it's just a bit laborious for our purposes, unless we can't find a simpler way of solving the problem. That's why I suggested trying to "paint" the edges of the layer with a gelatin-hardener like chrome alum, formalin (formaldehyde), or perhaps even acetone. The idea would be to "mimic" a hardened subbing layer by treating the existing layer with a hardener. The hope would be that the hardened areas resist peeling.

But actually, nail polish sounds like a better thing to try at the moment. Good luck.

Joe Farina Mon Jun 09, 2008 10:48 am #42
Tony did an earlier version of this same test. Instead of a final baking at 143C for 2 hours, he did a final baking baking at 100C for 3 hours. Those shifted completely back to the blue also. And judging from the photos he sent me, these earlier holograms had better, more uniform results. So I don't think the acid is breaking down.

Succinic acid makes gelatin more rigid. Since the acid reacts with the gelatin in a swollen state, it gets "stiffened" while stretched out. It doesn't shrink back completely when it dries. (That is, when the drying is done by a non-shrinking method like a microwave or desiccator.) Then, when the heating is done in a regular oven ~100C, the gelatin shrinks.

There's my explanation.
