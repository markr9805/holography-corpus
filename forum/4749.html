Colin Kaminski Wed Apr 13, 2005 4:53 pm #6
Before I discovered holography I was quite fascinated with Mylar mirrors. Here is what I found out.

You have to have a great piece of mylar. Many have stresses built in. If you have a roll you can find out where good pieces lie by eye.

The lip that holds the mylar must be meticulous. I don't think any of my frying pans are made well enough but I was able to salvage the parts from a banjo and make them work.

Holding a constant vacuum for a long exposure would be quite a problem. A minute or so should be easy.

I never found a hoop to make a really large mirror for doing image planed H1s. I think this is where the idea would be great.

Fast f# tend to make the mylar wrinkle and cause large wavefront distortions.

I started with a book called Unusual Telescopes and I would recommend looking up that reference if you want to try one. It only has about 3 pages devoted to Mylar mirrors.

First, I confirm it'd be paraboidal. The actual shape would depend on the velocity. However, I wonder if in a gravity-free zone it'd be spherical. This raises the interesting question: Is it possible to make mirror shapes in space to whatever curve and spec you wanted.

The odd thing in the article about the chap making his own paraboloid in the above link was that he was worried about spherical aberrations. Spherical aberrations arise due to the difference between a parabola and a circle, due to Fermat's Principle of least Time. With such low errors as his error analyss shows he should not need to worry about spherical aberrations. I'd have thought that coma would have been a more serious problem and for that a Schmidt corrector may not be of much value. Interesting that the larger errors were at the centre of the mirror and positive - I'd have expected them to be negative, until I realised that he may not have taken surface tension into account.

Well, let's see. The force at the centre of the mylar is clearly straight down, since there are no counteracting forces of any significance. There will be a small surfce tension force which will "pull in" the mylar and "flatten" the bottom a tiny bit, which leads me to explain the error analysis in the paper by surface tension forces. But I think we can safely say that the force in the centre is all down to within an order of magnitude The forces at the edge are composed of the reaction force of the attachment (Newton's 3rd law) which will be acting "up" but tilted at an angle, depending on the attachment, and gravity acting down. Thus the resultant is a line downward but tilted off vertical at some angle depending on the attachment. Now the deviation of the mylar will be a vector along the resultant force. This means that it'll be straight down at the centre (minus surface tension) and twisted at the edges and continuous. So, it seems that the exact shape is very dependant on the attachment at the edges and the weight of the water. The larger the sheet of mylar(ie the larger its diameter), I think the more hyperbolic the surface and the smaller the mylar, the more parabolic. To be truly circular, you'd need to fill the mylar with mercury and have a bloody strong attachment to the edges, since Hg has a density of 13 g/cc!
