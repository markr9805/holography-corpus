Jeffrey Weil Mon Sep 19, 2011 9:41 am #2
It's about how far away the object has been recorded from the image plane. The further away the more redundant the recording becomes.

It has nothing to do with the type of holography involved. If you made your single beam reflection holograms with the object far away from the film you would get the same effect. I'm not saying that's practical with sbr I just bring it up as an example.

Dinesh Mon Sep 19, 2011 10:48 am #3
> Tony: We all know that taking a hologram and smashing it into bits will still retain the image albeit at a small angle of view.

Not true, mostly. This is a hangover from the days when the word "holograph" - total or complete writing - was taken too literally. The idea being that if every part of the hologram contained all the information, then any small part of the hologram should contain all the information about the image field and so the entire image field should be visible.

The hologram is a window to a scene (or single object). The "frame" of this window depends on what the type of hologram you're shooting, in some cases the "frame" is the plate itself and in some cases the "frame" is detached from the plate. Just like any frame in any window, what's visible through the window depends on how near, or far, is the object/scene you're viewing, with respect to the size of the window and the size and position of the surrounding frame.

Basically, the cone angle of the light coming from the object/scene to your eye should encompass the size of the piece (or entirety) of the hologram. If the area of the object/scene is A, the object is at a distance r away, the area of the piece (or entirety) of the hologram is A' and the hologram piece is r' away, then

A/((r)^2) >= A'/((r')^2)

If, given the area of the "sub-hologram" and it's distance away from your eye as above, this equation is satisfied, then you can see the entire image. If not, the image will be clipped.
