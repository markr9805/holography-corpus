Ed Wesly Thu Jul 26, 2007 8:10 pm #2
It's not that difficult to make a simple one color (or should I say one spectrum) rainbow hologram. The big difference if you are used to making masters for reflection transfers is that the distance of the master from the copy plate will be larger than what you are used to in order to get the object to appear in one hue rather than the top of the object being red and the bottom being green.

How big are you planning on making the holo? If you want something 4" by 5"-ish, start with object to master distance for the H1 about 30 cm. The master could be made on a slice of an 8 by 10" plate. You could use a 4" by 5" plate for a master, but this wouldn't have too much in the way of horizontal parallax.

When flushing the real image out of the master to put onto the copy plate, use only a strip of the master. Passing the undiverged beam through a glass rod or test tube filled with glycerine will generate a stripe of light that would be decent enough to use.

This would be in one leg of the beam paths. The other would have the reference beam directed to the copy plate. At all stages the beam path lengths should be as long as physically possible if you don't have the necessary collimators.

Focus the real image onto your copy plate, add a reference beam at 45 degrees or so from the same side of the plate as the master. If you want a top lit hologram at the end of the day, the reference should come from the bottom of the plate.

Although this quick and dirty scheme is not too difficult, it can be vexing if you are not familiar with making a master hologram, i.e. the classical laser transmission hologram. To go beyond a single slit exposure requires figuring the alpha angle, or the tilt the of master for multi-color registration. In the usual transmission master to reflection copy scheme the H1 and H2 are parallel to each other; in the rainbow and achromatic schemes the H1 "points" to the reconstructing light source.

This will probably make you ask more questions, but that's the point of the fourm!

Ed Wesly Fri Jul 27, 2007 4:52 pm #9
Slit width: the eternal debate between saturation, speckle and depth.

To add more fuel to the fire here, I will relate my experiences with the Craig Newswanger (perhaps one of the greatest holographic engineers ever!) perfected stereogram machine that I worked with at the former CFC Holographics.

Images were projected onto a backlit projection screen using a TI Digital Multi Mirror Device, which I would highly recommend over any LCD screen. The H1 was placed about 40 cm from the screen (I don’t work there anymore so this is from recollections which could be hazy and I am not in the mood right now to perform the requisite calculations!) and tilted at Benton’s alpha angle, not parallel to the screen.

The top slit was blue, since this H1 was being recorded with 458 nm Argon, and this slit was placed at the middle of the screen. The collimated reference beam came in along the normal of the 8 by 10 holoplate.

The plate was partitioned into 3 slits with gaps between them and above and below them for a total of 7 (draw it out and count them) partitions. They were all approximately 20 mm. Yes the slits were about an inch wide!

But that is measured along the plate. If you factor in the tilt angle, the size of the slit apparent to the viewer (its vertical height) would be a more modest 6 to 8 mm.

These made excellent color registered stereograms. An example that the readers of this forum may have in their possession is the dropped in MicroSoft Windows flag found on the Windows NT, 2000, Me and XP discs with the dot matrix background, or the Harry Potter trading cards (from Wizards of the Coast) that were out at the beginning of this century. Light one of these up with a diverged laser beam, and you will see the slits jump out and focus in space!
