favalora Sun Jul 22, 2007 11:16 pm #5
Andy, welcome to the forum. I have never made a CGH, but have seen several very good ones made by alumni of the MIT (former) Spatial Imaging Group. There are several ways to proceed making them on your own. You are correct about laser printers lacking the resolution needed for a good CGH, although some people have reported success there. In general, you want 1000-2000 dots per millimeter in the holographic substrate.

One trick you can use is to print it out (at whatever resolution you can), and then photograph it. You can shine a laser through the film negative. However, I don't know what type of film to recommend with enough resolution to result in any sort of decent reconstruction. (One drawback of having poor resolution is that the reconstruction angle will be very narrow. That is, you won't have any "look-around" (motion parallax). You'll simply reconstruct what looks like a 2-D image.)

You can also make a multiplex hologram by depicting the scene from a variety of viewpoints computationally, and then doing a "rainbow" hologram. For some ideas on this, see the papers and theses by Michael Halle [here,](http://www.media.mit.edu/spi/spiPubs.htm) such as "the generalized holographic stereogram." You can record a multiperspective hologram onto holographic film in a series of steps.

Maybe I didn't answer your question. The accuracy of your computer physics modeling can probably be bounded by the resolution of your real-world holographic substrate.

g

favalora Fri Jul 27, 2007 11:45 pm #17
Amplitude versus intensity

Hmm. I suspect that he might have plotted electric field amplitude rather than the irradiance, which is proportional to the time average of the square of the electric field amplitude. That is, according to Benton's course notes, if

E(x, y, t) = A sin (2 pi nu t) V/m,

then its average squared value will be

A^2 / 2,

and it will deliver an irradiance of

I(x,y) = (epsilon-naught c) < | E(x,y,t) |^2 >time avg = (epsilon-naught *c / 2) A^2

= 2.65 x 10^(-3) E^2(rms) W/m^2

in MKS.

Fringe Applet

I think you (and others here) may find this Media Lab demonstration of fringes very interesting. [(here)](http://courses.media.mit.edu/2002spring/mas450/holodemos/index.html). Oh, wait, they're not available at that URL anymore! Perhaps someone can find them by Googling.
