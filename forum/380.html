This afternoon I set-up to make an H1 of two metal etched business cards at right angles to one another.

In the meter readings that follow, the figures are in Kohms of my CdS cell exposure meter and I have prepared a graph that converts these readings to microwatts per centimetre squared, which multiplied by seconds = microjoules (uJ)

I set the beam splitter at a Ref = 25 and an Object = 180 (1:7 ratio) - exposed for 45 secs to give me a total exposure of 25 uJ - produced a dark - very dark grey appearance after a 4 minute development in CW - C2 at 20 deg to produce a really bright H1 after bleaching in ferric/EDTA

Noticed however that the Ref beam had wandered a bit, such that one side of the film received more Ref light than the other - and not surprisingly the brightest part of the holgram was where the film received the brightest part of the Ref beam.

I then measured the actual readings where the hologram was brightest; the readings were: Ref = 17 and Obj = 210 which translates to 34 uJ and a Ratio of 1:12

That ratio's moving a bit away from the textbooks saying 1:4 - isn't it

There's something dodgy about the way I measure the object beam. Let's say you have a very small object which is matt white and a similarly matt white very large object. The object light reading at the film plane will always be greater for the large object, although they both have the same unit area brightness at their source. Setting the exposure time correct for the number of uJ hitting the film will give the correct exposure of the film - so does the ratio matter at all?

If it does matter, how does one measure it - perhaps with a piece of card the size of the film being used and of similar peceived reflectivity / colour as the object - and then set the beam splitter to the classic 4:1 ratio

Dinesh Wed Nov 02, 2011 2:45 pm #9
Yes, ratio does matter. No, it doesn't have to 4:1. That's an archaic leftover from another time for another reason. The amount of light that hits a specific part of the hologram and ratio at that point determines the recording at that point. Since it's difficult - if not impossible - to measure point-by-point over the entire plate, you need to average over the plate area. You need to be careful of dynamic range and intermodulation noise.

Let's start at the beginning. If anyone feels an overwhelming desire to sing "Doe, a deer, a female deer" Please leave the room! Anyway, what you're trying to record are a set of sinusoidal fringes. The amplitude (height) of the fringes determine the efficiency (brightness) of the hologram. The separation (frequency) of the fringes determine the reconstruction colour. For a grating, the entire plate has a recording on one set of fringes with one spatial frequency. For a real object, the plate "sees" an object wave and a reference wave and the interference of the two waves creates a sinusoidal variation of light which is then recorded. However, there are two effects going on: the actual "real" sinusoidal fringes generated by the interference which sits in space regardless of the plates existence, and the recording of those fringes by the physical mechanism of the emulsion. The actual sin wave increases very fast at the outset, slows down, becomes horizontal and then descends, going the other way in the same manner. When you try and record this, the small variations in the rise of the sin wave needs to be recorded as small variations in the physical medium; grey scales for silver halide. To record faithfully, the grey scale changes in the silver must reflect the subtle changes throughout the sin wave. If it does not do this - or cannot - then the recording is not faithful. Since no medium can react to small, subtle changes and track a mathematical sin wave, no recording will ever be exact, all physical media react to the variations of the light wave in a different way, so whatever is recorded is a facsimile of the "actual" fringes with a physical distortion thrown in. Also, the actual fringes created by the two beams in space are no longer sinusoidal, but can be deconstructed into pure sinusoidal fringes, with different frequencies. This is like the overtones in music, any given note may have a vary large set of overtones. However, each overtone has a different frequency and is recorded with more or less fidelity.

Also, just as in music, where the range of overtones gives the music it's structure, the range of "overtones" in the fringe structure gives the image reconstruction it's structure. But, here's the crucial detail, the range of overtones is determined by the beam ratio. So, think of the hologram like an amplifier. If you drive the amplifier too hard, turn the gain up too high, the amp can no longer respond correctly - linearly - to the sound and you "flat-top" the amp and create noise. Similarly, if the ratio is too high, you "flat-top" the fringe structure and lose detail to noise. If the ratio is very, very high you lose all structure on the fringes and get no image at all. Also, recall I said you can deconstruct the actual fringe structure to a set of sinusoids. If part of that set has too high a frequency component, the emulsion can no longer capture it. This results in lost detail. If the beam ratio is too low, then the subtler variations in the fringe structure is lost. This would be the equivalent of setting the amp at too low of a gain. You lose certain frequency components at both ends of the spectrum. The height of the complex fringe - or rather, the average height - is also a function of the ratio. Too low of a ratio and the amplitude is also low, making the image dim.

The question, then, is how much is too high or too low? In the early days, film manufacturers would give you the Hurter-Driffield curve which is the characteristic response curve of silver halide and gives the amount the film goes dark as a function of exposure (actually, the log of the exposure). This curve looks like an elongated "S". Just as in amplifier theory, the idea was that you based your exposure at the centre of the long part of the S. In this case, you had the maximum possible variations of exposure and still remain within the straight line part of the S. Too high a ratio and the variation of exposure would lead to a regime on the curved part of the S, the top and the bottom. In this case, the film would not develop correctly to capture the higher frequencies - you've 'flat-topped' the emulsion. If you have the H&D curve for Agfa, you'll notice that if you do set your exposure at the centre, the variation of exposure that takes you into the curved part of the H&D curve is +/- 2. Hence the magic 4:1 given in textbooks. By the way, the H&D curve implies that the film is linear, at least over the straight line portion. The longer you develop, the darker the film gets and, so long as you stay in the straight line portion, you can still capture subtle shades of grey. If you bleach the film, you lose the linearity.

Today we all bleach and, with non-silver holography we no longer need an H&D curve. So today, the "best" ratio can be as low as 30:1 and as high 1:1, depending on the medium, the number of exposures and a whole variety of physical phenomena. The things I said needed watching are dynamic range and intermodulation noise.

Dynamic range refers to the range of brightness of the object beam. You still need to know the limits of the range of overtones your emulsion can take. If the variation of brightness of the object is too high, the film may not be able to capture the entire range; some of the overtones will be lost because the film can't react to it. Every emulsion has a dynamic range.

Intermodulation refers to the fact that one edge of the object will interfee with the other edge of the object, so the light from different parts of the object are mutually interfering. This causes a set of low frequency gratings that will diffract light into the image area and cause noise. There are all sorts of techniques to overcome this. But that's another story.
BobH Wed Nov 02, 2011 8:19 pm #10
I'd say beam ratio is always important, and especially with "masters". There's confusion because of the nonconventional way the power is being measured. If you saw better efficiency in the area of the hologram where it got more power (but had a higher beam ratio), I think you were just generally underexposing the material in the first place. The increase of diffraction efficiency caused by increasing power was greater than the decrease of DE caused by the higher beam ratio. The image was probably clearer in the best area as well.
