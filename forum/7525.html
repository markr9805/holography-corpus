Glenn Sun Sep 14, 2008 1:30 am #1
Some sources allege holography as being a "lensless photography." I disagree, for the following reason.

In conventional photography, there is a light source, an object to be filmed, a lens, then the film the image is focused on--in that order.

In holography, the sequence may be different, but the components are the same. You've got the light source (object beam), the lens (SLM--usually a little convex lens to spread the laser beam), the object to be filmed, and finally the holographic film (in color photography, colors are rendered thru interference of thin films, and is the rough analogue to the reference beam in holography).

Thus in both instances you have the same components to the system, just a different way of arranging the components in sequence.

A good insight? Or another instance of mastering the obvious?

Regarding the natural laser diode beam spread, this is in fact caused by a rectangular pinhole lens - the micron-scale emitting facet of the diode itself. Anytime you have an aperture, you have in effect introduced a lens in the sense that it creates a non-isotropic beam pattern. Glenn,

I do not understand the relevance of monochromaticity to whether light bounces off a surface by scattering or specular reflection. That choice is surely determined by surface roughness, whether light is broad band or not. In general any surface not a mirror will scatter light. Most often the distribution of scattered intensity is very roughly a bell shaped curve with perhaps a mild peak in the specular direction. If the light is coherent you get speckle in space in the scattering field, the result of self interference among the scattered photons, as I think you know. If incoherent, you do not.

And yes, the beam spread off the diode will be gaussian, but I think diffraction is likely a secondary effect to the mode structure of the laser. If it is a single transverse mode diode, then it will be a single gaussian. But if it is a higher mode, TEM 02, for example, then it will be multiple gaussians or near gaussians. In a multimode case, it is likely the diode aperture's diffraction will not much affect the beam distribution.

The best way to appreciate the coherent quality of laser light is to take a laser pointer and shine it thru a microscope set at around 300x magnification. There you will see very awesome-looking diffraction and interference artifacts! It will also teach you a lesson that book-reading alone can't teach, and that lesson is the importance of having a purely coherent beam used in the hologram-making process. Reading about it, or exposing your own holograms just doesn't do justice, whereas seeing the coherent light with its interference patterns under a microscope does!

Knowing that, and seeing with what precision truly coherent monochromatic laser light and its consequent interference effect can affect how coherent light is reflected off of non-mirrored ("rough") surfaces, goes a long way in understanding the real difference between specular reflection and scattering.

In other words, looking at laser light thru a microscope will illustrate to you in a visible way that laser light reflects off objects specularly (as with a mirror) rather than diffusely (as incoherent light does off a rough surface).

And since laser light reflects off of all objects specularly, you can obtain the reflection of an object on a nearby screen if you use laser light (the same experiment cannot be repeated with incoherent light).

As an experiment, guys, take this (you can get one at a liquor store for around $1.99):

![Image](http://www.scitoyscatalog.com/Merchant2/graphics/00000001/small_5_head_laser_pointer.jpg)

and unscrew its collimating lens. You will find the lens on the beam output side. It unscrews with a screwdriver. Remove the spring that keeps the lens in place, and plop the lens back in (since there's no spring to hold the collimating lens at its fixed position, the collimating lens drops all the way to the base; at just millimeters away from the laser itself).

With the lens back in, remove one of the HOE's (those four little screw-on caps that come with this package are actually Holographic Optical Elements, or HOE's) and plop it right on top of the lens.

Now go into the bathroom with the laser pointer as just described and shine it on the ceiling. What do you see? Yep! The interference pattern from that particular HOE! It's pretty awesome to see the concentric rings from the HOE...

...But I digress...

Now get an inexpensive optical microscope like the HelloKitty model shown below. One can be had at a thrift store for around $2.99. Set it on 300x magnification.

Now take the laser pointer and shine its laser light directly into the microscope, magnifying lens side, and you'll see utterly fascinating diffraction effects; something that you just can't see with the naked eye!
