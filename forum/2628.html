Emulsion defintion:

[http://www.wordiq.com/definition/Emulsion](http://www.wordiq.com/definition/Emulsion)

To silver a hologram is to deposit a layer of silver directly on the emulsion which is a relief hologram as in photoresist. The method deposits the silver one atom thick at a time, thus preserving the fringes and making the hologram electrically conductive. Research silver atomizing for mirror making.

Nickel Electroplating, Ahhhh. You could probably get many answers to which is the best anode to use. Here is a company I am NOT familliar with that came up on a Google search. There is some information therein.

[http://www.incoltd.com/products/plating/material.asp](http://www.incoltd.com/products/plating/material.asp)

Once you find which anode you want to use, the next concern would be what tensil strength each of your shims (dies) should be. Mother shims would be different then child shims. Child shims are created from mother shims and are what is used in the embossing machine. Tensile strength differences would be achieved by varrying the voltage, PH, time in bath and temperature. Mother shim needs to be strong enough to handle making child shims directly on them. Child shims need to be flexable enough to handle the repeaded straining from the printer. The tensile strength differences have to be enough to allow the separation of the mother from the child.

Good luck!

[quote:6fa9b317e4]can someone please explain what an emulsion is? [/quote:6fa9b317e4]

An emulsion is a layer of photosensitive material on a substrate such as glass or flexible film. Since A hologram is a photograph of interference fringes, an emulsion is necessary to capture the light intensities of the fringes.

The most common emulsion, for both holography and photography, is Silver Halide. This is an layer of gelatin, approximately 8 microns thick, in which are embedded tiny crystals of a Silver halide, mostly Silver Bromide. These crystals of Silver Bromide react to light and chemically reduce to the element Silver, which is black in such a finely divided state. After exposure, the exposed part of the plate (the glass substrate + emulsion) is black and the rest is clear. I've written a small essay on Silver halide ( [http://www.triple-take.com/Articles/](http://www.triple-take.com/Articles/) - download "SilverHalide.pdf") if you're interested in the precise workings. However, there are others on this forum more knowledgeable than me about the workings of SDilver halide. Another emulsion is Dichromated Gelatin, or DCG. This is a layer of gelatin with Ammonium Dichromate dissolved in it to a thickness of about 10 - 15 microns on a substrate of glass. Since DCG is usually "home made" the thickness and exact formula varues.

[quote:6fa9b317e4]Also what does it mean to "silver" a hologram?[/quote:6fa9b317e4]

In the mass production of holograms, the hologram is first shot on a substance know as Photoresist. This is an emulsion, usually about a micron or less, of a photosensitive material that hardens on exposure to light. Because of the very small emulsion depth, the surface of the material is hardened where there are fringes abd are soft everywhere else. The plate is then dipped into an etching solution just long enough to dissolve the softer parts and leave the harder parts as a surface profile - the holographic fringes. This is then electroformed. In order to do this it must be placed in a conductive solution so that a metal layer can be formed over the surface profile by electrolysis and can take on the exact same profile. However, the plate, usually glass, must first be made conductive itself. To do this they spray a metal solution onto the plate such that, on contact, a layer of metal is depostied on the plate surfce. This is known as "silvering".

Here's a fairly good overview of the process: [http://www.jrpusa.com/en/electroform.htm](http://www.jrpusa.com/en/electroform.htm)

I've just been made aware of a contradiction in the essay on Silver Halide. Apparently I've mixed up "low sensitivity" and "high sensitivity".

I've always used "sensitivity" as a number. That is, a particular medium has a sensitivity of n mJ/sq cm. The lower this number, the shorter the exposure and so I call this "low sensitivity". By this criterion, Silver Halide has an incredibly low sensitivity. However, as Tom B has pointed out, popular usage uses it in the opposite sense. The lower the exposure requirement, the more sensitive is the material to light and so a low exposure means a "high sensitivity". By this criterion, Silver Halide has an incredibly high sensitivity. I thought I'd changed all the "low sensitivity" to "high sensitivity" but obviously I missed a few.

Sorry.
