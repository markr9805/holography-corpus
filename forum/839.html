Dinesh Wed Apr 23, 2014 11:17 am #4
> 142laser: This is the original idea behind holography correct? First conveived as a new kind of microscope?I seem to remember hearing that but never read it. Thanks.

Not quite. He was trying to improve the resolution of an electron microscope. In the first chapter, he starts by stating that the spherical aberration of an electron microscope is 5 Angstroms and that the resolution limit is proportional to the fourth root of the spherical aberration. Thus, he was writing about a new microscopic principle, not a new microscope. I've seen many a written document stating that Gabor invented holography by thinking of a new type of microscope, but all of these written documents seem to think that he was trying to invent a new kind of optical microscope. What Gabor was trying to do was to do away with the objective lens in an electron microscope, because he saw the primary reason for the spherical aberration was the objective lens. By the way, this is an objective lens for an electron microscope, not a camera lens. He justified the principle by use of an optical obective lens, and he specifically states that he used light rather than electrons for this first experiment.

Another thing not often mentioned (or even noticed perhaps) is the extremely small size of the apparatus. He states that the NA of the system is 0.04, or, the angular divergence is just 5 degrees. The film was extremely small!

One other irony is that this principle was trying to do away with objective lenses for electron microscopes, however, he states:

"Other imperfections of the reconstruction are chiefly due to the defects in the microscope objective used for the production of the point focus...".

He apparently got the idea while playing tennis. He did a few back-of-the-envelope calculations and saw that the idea was workable.

Dinesh Thu Apr 24, 2014 12:08 pm #7
> BobH: Another significant sentence in this piece is "The resolution is markedly imperfect only in the center, where the circular frame creates a disturbance." Doesn't this suggest that the system only works when off-axis? I

I don't think so. His technique involved passing a beam through a transparency with some scattering centres on it - the words "Fresnel" "Young" and "Huygens". Since these partially opaque scattering centres were at the centre of the transparency, the "reference beam" would have been partially blocked by them. The idea was that the outward spherical waves coming off the scattering centres would interfere with the light going through the outer, relatively clear, section of the transparency. Also, the edge of the scattering centres themselves would have caused some diffraction, creating a diffraction pattern superimposed on the interference pattern, which you see on his photographs. So, it's not that the technique only works when off-axis, but that the technique doesn't work if there is a opaque disturbance on-axis. The disturbance would inhibit the reference beam in those areas of the centre and cause it's own scattering pattern. If, however, the disturbance at the centre were a phase only disturbance with zero opacity, then there would have been no such imperfection at the centre. I recall someone saying that he made an "in-line" hologram of a fish tank, which he called a "Gabor" hologram. He seemed to get quite angry when I pointed out that it was not a Gabor hologram, but that it was an inline hologram. The difference is two-fold: the water in the fish tank, and, partially, the fish themselves, were transparent. Thus the object only altered phase, not intensity. Secondly, unless the fish tank were about a few millimetres across, he would not have got the Fresnel zone Soret effect, but a set of Bragg planes, except within a few degrees at the centre of the plate. There is an analysis somewhere which gives a more rigorous demonstration of the difference between a large, inline, transparent object and a small transparency with a few opaque scattering centres. It's in Goodman, I think.

I know that today, you can image molecular lattices with electron microscopes, as Gabor wanted to do. But, I wonder if electron microscopes could resolve molecular lattices in his lifetime.

Dinesh Fri Apr 25, 2014 11:09 am #9
> BobH: I thought he used binary transparencies; totally opaque or totally transparent and it was the diffraction off the edges of the words that interfered with the reference light transmitted.

I don't think it was binary. The whole point is that there should be two fields propagating from the transparency, the direct field of the source going through the transparency unaltered (except for attenuation) and the scattered field of the letters. That is, the light from the source hits the letters, which then scatters in the forward (towards the film) direction. If the letters were completely opaque, there would have been no scattered field in the forward direction.

Bob seems to think that the mention of a "imperfection at the center" implies off-axis holography a la Leith Upatniks. That is, if I read Bob correctly, he thinks that Gabor had an inkling that he could get rid of the twin image by offsetting the reference as Leith Upatniks did, that holography would only "work" with an off-axis reference. My feeling is that the disturbance was due to the greater density of the letters. That is, the "reference", ie the undistorted direct field of the source, is attenuated by a larger amount in the areas of the letters. Considering the extremely small size of the equioment and the closeness of the film from the transparency, I get the feeling that the object shadowed the film, causing a photogtraphic image surrounded by a diffraction pattern due to Fresnel diffraction. You can see this sometimes when a baffle too close to an edge causes a "ringing" at the plate.
