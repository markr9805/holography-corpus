JohnFP Mon Nov 22, 2004 7:11 pm #2
I used to make embossed holograms. In order to make an embossed hologram you need to have a relief material. That is, the holographic fringes have to be in the surface of the material so that it can be stamped in the mylar. Most Holograms for embossing are made from Photo resist which usually needs a HeCd laser. So if you want to make an original H1 to be copied in Photo Resist, then silverized, then nickel plated and finally embossed your master (original H1) needs to replay relatively efficient in the HeCd wavelength (442nm - Violet).

There are many threads on this in this forum.

You can make masters from silver halide. In fact, you actually should. I've heard of companies making masters direct on resist, there was a phase where we in the company I worked in was thinking of trying this, but resist can't take the spatial frequencies. However, since resist needs a deep blue laser (Argon 457, HeCd 442, Krypton 413), if you make the H1 with HeNe and reconstruct with, say 457 which I use for resist, then you'll get a lot of distortion due to the change in lambda. You may need to take that into consideration. Colourholographics make BB450 especially for mastering for resist becausse of this reason.

The masters are generally just laser transmission holograms, ordinary H1's. The reference angle doesn't matter, since the reconstruction angle of the H1 has nothing to do with the reference of the H2, which does need to be on resist. Of course, if you have limited table space, you need to consider the geometry constraints of reconstruction angle and H2 reference. In general, you can use an H1 ref at anything between 45 and 60 for the silver halideH1. Remeber that the H2 is a rainbow and so you only need to reconstruct a slit from the H1. If you're doing multiple H1's, make 'em on seperate plates. Besides the obvious advantage of greater angle of view, you can always adjust the beam ratio for each H1 in the final H2, depending on the subject matter. So, an H1 with a uniform image may need one set of ratios and another H1 with a very bright feature may need another. If you make all the H1's on the same plate, you have no way of adjusting for dynamic range.

The H2 needs to be on resist, as John stated. For the H2, you need to keep the ref angles small since resist has a limited frequency response - somewhere between 25 and 40.

To get a good embossed piece there are several factors. First, make sure that you're H1 is very, very clean. Resist magnifies noise a lot. Second, expose so as to etch quickly. Long etch times add noise. Third don't etch too deep or you'll get orders. There are schools of thought for diluting the etch very little. I've always found 4:1 to work well for me with Microposit 303 (?)

There's a good explanation of the process here: [http://www.jrpusa.com/en/holography.htm](http://www.jrpusa.com/en/holography.htm)

IHMA ( [http://www.ihma.org/](http://www.ihma.org/) ) is a good overall source of embossed holography. They're basically the umbrella organisation for security holograms, but security holograms (credit cards, product ID etc) is the largest section of embossed holography.

Some companies are ABNH ( [http://www.abnh.com/](http://www.abnh.com/) ) where I worked, Applied ( [http://www.aotgroup.com/](http://www.aotgroup.com/) ) where I also worked, CFC ( [http://www.cfcintl.com/productLine.asp?productLineID=3](http://www.cfcintl.com/productLine.asp?productLineID=3) ) and many more.
