Tony Tue Aug 03, 2004 12:20 pm #3
In general, polarization should not be an issue in making H1/H2 copies as long as the beam paths are all in one plane (usually horizontal). If your polarization is off, the first thing you’ll notice is woodgrain in the H2 due to internal reflections in the glass.

I’ve had a lot of practice making H2 copies and can offer the following insights:

1. Stability is key. You’re making a reflection hologram, so stability has to be good as the fringes are smaller for reflection than for making the transmission master. My recent experience indicates that in addition to mechanical rigidity of the table and mounts, and vibration isolation, you also need to be concerned about thermal and air circulation effects. I got variable results until I provided baffles designed to keep the heat from my Hene Laser from creating thermal air currents, or heating of the surface of my hexcan table. If you’re using film, you need to make sure you are supporting it motionless – there are tricks to this. After getting everything stable, my results are consistently bright. Before, sometimes I would get ‘lucky’ and get a bright result, without really understanding why.

2. Make sure your chemicals are fresh. I was running towards the end on a batch of D-19 and when I doubled the exposure time the density did not increase appreciably. This indicated that density was being limited by ‘tired’ developer, not by exposure. I switched to a fresh developer and voila!

3. There is really a lot of latitude as far as exposure is concerned. My trials confirm what is noted in Saxby that you really have to go far in terms of exposure to make a noticeable difference in brightness of a reflection hologram.
MichaelH Tue Aug 03, 2004 2:04 pm #4
> In general, polarization should not be an issue in making H1/H2 copies

Not entirely true. In some ways polarization is more critical when making H1-H2 copies since the polarization isn't changed as a result of the light being diffracted by the H1.

There are at least two aspects of polarization that you have to keep in mind when making a hologram.

1) The polarization at the plate. To minimize reflections both at the front surface of the plate and internally when using a glass sandwich you want the incident and reflected beams to be in the same plane of polarization (P-polarization). Depending on your setup, you may want the opposite (S-polarization) as some researchers claim that you get brighter holograms as a result. I've never seen this but also haven't done exhaustive testing. All my masters and copies are P-polarized to minimize reflections. If you're using S-polarizatin you will end up with a woodgrain pattern in your film if you happen to be using film sandwiched between two pieces of glass.

2) You should make sure that your reference and object beams are polarized in the same plane. This is especially true when making H1-H2 copies. If the polarization between the two is off by 45 degrees, you'll get a very dim hologram. If they're off by 90 you'll get no image at all even though your film will go just as dark in the developer as if the polarization is equal.

Regarding #2, for H1's the object polarization can be critical or totally unimportant depending on your subject matter and desired end-result. For many display holograms the subjects tend to be diffuse reflectors which depolarize the light. I've found that I can most often ignore the polarization of the object beam when making an H2. When making an H2 though, you must pay attention to the polarization as it's not altered when passing through the H1 on the way to the H2. If the polarization is off you'll get a dim to invisible image.
Dinesh Tue Aug 03, 2004 2:24 pm #5
As Colin stated, reflecting the beam through two mirrors to different planes will transform the polarisation. In the same plane, there will be no change in polarisation.

Assuming that the expanding lens to the collimator is on the same level as the collimator, the action of collimating a beam does not change polarisation. The same with the beamsplitter, assuming that the 'variable' part does not involve a poarising cube. If you use a polarising cube to split the beam, each side is polarised at right angles to the other. In this case, you need a half wave plate on one side to twist it back so both beams are along the same direction.

The master hologram will not appreciably change the polarisation. Gelatin is very slightly birefringent, but too small to worry about in practice. Assuming that the angle at which you made the H1 is large and you made it on commercial Silver Halide plates, the H1 is a thick, or volume, hologram. In this case, the reconstruction is by reflections off the Bragg planes within the hologram. However, so long as the H1 reconstruction and H2 reference beams are in the same planes, the Bragg reflections won't affect polarisation.

Notice however, I keep saying 'on the same plane'. Polarisation is known by the letters 's' and 'p'. These names tie the actual polarisation, the electric field vector of the light, to the plane of the beams. When a beam reflects, the incident beam and the reflected beam form a plane known as the Plane of Incidence. So let's say that your laser gives out vertically polarised light. So far this is neither 's' nor 'p' since it hasn't reflected yet and so there's no plane of incidence - it's just 'vertical'. Now the first mirror rotates it in the same plane, ie no change in the level of the light. Now the plane of incidence is horizontal and the light, being vertically polarised, is 's' polarised. If the first mirror rotated the beam 'up', then the plane of incidence is vertical and the beam is 'p' polarised.

In a hologram, the fringe contrast is determined, among other things, by the angle of polarisation of the two beams. If the beam is 'p' polarised and coming in from different angles - say head-on for object beam and 60 degrees for reference - then the polarisation vectors make an angle of 60 degrees and the fringe contrast - hence efficency - is reduced by the cosine of 60, or 50% (actually less because of the refractive indices of glass and emulsion). However, if the beam is 's' polarised this means that the polarisation is vertical for both beams in a horizontal set-up and the angle between the pol vectors is 0 (they're both parallel and pointing 'up') . Since cosine of 0 is 1, there is no loss ino fringe contrast. However, if either beam is sent slightly 'up' or 'down', in other words the two beams are not perfectly level, then each beam is a mixture of both s and p When the beams meet at the plate, there will be a difference in the polarisation due to this effect and so will cause a loss in brightness. It's therefore very necessary to make sure that the beams are not sent a little upward by one mirror and then downward by another. One way to do this is to have an iris set at the beam level you want. Then pass the iris down the beam paths and make sure the beam is going through the iris opening at all times. This is like making the beam a piece of dtring and making sure that the string passes through the iris opening throughout the entire paths of both beams.

[quote:8a398ca61d]If the polarization is 45 degree to the vertical then is the "plane" of the optics really in a plane just because they are a relative distance from the table?[/quote:8a398ca61d]

I think we may be confused with the word 'plane'. As Michael says, two lines form a plane. In case of polarisation, the two lines are the incoming beam and the reflected beam. The beam can come in at any angle and, so long as it isn't reflected, there's no plane of incidence no matter what the angle of the electric vector is. The electric vector, by and of itself does not form a plane since the vector determines the line of polarisation. Thus you can have a circularly polarised beam in which the E-vector, the "polarisation" is rapidly revolving about the beam direction, even though no plane of incidence has been defined. Once the beam reflects, the line joining the incoming beam to the outgoing beam forms a plane So, if you have a beam traveling parallel to your table and it reflects off a mirror so as toi change direction but still be parallel to your table, the two beams (incoming and reflected) form a plane of incidence. If the beam is plane polarised that is polarised along only one line,, then the direction of the E-vector, the polarisation direction, can be stated with reference to this plane of incidence. However, the beam can also be circularly polarised, in which case the E-vector rotates about a line along the beam, or "unpolarised" ("randomly polarised") in which case the E-vector is along a particalr line, but that line varies very, very quickly. In neither of these cases is the polarisation states p or s and the E-vector does not define any plane.

However, if the E-vector is in one of two special states, either at right angles to the plane of incidence or parallal to it, then the polarisation state is completely s or completely p. Note the plane itself does not have to be either vertical or horizontal, only the polarisation vector relative to the plane. If the polarisation is either completely p or completely s, then certain effects pertaining to either p or s would take place, the twisting of the polarisation vector when the beam is perpendicularly reflected, for instance. If the beam is not completely polarised in p or s, but is at an angle to the plane of incidence, then the polarisation can be resolved into p or s. Thus if the polarisation is at an angle theta to the plane of incidence, then the polarisation state

\P> = cos(theta)|p> + sin(theta)|s>

In this situation, any p effect will take place with a "strength" of cos(theta) and any 's' effect will take place with a "strength" of sin(theta)

If the beam is at 45 degrees to the plane of incidence, then you get 1/2 of p and 1/2 of s since cos(45)=sin(45). Thus for holography, you get "cos(theta) of woodgrain" and "sin(theta) of no woodgrain" also the fringe contrast is dependant on s or p and so the fringe contrast is now a mixture of the cos and sin terms. Had the beam been pure s, there'd be woodgrain but maximum fringe contrast and had the beam been pure p there'd be no woodgrain (only at brewster's angle) but reduced fringe contrast. However, if the beam is purely vertically or horizontally polarised, but the plane of incidence is at an angle to the horizontal then the beam is now partially p and partially s. Once again, this has repurcussions on the fringe contrast.

There are two guiding principles":

1. the polarisation can never be along the beam direction

2. The s or p states maintain along any plane. if the plane is not constant, then neither is the constancy of s or p.

In the case of a holographic setup where the beams go up and down through small angles and are not uniformly parallal or perpendicular to the table, condition 2 applies and the polarisation states vary between s and p. Therefore the hologram suffers.

[quote:1e196d7eb4]why is a polarization axis finder so expensive? [/quote:1e196d7eb4]

You can use standard polarised sunglasses. The pass axis is vertical since it's designed to stop glare from sunlight. Glare from reflected sunlight is s polarised, which, because the plane of incidence is vertical, happens to be horizontal. By stopping horizontally polairsed light, you cut the glare.

Unfortunately, I don't understand what your 'angle A' and 'angle B' are. The polarisation states, s and p, are defined by two beams, one beam of light alone cannot decide the state of polarisation. The two beams must be formed by reflection from a mirror or mirror-like surface, eg water. I don't see the incoming beam in your diagram however, the beam seems to be going slightly upwards. So let's assume that the plane you've drawn is a mirror and the beam you've drawn is the reflection of a beam coming in from below. In this case, the plane of incidence is vertical, because the lines of the incident beam (from below) and the reflected beam (going up) form a vertical plane. It's an imaginary piece of paper that lies alongside the two beams (incident and reflected). If I say, "Take a piece of paper and line it up along the beams" you can see that with only one beam, the paper can take on any orientation. You need two beams to orientate the paper. The orientation of the paper is the plane of incidence.

In this case, since the plane of incidence is vertical, the ligt in 1 is s polarised. This is because it's polarised perpendicular to the plane of incidence - across this imaginary piece of paper. Picture 2 is p polarised because it's polarised parallel to the plane of incidence - along the imaginary piece of paper.

If you draw a line at right angles to the mirror surface, the angle between this line and the reflected beam is the angle of reflection. When this angle is zero, there is no plane of incidence (only one beam) and so reflections for both s and p are the same (4% for glass). As the angle varies, the reflection for s and p change. For p (picture 2 under our assumption that the incoming beam comes in from below), the reflected beam fiorst falls gradually, ie it gets dimmer and dimmer. Then finally at some angle known as Brewsters angle, it disappears entirely. Then it increases, getting brighter and brighter, until around about 80 degrees it reaches a maximum when it's strength equals the incoming beam. For s polarisation, the reflected beam starts at 4%, and gets stronger and stronger as the angle increases. At roughly 80 degrees, the reflections are equal again. Therefore if you reflect a beam of light from a piece of glass at Brewsters angle, there will be no p in the reflected beam. If the beam is all p, then there will be no light at all. If the reflected beam is partly p and partly s, then there will be some reflected light from the s component.

In holography, if your shooting with a horizontal reference, ie all the beams are the same height from the tabletop, then all the reflections are in a horizontal plane. In this case, vertical polarisation is s and horizontal polarisation is p. When the reference beam hits the plate, some of it skips past the front surface and some goes through to the back surface. The light that hits the back surface then is reflected back to the front surface which is then reflected to the back surface etc. Whereever the emulsion is - front or back surface -it'll record all these reflections and you see a pattern like woodgrain. To avoid all this woodgrain, if you shoot at Bresters angle with horizontal polarisation, the reflected beams intensity should be zero. That said, it's important to realise that the even if the beam is perfectly horizontally poalrised, but the plane formed by the various beams is not in a perfectly horizontal plane, you won't get all p and so there will be some reflections. So if one mirror takes your beam a little bit up and another takes it a little bit down, the plane is not horizontal and you have part s and part p. The s component will reflect and so you'll get some woodgrain. If you shoot with an overhead mirror, the plane of incidence is vertical and so the beam needs to be vertically polarised to be p . The trick with the raising of levels to alter polarisation only works when all the beams are reflected at right angles, so be sure that the beam hitting the last mirror before the light is sent up at some angle is vertically polarised.

Well, back on the polarization topic. There was a previous post that bothered me when I read it and I've finally got time to reply.

> Dinesh: I think we may be confused with the word 'plane'. As Michael says, two lines form a plane. In case of polarisation, the two lines are the incoming beam and the reflected beam. The beam can come in at any angle and, so long as it isn't reflected, there's no plane of incidence no matter what the angle of the electric vector is. The electric vector, by and of itself does not form a plane since the vector determines the line of polarisation. Thus you can have a circularly polarised beam in which the E-vector, the "polarisation" is rapidly revolving about the beam direction, even though no plane of incidence has been defined.

There are actually several things that form planes. Of these are three points, a line and a point, a line and a vector, two perpendicular vectors and so on...

A linearly polarized laser can be thought of as an emitter with two fixed vectors. The beam being the first vector and the polarization vector being the second. Because the pv is perpendicular to the beam the two form a plane regardless of the presence or absence of a reflected beam.

For me, it helps to think about the reflection in terms of up/down and left/right relative to the plane formed by the beam and pv.

If you're paying attention to the brewster angle (which I do since I haven't been able to get myself to index match with any regularity and I do use film rather than plates) then you should use p-polarization (parallel).
