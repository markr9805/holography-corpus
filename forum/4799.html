Joe Farina Mon May 02, 2005 6:44 pm #1
When the image from a color hologram is reconstructed, the colors will depend on the spectral composition of the illuminating source.

But could a color hologram be "tailor made" for a specific type of illumination?

Say for example, one wanted to make a color hologram, and illuminate it with a white LED. The spectral makeup of white LED's can be quite irregular (perhaps they all are) with a strong spike in the blue. I assume that if a color hologram were made without giving any thought to the final illuminating source, and this hologram were lit with such an LED, the colors would be quite distorted. I guess there will be some distortion no matter what natural (i.e., direct sunlight) or artificial (halogen, LED, etc.) light source is used, but it should be an acceptable amount of distortion (i.e., not that noticeable).

I was trying to figure out a method for making color holograms based on the final illuminating source, and this is what I came up with.

1) Prepare the object by painting it with colors that look good under the kind of illumination that will be used to reconstruct the holographic image later (say in this case a white LED).

2) The spectral composition of the illumination will need to be known at this point. Say there is an accurate graph showing it. (On a side note, is there an instrument which can measure this? I think it would be better to rely on measurements rather than published graphs. I have a cheap spectroscope with a diffraction grating and a scale, but I was looking for something more precise.)

For the sake of argument, say the white-light illumination source has a "value" of "50 units" at 457nm, 40 at 532nm, and 30 at 633nm. Assume these arbitrary units are linear measurements of light intensity.

Take the three lasers and match them to the illumination source. That is, plan on 50mW for the 457nm exposure, 40mW for the 532nm exposure, and 30mW for the 633nm exposure.

Granted, there will be only three points on the "graph" of the white-light source which will be matched by the three lasers. (Of course, if four lasers were used, another point on the graph could be matched.)

So, at this point, the object should still look good under laser illumination, close to what it appeared like under the white-light source, I assume.

3) Since the holographic recording material will have a non-uniform response to the various wavelengths, the above figures (50mW, 40mW, and 30mW) should then be modified, based on this non-uniform response. I guess the wavelength-dependent response will need to be determined by experiment (say for example when PFG-03C is considered), even though the graph has been published.

Assuming the hologram is processed without shrinkage, when the hologram is illuminated with the orginal white-light source (the white LED), then the reconstructed image should be pretty close to what was seen originally. At least I think so.

I am going through all this rigmarole because I want to use white-light LED's for lighting color holograms, and these devices have a really irregular spectral output.

Joe Farina Tue May 03, 2005 12:27 pm #4
Perhaps the Luxeon warm white LED's are slightly better than the cold white ones, but they still have a highly irregular spectral output.

According to their charts, some spectral output figures for the warm white LEDs are:

450nm......0.2

500nm......0.4

550nm......0.75

650nm......0.9

The cold white LED's:

450nm......1.0

500nm......0.2

550nm......0.6

650nm......0.2

Although the warm white ones have a smoother curve, they simply have more red, the cold ones more blue.

Direct sunlight has a substantially different kind of spectral output. While not a "flat horizontal line" across the wavelengths, it is much closer to this than the artificial sources that I know of. For sunlight, the intensity does drop off somewhat in the blue, however. On the other hand, diffuse skylight coming from the north on a clear day has a very large amount of blue, as would be expected. Of course, this kind of light is diffuse, and wouldn't be good for holograms.

The point I'm trying to make is that variously-colored surfaces change their appearance, sometimes dramatically, depending on the color characteristics of the illumination. I think this should be kept in mind when making color holograms.

Kaveh Wed May 04, 2005 3:30 am #6
Color holography is always complex, but here are some thoughts.

First question is, do you want to make a natural color hologram, or a 'pseudocolor' one? If you are thinking of preparing the object by painting it, it appears you are looking at the second type, as the colors will not be those of the natural object.

Your LED will have peaks at certain points, but will emanate light throughout the visible spectrum, but when you use lasers to record, you only have information on those specific wavelengths. So even if the peaks of the LED are matched to your recording wavelengths, you may not get the same colors reproduced.

I think that most white LEDs use blue/yellow to get white. At least in the early days this was so. These are not good colors to record a color hologram with if you want natural color. You only have say 3 points of the spectum to test when recording, so it is important to choose wavelengths carefully. In my opinion the ideal wavelengths are around 450, 540, and 610 (looking at other people's data). So the wavelengths you mention should give very good color rendition.

There is a childishly simple way to find out if your wavelenghs can render colors well, and that is to illuminate an object with them and just look at different objects. This I think is as good as any theoretical analysis. You will be amazed how good images can look in just two wavelengths!

So my approach to making natural color holograms, if that's what you want to do, is to choose the wavelengths first, then choose a source that has enough light in these wavelengths, to reconstruct the image.

Regarding the ratio of the powers, I think that the eye is quite foregiving. Changing the ratio of powers in each wavelength is equivalent to changing the color temperature of the light which the eye adjusts to rapidly.
