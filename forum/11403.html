There was a bit of handwaving. You can only see the entire image from a cut piece of the original hologram if the image was far enough away, and the cut piece was large enough, relative to the image. In other words, the angle subtended by the image at some point at some distance from the film must be equal to the angle subtended by the film at the same point. If the image is of height i, and the piece of film is of dimension p, then the defining characteristic is the i/p must be less than the distance of the image from the film. So, for example, you cannot see a 10' tree a foot away through a hole half an inch wide.

There were also a few errors. Thomas young did not use a 'monochromatic source" for his two-slit experiments. "monochromatic" sources did not exist in 1801, and don't exist today. A monochromatic source would have to have an infinitely thin linewidth. Also, it's a common error to assume that the two-slit experiment "proved" that light is a wave. I think this source of error arises because De Broglie's hypothesis that particles had wavelike characteristics was proved with a two-slit experiment. Huygens (1629 - 1695) had already postulated a wave theory. Grimaldi (1618 - 1663) had already discovered diffraction (a wave effect) and Malus (1775 - 1812) had discovered polarisation. What Young did was to alter the polarisation of the light going to one slit. If the polarisation of light going through one slit was perpendicularly polarised to the other, the interference disappeared. This showed that light was a transverse wave - a fact not known till then.
