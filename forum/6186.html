And I wondered if you could also make a reflection hologram on a wet HRT plate. Since reflection holograms are more prone to failure due to “movement”, you might have to slow the process of water evaporation (which will inevitably take place – unless you're doing your exposure in a 100% humidity environment). I have not gathered what size beam diam, was but with one minute exposure in wet TEA it must have been grossly, grossly overexposed.

Long bleaching time is a symptom of that too.

BB640 plates untreated with TEA keep for years stored even at room temp. then once given the TEA treatment they are as good as new.

Transmission holos allow you to get away with murder on movement with only a 5mw laser where "murder" means murdering any potential reflection fringes by gross swellings or contractions occurring perpendicularly to the plane of the emulsion but that still allows perpendicular transmission fringes to form OK to form visible transmission images. However, even these fringes are going to be distorted and will replay at strange unintended viewing angles if you make them in very swollen emulsion. As Martin and Dave suggest , working with swollen emulsion and a few seconds of exposure time you do need to cover it to stabilize it with a glass sheet, or if perfect transparency is not essential then ordinary thin polythene works fine (with all the air bubbles pushed out of course ) otherwise you can use cellophane as a non -optically active plastic . You must also give it all TIME to settle , with a 5mw HeNe and wet emulsion I never find I can get away with less than about 30 minutes settling time usually over an hour is needed.

Uli Wed Apr 18, 2007 3:25 pm #11
The best bleach that I have used with our plates is EDTA bleach. I do not know the composition of JD2, but if as Martin suggest, is a dichromate reversal, it will work well in transmission, but not in reflection, you get a bit of a foggy image. Among other sources (Saxby's or Bjelkhagen books, for instance) you can find the recipe for EDTA bleach in our webpage at colourholographic.com.

Also, an ascorbic acid developer like the one you mention works very well for transmission holograms and reflection gratings, but for image reflection holography I will recommend you to use Pyrogallol developer (again you can check our webpage) with EDTA.

It is weird to use the wet plate for recording, maybe you can do that in transmission (the spatial frequecy is much smaller), but I would be surprised if you will get anything in reflection. I recommend you the following procedure:

3% TEA solution (as you have used) for 2 minutes,

soak the plate in pure water (deionised or distilled) for another minute or 2,

dry the plate using a squeegee or blotting paper with a photographic roller (I personnally prefer the last method) and worm air (a hair dryer not too close to the plate and warming the glass side of the plate, do not get the plate too warm),

leave the plate to recover temperature for 10 or 20 minute (in reflection is better to be on the safe side).

Continue with the exposure and processing.

Good luck

That makes me wonder if anybody ever tried the Walker/Benton pseudo-color method on HRT plates. The method consisted in using various isopropyl-water concentrations to provide different degrees of emulsion swelling and hence color control. See: Walker/Benton, In-situ swelling for holographic color control, SPIE 1051, 1989 or US 4986619, Walker/Benton, Holographic color control systems.

Our department has been making reflection gratings in aqueous solutions

for 15 years and to tell you the honest truth, one of our number here only today just asked me to show her how to shoot a DRY hologram!!!

(The only difference will be more exposure needed.) Quite a few people have shot hundreds of holographic gratings as part of their PhD theses and have gone off into the world with no awareness of shooting ordinary dry ones other than the initial introductory one of a trivial toy that I first showed them when I demonstrated the Denisyuk holography principles.

On Martin's question about the Benton/Walker method yes we have frequently had to use that (using just methanol or ethanol/water rather than ipa) to get the colour of the grating within the right range for our spectrometer measurements. Otherwise the hologram on measuring pH, or glucose levels or metal ions and so on will go out of range.

Uli Thu Apr 19, 2007 8:34 am #20
Rudy Berkhout has been using what he calls a "liquid gate" with a solution of water and alcohol, and with the plate immersed in the liquid, changing the rate of the solution between exposures to get different swellings-->colours. He needed a long time for the solution to settle to avoid fringes, since he was shooting reflection holograms. I bet that Jeff made something similar for his wet holograms. The difference between this process and what holography4fun has made is that he is exposing with the plate drying in the air, and this is a dynamic process. For a transmission grating, especially if he is using a symmetric setup and the fringes are perpendicular to the plate, there would not be a big problem (apart from non flatness of the emulsion-glass layer). Once you get away from this setup is when everything will probably go wrong.

I agree, exposing a hologram in a liquid gate vs. exposing a wet emulsion in plain air isn't quite the same. Having said that, I'd like to stress on the importance of controlling air humidity of the recording environment in the latter case.
