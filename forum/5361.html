Ed Wesly Thu Feb 02, 2006 7:47 pm #1
I just read a comical post about someone trying to make a multi-color transmission H1 to be transferred to a multi-color copy. Red and blue wavelengths were involved, and both of the beams followed the same optical paths. The object was exposed to red light, with a red reference beam, and then a blue exposure was made, with the same object lighting and reference angle.

Professor Equation was surprised, much to his chagrin, that there were two images reconstructed simultaneously when this master was lit with one or the other wavelengths! For instance, when the H1 was lit with red light, an image was formed where the object had been, plus another spurious one at a different angle, where the blue wavelength's interference pattern diffracted the red light. Evidently this holographer had not read the papers of our own Kaveh Bazargan, amongst others. (probably because there were not enough equations in it!)

Which led me to think of how to record two or three different lambdas on one plate without them cross-talking. If the H1 were recorded in a reflection hologram mode, with the collimated reference beam from the side opposite the object, but instead of a Denisyuk mode where this same beam is also the object light the object is far enough away so that it does not receive any of this light but is independently lit, like a transmission H1. A multiple beam reflection hologram, really nothing new, only the fact that the object is far enough away from the plate to clear the conjugate beam which is flushing out the real image. Then the H2 is placed in this reflection H1's real image to make a copy, with the reference beam coming in from the appropriate side to record a transmission or reflection copy.

Anyhow I am sure that I am not the first one to think of this scheme, but I am wondering if anyone here has themselves or knows of someone who has tried this, namely an H2 from a reflection H1, in a split beam mode, not like the way dichromated pendants are made in a single beam technique. The "only" perils I can think of are the usual extra stability requirements necessary for split beam reflection holography, plus the trick of keeping the processing suitably non-shrinking to not alter the reference angle. And of course the attendant drop in diffraction efficiency from the multiple fringe patterns.

Because a multiple color H1-H2 scheme might require either a recording set up with independent reference angles for transmission style H1's, more optics to align, or recording on three separate plates, then with insane tolerances for repositioning, it might be worthwhile to have the one plate with all three lambdas recorded on it with collinear reference beams. One would think that a red recorded and red replaying reflection holographic image would not conjure up an image when illuminated with blue or green light, but not being well-versed in Kogelnik's Coupled Wave Equation I might be wrong. (Perhaps I will have to call in Professor Equation as a consultant!)

I don't know anyone who has done this, but it's a good way to circumvent the cross talk. But there are headaches that you have pointed to. Shrinkage would have to be the top one for me. The drop in diffraction efficiency only means a longer exposure for the transfer stage.

There may even be a hidden advantage. You may get less grain noise, i.e. scatter. Most scattering is in the general direction of the reference beam, i.e. in the opposite direction to the image.
BobH Fri Feb 03, 2006 12:10 am #3
I've done something very similar, to accomplish image reduction. The technique is as follows:

a) Record a conventional transmission (master) hologram on a 12x16" plate, with the object (let's say) 10" behind the plate.

b) Record a large reflection hologram (H2) of the reconstructed pseudoscopic real image from the master hologram, with the new recording plane (let's say) 20" from the master.

c) Reconstruct the (now orthoscopic) real image into a negative lens of adequate diameter so as not to vignette, and placed to provide an image of reduced size.

d) Record another transmission hologram (H3) at a distance from the lens to allow its reference beam to miss the lens.

e) Use the H3 to make a final hologram of the reduced image.

I know, scatter city! If all the processing is done with this in mind, however, the results are acceptable. I used a reflection geometry for the H2 to fit the system on my table at the time. Steps b to d are what Ed suggests, with the object being a transmission hologram.

This is a problem in all true color holography.

The other problem is in re-creating white. There is a reason that Yves uses very bright colors.

I have thought about this a little and came to the conclusion that I would have to make a film holder that used gravity and 6 contact points made from ball bearings to make three transmission H1 masters. Tom B. Sat Feb 04, 2006 5:11 am #9
> Martin: [I actually assumed the recordings to be made on one emulsion. But I was wrong about my statement: if the emulsion collapses, the wavelength shift of the red exposure will become larger compared to the blue exposure.

This was surprising at first, but I can see it being true if you are talking about absolute shift rather than percentage shift, e.g. 500 nm and 600 nm fringes recorded with a uniform 10% shrinkage would give a -50 nm shift for 500 and a -60 nm shift for 600 nm.

(later edit) - this is for fringes parallel to the emulsion plane. Slanted fringes would be shifted less; for perpendicular fringes (transmission holo) there would be no shift at all with shrinkage.
