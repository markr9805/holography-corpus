JohnFP Fri Jan 28, 2005 5:54 pm #2
I played with color shifting toward the red end of the spectrum with slavich film a while ago and you may be interested in the technique. I used regular sugar disolved in water. I developed the hologram as usual and let it dry. Then I soaked the finished hologram in the sugar water concentration and dry as normal again. Different concentrations of sugar yield different shifts. The good part about this techniuqe is I was able to repeat the process many times. Start with a low amount of sugar concentration and work up. Although I do not have exact numbers the technique does lend itself to fine tuning.

Good luck.
Tom B. Sat Jan 29, 2005 9:13 am #3
It's not clear if you want a permanent shift for display, or just want a temporary shift. For a temporary adjustable shift, humidity works - just seal the holo in a closed (transparent) container with a damp sponge while illuminating with the green laser. At peak brightness, remove the sponge, and maybe add some dry air to back off a bit (it's hard to recognize a peak until it has passed)

For a semi-temporary shift, any sugar, corn syrup etc. will do. The downside is that microbes also find these things tasty and they might end up eating or fermenting your hologram if you don't wash it out afterward. Saxby suggests as a starting point using similar ranges of concentrations (5-20%) as for TEA, resulting in similar color shifts.

For a permanent shift, Sorbitol (a sugar substitute) is often used. It's less attractive to bacteria and health food stores supposedly stock it - but not in my town. They did, however have Xylitol, even more distasteful to bacteria than Sorbitol - I have not tried it yet for swelling, but it tastes pretty good

Tom B. Sun Apr 03, 2005 5:32 am #14
> Joe: If I may ask, where has it been said that printout is self-limiting in finer grain emulsions?

I tried to find the quote in Bjelkhagen "Silver Halide Recording Materials for holography.. 2nd ed." and found (P. 208) "... in display holography which uses high resolution materials, darkening of a phase hologram reaches a maximum after which no further darkening takes place". Admittedly this is a bit weak, since the maximum could be pitch black but it has been my experience that sunlight exposed holograms on unprotected fine-grain materials turn more or less grey, but never get really dark.

After lots of rinsing I now do a final rinse in 1% acetic acid (4% cheap white vinegar diluted to 1%) as per Phillips [referenced on p. 211]. The Vila and Wesley study referenced on the same page and summarized on p. 212 indicated that this treatment gave good protection. One downside of the vinegar bath is that it interferes with photoflo so sqeegeeing is a pretty squeaky awkward affair. But then I believe that photoflo has also been implicated in printout, so maybe it's better not to use it anyway.

Not sure what drugstore iodine tincture is based on exactly, but I assume it's those 25mg/L of Iodine and Potassium Iodide dissolved in ethanol. Its concentration looks rather weak compared to a iodine bleach like this for example (from Markov):

iodine..................10.0g

KI...........................1.0

ethyl alcohol........750ml

water..................250ml

By the way this is a very interesting bleach for ultra-fine emulsions. Actually, Markov introduced it for full-color holograms on PFG-03c processed in a colloidal developer.

It's likely to provide enhanced printout stability.

Regarding the yellow stain formed by that bleach, a short rinse in 50g/L ascorbic acid, followed by a thorough water rinse used to clear the emulsion (PFG-03).
