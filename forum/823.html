Ed Wesly Mon Mar 17, 2014 7:22 am #3
Developing only 5 seconds? That’s pretty short! When I shoot the equivaent to the Slavich PFG-03C, the Sphere-S GEO-3, I standardize at 30 seconds development time and vary the exposure time to make a decent density. [http://nlutie.com/ewesly/HnEPortfolio/ColdJD4.html](http://nlutie.com/ewesly/HnEPortfolio/ColdJD4.html)

So if you go this route, you will probably decrease your exposure, which helps out for stability, as it looks like in your second photo you might be having some plate movement; the bottom doesn’t move enough, but as you get up higher the plate is moving more, and you lose the image. Think of a door closing; the side nearest the hinge doesn’t move as much as the edge opposite the hinge. So check your plateholder for stability.

The white patchy haze is caused by too much density, so you could stand to spread out your reference beam a little more to make it even all over the late. Do you have a spatial filter yet? If you are doing this type of hologram, it would be a good idea.

Slavich is not noted for their coating quality, so the spots could be there courtesy of the factory. Developing a fogged plate, with no holo exposure, might show these same spots.

For something like this material, with its super fine grains, it is prone to fog from the oxygen in the atmosphere! This is why the Ultimate plates come vacuum-packed. Some of your brightness problems may be arising from the plates developing some fog density on top of the holographic exposure. Cut off a chunk of a plate and develop it and see if it turns dark with no exposure to light. If it does, then use a rehalogenating bleach to erase the fog. [http://nlutie.com/ewesly/Publications/recycle.pdf](http://nlutie.com/ewesly/Publications/recycle.pdf)

Whatever they use for gelatin in both the Slavich and Sphere-S plates does take a long time to dry. If you use alcohol baths after the wash then it will dry quicker. I haven’t tried it, but Stas from Geola says it’s a must. [http://materials.geola.lt/index.php/holomaterials/color](http://materials.geola.lt/index.php/holomaterials/color) It does make sense, but I just let the GEO-3 dry overnight.

It is normal to see a “spurious transmission image” (either Jeff Blyth or Graham Saxby dubbed it that in a holosphere article). A transmission image is formed simultaneously with the reflection image, with the reflection of the reflection hologram’s reference from the back of the plate providing the reference for the transmission image.

Looks like you are on the right track, the color of the doll’s head looks pretty real, sometimes I wonder if the blue component is necessary for some scenes.

Keep up the good work, you are almost there!

Dinesh Thu Mar 20, 2014 11:27 am #17
Perhaps the increase in hardness is a result of nucleation by impurities in the gelatin. Perhaps the coldness of the water causes polymerisation with impurities in the gelatin (or in the gelatin itself), because of the lower KE and so the higher the capture probability and so the higher the probability of polymerisation (especially considering the polarity of the water molecule) while the warmer water has a higher KE and so lowering the capture cross section.

Of course the another explanation is that lower the temperature of the water, the more the precipitation of impurities. Some of these impurities may polymerise with the gelatin molecule. For example certain chromium salts are known gelatin hardeners. Thus, if these substances were dissolved in the water, then decreasing the temperature of the water would precipitate them into the gelatin matrix. The presence of the gelatin may act as a catalyst for the precipitation.

The internet is a perfect crucible for experimental verification! If people across the globe measure the increase in hardness of gelatin and find a consistent result, it shows that the culprit is the water itself, since correlation of impurities in water on a global scale has a very low probability. However, if there is a variation of hardness across the globe for the same temperature, then I would suspect dissolved substances in the water.

Regarding cold water treatment:

I don't think it has anything to do with gelatin hardening.

A cold water pre-soak simply provides the means for even development throughout the emulsion - particularly if the developer temperature has to be kept low (to prevent gelatin damage as in the PFG-03 case).

As for the JD-4 hardening action, it's probably due to its metol part. The oxidized metol seems to form a PBQ-like agent that can crosslink gelatin (as can be seen by the "PBU-metol" bleach).

Ed Wesly Sun Mar 23, 2014 5:37 am #29
It doesn't have to be that close to freezing cold! For the cold pre-soak, I just use the cold water that comes out of the pipes, typical Chicago water temperature from the cold tap is in the 45 to 50 degree Fahrenheit range, 7 to 10 Centigrade, and that is fine for the pre-soak.

Maintaining the 65F (18C) is done simply by putting the tray inside of a larger tray with cold tap water, or even ice cubes. These are all old school photography darkroom operating procedures.

I have stainless steel developing tanks that sit in a bigger bowl with the cold water when developing 4 by 5's. No need for refrigerators, but what is great is an automatic temperature regulating faucet. Then the darkroom sink can be filled with the temperature stabilized water and the trays sit inside that.

Whatever the mechanism for the success of this processing tip, it is essential for these materials, just try processing without it, and you will see color blotches or the emulsion come clean off the plate! Pre-soaks in photographic processing have been around forever, and it does promote even development, but is not necessary for all emulsions. Photo paper usually doesn't need it, sometimes it does help film. But the ultra-fine grain holo products do respond nicely to this regime!

I don't seem to be hitting the real sweet spot an not really sure if theissue is development or exposure. While I was able to get two colors with both single beam and split beam systems, but never really got everything to work right at the sam time. Never really got the sensitivity/exposure curves for the to colors refined as I would like - I seem to have gotten the best results with 623 at 3000 uW and 532 at 8000 uW.
