To my understanding, most dot matrix holograms are 2D. They are composed of a grid of diffractive dots that can be encoded to be different spectral colours based on the grating equation. But I’ve read about 3D dot matrix holograms, wherein the hologram is divided into so-called hogel areas that each contain a series of diffractive dots. If diffractive dots are simple diffraction gratings with fixed groove spacing per dot, how does a series of them within hogel areas create a 3D effect?

lobaz Tue Sep 23, 2025 4:15 pm #4
Great. Imagine what is going on behind one lens in a lenticular image. There you have several spots of ink. When you look at the print itself, you see just a mess. But when a lens is placed over, you see one spot from one direction, other spot from another direction and so on. With properly selected structure of ink spots and a lens (lenticular film), you can easily create a flip effect, an animation or even 3D. It is just a matter of what images you interlace.

A dot matrix based hologram can be very similar. You split the area of a hologram into squares called hogels. Imagine this is a counterpart of one lens in a lenticular image. A hogel is composed of several dots. A dot is a counterpart of an ink spot. As a dot is in fact a grating, you can prescribe what colour should go in a particular direction - exactly what you would do with a lens and spots of ink. Got it?
JayFritoes Tue Sep 23, 2025 4:57 pm #5
So, unlike a traditional hologram, a 3D dot matrix hologram doesn’t actually reconstruct an object wavefront? It’s the illusion of 3D?
lobaz Wed Sep 24, 2025 3:12 am #6
You are right, the aim is to make an illusion. For small holographic strickers, it is often OK to use about 7 views to make a decent smooth illusion.

However, if you try to think about what happens when adding more and more views and combining them in a coherent way, you may end up with a wavefront reconstructin.
