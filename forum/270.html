I was wondering how the diameter of the input beam affects the divergence (beam spread) of the output from a spatial filter. I guess that whenever a beam has a larger diameter, the divergence will be greater (when going through the objective or lens).

On the Edmund website, they use a formula for finding the pinhole size for a spatial filter:

1.27 X 0.633 (HeNe wavelength) X 5.5 (focal length of objective in millimters) = 4.42

This is divided by the beam diameter (lets say 1.3mm) to get the "beam spot diameter" in microns (3.4) which is multiplied by 1.5 to get a suggested pinhole size of 5.1 microns (say 5).

I want to compare this to using a 9mm focal length objective (with a larger beam diameter) but keeping the pinhole at 5 microns. (I have the Edmund 5.5mm focal length "laser singlet" objective, but these objectives appear to have a problem when used with spatial filters, and have been discontinued. I have the 9mm version on order, but it hasn't arrived yet.)

Plugging the 9mm figure into the above equation (using my junior-high school algerbra, which is as far as I ever got in math), the input beam should be 2.2mm for use with the 9mm objective and 5 micron pinhole.

My question is: will the first example have a larger beam spread after the spatial filter, will they be similar, or somewhere in between?
BobH Sat Jul 16, 2011 10:35 am #2
I've always found that equations for pinhole size always resulted in diameters that are waaaaay too small for practical use, and have never used a hole smaller than 10 microns. Beam wandering because the laser is off the table and beam pointing stability of gas lasers make keeping a small hole aligned a constant hassle. Also, the artifacts in the beam profile that are being removed by the hole are typically so minor that a larger hole can be used to get an adequately filtered beam. Equations typically calculate beam waist without regard for these practical user related considerations.

Dinesh Sun Jul 17, 2011 8:46 am #4
The relationship between the beam diameter and beam spread is given by the NA (numerical aperture), usually quoted on the objective itself while the pinhole size is determined by the Airy diameter, which depends on the wavelength and the beam diameter. The calculation you quote gives the Airy diameter, but the NA of the lens gives the beam spread. The two are related and so knowing the Airy diameter (the pinhole size) can help in determining the relationship of the beam spread to the beam diameter. However, the lens will cause the beam to diverge anyway, regardless of the pinhole. The pinhole is just there to remove artifacts in the beam (possibly not so well, as Bob suggests!). It's the NA that's probably more important for the relationship of beam diameter and beam spread, which is a function of the f# of the lens.

The Airy disc diameter is(pinhole size) :

p = 1.22(lambda/d)

where d is the lens diameter. Assuming you fill the entire aperture of the lens, this is also the beam diameter.

The NA (the arcsin of the half angle of the beam spread) is given by (roughly)

NA = arcsin(arctan(d/2f))

where f is the focal length of the lens. But, f/d = f# (the f-number of the lens).

So, without knowing the f# of your 9mm lens, I can't see a way of knowing the beam diameter and the beam spread.

I don't know what the f# of a microscope objective is, off-hand, but in general, the larger the magnification factor, the smaller the focal length, the larger the NA and the wider is the beam spread. So, in answer to your question:

> Joe Farina: My question is: will the first example have a larger beam spread after the spatial filter, will they be similar, or somewhere in between?

Yes, probably, the first example will have a larger beam spread. However, without knowing the various f#'s, I can't say for sure.

Ed Wesly Mon Jul 18, 2011 12:21 pm #6
The numerical apertures marked on microscope objectives are accurate when the whole aperture of the lens is filled. This is rarely the case when inputting a laser beam.

The f/# of a lens is defined by the focal length of the lens divided by the aperture. If Joe’s 9mm f.l. laser line focusing singlet is entered by a 2mm diameter beam, then it is working at f/4.5.

Numerical aperture a la Melles Griot ([http://www.cvimellesgriot.com/products/ ... rmulas.pdf](http://www.cvimellesgriot.com/products/Documents/TechnicalGuide/Paraxial_Formulas.pdf)) is a little different than the Wikipedia entry ([http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_aperture](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_aperture)) but the illustration in M-G shows it all. Numerical aperture = sin theta, theta being the half-angle of divergence after a collimated beam leaves the lens. Numerical aperture = clear aperture/2f, so in the example above, the Numerical Aperture = 2mm/18mm = .111… This equals sin theta, theta then equals 6.379 degrees, so the full spread angle = twice that or 12.759 degrees.

So all you need to know to determine the spread angle is the size of the input beam, which is a whole other can of worms to open when measuring laser beam profiles, and the focal length of the beamspreading optic. But where’s the focal length marked on a microscope objective? Check out this chart, again from M-G, correlating magnification with focal length. [https://cvimellesgriot.com/Products/Doc ... og/OAS.pdf](https://cvimellesgriot.com/Products/Documents/Catalog/OAS.pdf)

The reason why Bob complains that suggested pinhole sizes are way too small is that the manufacturers, especially Newport, calculate the best possible case of resolution of a microscope objective assuming the objective is diffraction limited and the whole numerical aperture is used, (hardly ever the working in the lab case!) using the Airy’s disc formula posted above or a variation. ([http://www.cvimellesgriot.com/products/ ... ection.pdf](http://www.cvimellesgriot.com/products/Documents/TechnicalGuide/Lens_Selection.pdf)) Bigger NA yields smaller resolution.

You can “zoom” a microscope objective by using a pre-spreading lens. Best results are obtained by using a long focal length lens, see my reply to: [http://holoforum.org/forum/viewtopic.php?f=11&t=213](http://holoforum.org/forum/viewtopic.php?f=11&t=213)

Hope this helps!

Dinesh Mon Jul 18, 2011 3:47 pm #8
> Joe Farina: I wonder why more people don't do this, it seams like an easy way to fine-tune the relationship between the objective/pinhole, and to vary the overall divergence.

Because if you pre-expand a beam before it goes into the spatial filter, then the point at which the beam converges is no longer at the aperture of the pinhole. The pinhole is place at the focal point of the lens (for obvious reasons to do with the fourier transform of a collimated beam - remember that the pinhole is just an optical transform). If you expanded the beam, it would collapse at some other point further away than the focal point and the fourier of the noise may not be as easily transformed away. It may be possible to move the pinhole to the new position, but the relationship between the image point and the object point is not linear and the travel on the pinhole mount may not be able to track it. Another point is the laser beam is Gaussian. In this case, the Airy disc diameter is not the calculated one, since the calculated diameter is given at the focus of a lens receiving collimated light. Also, the edges of the Gaussian may not fill the aperture of the objective. If you pre-expanded it by a very small amount, you may be able to get away with it. All lasers give out raw beams that are slightly divergent anyway, the specs give you the beam divergence, so the beam is already "pre-diverged" by a very small amount. You could also use a larger than necessary pinhole and allow a little noise. Our blue Melles laser has oval beams - one dimension diverges more than the other - and we can pinhole it, but not cleanly.

Jeffrey Weil Wed Sep 14, 2011 8:46 am #11
Hello John,

I'm not sure your diagram is correct. I don't think it's about the thickness of the pin hole, its about the size of the spot you can produce with a non-best form singlet.

A compound system, like a standard microscope objective, can focus the spot to a diffraction limited size, small enough to get through the matching pinhole.

A standard singlet will form a waist larger than the objective would, aberrations from the singlet will keep it from getting through that same hole.

A best form lens might do the trick. Not sure as I've never tried to use one. Here's a decryption of a best form. You'll see in the text it talks about spot size.

> holorefugee: It has always worked for me.

True. The microscope objective we all use for a beam expander is just that, a microscope objective lens. Thus , it's designed to minimise white-light aberrations and create as near as dammit a diffraction limited spot, have a specific entrance pupil and a specific exit pupil and have good entendu. The fact that any diffraction-limited system gives rise to Airy rings is the reason we pinhole, to reject the non-zero Airy frequencies. However, singlets (by that I presume is meant a single lens, rather than a multitude lens system called a "singlet") cannot remove all aberrations unless ver-r-ry carefully aligned. Even then I'm not sure you can create a diffraction-limited focused spot with a single lens. In this case, you can't create a perfect set of Airy rings, there'll be a bit of a wobble on the rings.

However, as Jeff and Colin have said, if you can get a working version with which you're happy, forget the theory! (can't believe I just said that!) Theory gives you an understanding of an idealised system, fom which you can whittle away till you get a working version of a system. But most of the time, if you can make a working system by trial, error, a working knowledge and some elbow grease (an "empirical system", if you wish to be a little grandiose!) and you're happy with this, it's as good as any deep theoretical analysis.
