Tony Wed Apr 27, 2005 12:40 am #2
I've had success making pinholes in aluminum foil. Take a piece and fold it several times so you get many layers of foil. Use a needle and push it through several (but not all ) layers. Now sort out the pieces, and use the last one that was pierced - it will have the smallest hole. This was actually my first spatial filter - and it worked really well.

You might do a search on 'pinhole camera' to see if there are any other suggestions.

MichaelH Fri Apr 29, 2005 12:42 am #10
> JohnFP: No negatives intended, just a reminder that if what you have performes to your needs, then it is good enough.

Because there's always the possibility that another method will give you better (cleaner, brighter, quicker, sexier, pick your adjective) results.

As an on-topic example, many newbies don't realize just what a huge impact the use of a spatial filter will have on their holograms. You can be using a near-perfect lens for beam expansion and switching to a spatial filter will still often give you cleaner, brighter holograms. Sometimes you just need to try a different method just to see what it will do for you.

Granted, spending the money on an "if" is potentially a waste of money but if the equipment you're using takes constant tweaking to get the results you're after, take the plunge. For some equipment (like a SF) you won't ever regret it.

The spatial filter is a wonderful device! It makes the beam soooo clean and uniform. 8) I highly recommend them, and more is better!

Look for them on Ebay. Look for lots of equipment that might have one included. We know what they are but many others don't. Alternatively, look for good deals on miniature linear translation stages and/or x-y stages.

> JohnFP: amount of throw way for uniform coverage...

I think John meant to say that the amount of light you need to throw away through beam expansion past the edges of the plate in order to get even reference illumination will affect your purchase.

For the most even illumination you want your beam to be expanded so that it's actually larger than the plate it's illuminating. That's because the beam profile is gaussian (brighter in the center) and you'll need to throw away some light.

You might try Surplus Shed for the objectives. They have reasonable prices although I've not ordered from them and comment on the cleanliness of the lenses. You can sometimes find some good deals on eBay but there's a good chance you'll need to clean the objective before you use it
BobH Tue May 03, 2005 2:31 pm #15
I'd recommend a 25 micron pinhole with objectives at 10x, 20x, and 40x. The second pinhole I'd buy would be 10 micron, but only if the 25 didn't clean up the beam adequately after going through all of the above objectives. If the pinhole is on the large side, you'll have a much easier time aligning it and keeping the alignment.

BobH Sun May 08, 2005 3:00 pm #23
An alignment procedure follows. In addition to the spatial filter, you'll need a business card with a hole in it very slightly larger than the beam.

1. Mount the card so that the beam goes through the hole.

2. With the objective in and the pinhole out, mount the unit in the beam path, about an inch downstream from the card, so that the beam goes through the iris (at minimum opening to pass it). Be sure to mount it firmly, so that a slight bump will not knock it out of position!

3. With the iris fully open, observe the pattern of light reflected back off the objective and onto the card.

4. Adjust the position of the spatial filter body so that this reflected pattern is symmetrical around the hole in the card. This indicates that the beam is centered on the objective's optical path.

5. Remove the card and install the pinhole. With the objective as far away from the pinhole as it can be, you should see a small dim spot of light transmitted through it. Use a white card up close to the pinhole (with the room lights off if necessary) to see it. If you don't see it, move the pinhole around untill you do.

6. After finding the spot above, start translating the objective toward the pinhole. If the objective was well centered on the beam, the spot will not move as you bring the objective to focus. If the spot moves as you focus, keep adjusting its position as you focus the objective.

7. As you approach focus, you'll see the spot get much bigger and diffraction rings around it. When you're at focus, the rings will be gone. When you go past focus (getting too close to the pinhole), the rings will re-appear. Adjustments are "touchy" when close to focus.
