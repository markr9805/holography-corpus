[quote:7b9d1c966a]if i have designed a image by coreldrew, then how to make a master hologram of my design? [/quote:7b9d1c966a]

There are several ways of making a master of a flat object. The easiest way is to print the coreldraw image on transparency paper. Once you have the transparency, you need to mount it somehow. To do this, you can either sandwich the transparency between two glass plates or image it onto a photographic glass plate using an enlarger. Once mounted, you place a diffuser behind it and light the back of the diffuser. The holographic plate now "sees" a diffused light from the coreldraw image. You can also transfer the coreldraw image onto an LCD screen instead of copying it onto a transparency, but you may need to remove one surface of the LCD screen if the laser is already polarised. We covered this previously here: [http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1506](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1506)

[quote:7b9d1c966a]what is photo resist[/quote:7b9d1c966a]

Photoresist is a light sensitive material, called an "emulsion", applied to a glass plate to make holograms. The glass plate is also called a "photoresist plate". Holographic (and photographic) materials are light sensitive chemicals, that is, a chemical that alters some property such as darkness or hardness in response to light.In holography you're trying to make a photograph of an extremely small set of lines, seperated by dark bands. These lines are about 1 micrometer (micron) or about 0.025 in thick seperated by dark lines of equal thickness. In order to capture such small detail, the film must be capable of resolving down to this level of detail. In other words, the chemical must change where there are lines of light and not change where there is a dark line. Photoresist is a chemical whose hardness changes when exposed to light. This chemical is applied in a thin layer (about a micron thick) onto a glass plate. When exposed as a hologram, the photoresist hardens where it's exposed and is soft where there is no exposure. The resist plate is then put into a chemical which dissolves away the soft parts and leaves behind the harder parts. This exaggerates the difference between the soft (unexposed) parts and the hard (exposed) parts and allows you to see the hologram.

[quote:7b9d1c966a]if it is the carrier of hologram master, can it be replace by Mylar. [/quote:7b9d1c966a]

Once the hologram is on the resist plate, the "holographic data" is a surface profile. A set of hard lines that are raised with respect to the softer, unexposed areas, like mountain chains with valleys in between. This surface profile needs to be transferred to mylar (or any other plastic). To do this, the resist plate is placed in a tank of Nickel salts and electroformed (not electroplated - there's a differenence. You need to copy the surface structure, not bury it). Once electroformed, you have the exact replica of the surface structure on a sheet of Nickel. This is then used to stamp onto mylar and so transfer the hologram.

> When exposed as a hologram, the photoresist hardens where it's exposed and is soft where there is no exposure. The resist plate is then put into a chemical which dissolves away the soft parts and leaves behind the harder parts
.

Holographic Photo Resist (Shipley 1800 series) is one of the few positive-acting light sensitive materials I have encountered. Where light hits, i.e. the bright fringes, bonds are broken and that area becomes soluble in an alkali, the photo-resist developer. The bright fringe area washes away. The unexposed area remains hard.

Proof of this fact comes when we expose another hologram on the same plate as a previously processed one at work for alignment or test purposes. There is still plenty of usable resist in the unexposed area.

Bartolini in his seminal paper on photo resist for holographic recording chose this type of material because of the weak standing wave (Lippmann-like) fringe system that also appears in the layer due to back reflection at the border of the glass substrate and photo resist coating. The first fringe formed at the interface is a dim fringe. If the resist were soft right at the bottom of the coating, the whole layer would wash away.

True. Holography generally uses positive resists, where exposure causes the resist to soften, due to greater adhesion. JohnFP Wed Oct 13, 2004 11:39 am #6
I just wanted to point out the if you make a transparency of your image and use this one image as the object to make a hologram, the image will not have any depth, it will be as flat as the original, although depending upon where you place it in the holographic set up, the entire flat image will be that distance from the plate in the hologram. To make your image have depth you need to make a stereogram (multiplex hologram) which includes making many slit holograms of individual perspective transparencies of the image and combining these into a final hologram. I think HoloWorld has this process expained in detail.
