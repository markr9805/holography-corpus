[quote:38ea184d18]1. Explain why holograms made with red laser light may play back in white light as a green image. [/quote:38ea184d18]

When you interfere the two beams, one from the light reflecting off the object and one from the reference, the light forms interference planes, sheets of light seperated by sheets of dark. The books often show these as lines, but they're actually sheets. These sheets are seperated by half the wavelength of the interfering beams - 316nm if your using a HeNe at 633nm. Inside the emulsion, these sheets form physical layers. These layers may be hard gelatin seperated by softer gelatin or layers of gelatin with developed silver seperated by layers of gelatin with no silver. In any case, there is a physical difference between these layers. When white light hits the hologram, the light partially reflects off the layer interfaces. As the white light goes through the hologram (emulsion) and partially reflects off these layer interfaces, it meets the reflected light from the next interface, So light going through interface 1/2 meets light that's been partially reflected back from interface 2/3. This forward going light meeting with the back reflected light causes miniature interferences inside the emulsion. As a result, the only light that comes out, the image light, is one of a wavelength given by twice the layer seperation. If the hologram is shot with a HeNe at 633, then, before development, a so-called "latent image" of the hologram exists with a layer seperation of 316 nm. When the hologram is developed the various chemical processes cause the gelatin to shrink. This shrinkage now has the effect of "squeezing" the previously 316nm layer seperation into , say, 260nm seperation. In other words, the gelatin collpased by about 18% ((260-316)/316) and so did the layer seperation. The object now seems to be a color of twice the new layer seperation, in this case 2*260, or 520nm - green. The exact collapse is very dependant on the kind of development process you use. Properly developed, it won't shrink at all. Of course, if you want the hologram to come out a particular color, you choose the appropriate development to cause the appropriate shrinkage.

[quote:38ea184d18]. This one I have absolutely no idea about. The question is how is it possible to make a curved surface act like a flat plate mirror using holographic techniques. [/quote:38ea184d18]

The exact form of the object light coming out of the hologram depends on the shape of the layers in the emulsion. In a display hologram, the reference beam is uniform and, if collimated, plane. Thus the wavefront of the reference is like a flat bedsheet going towards the hologram. The reflected object light is a wave that very twisted - like a bedsheet held up in a strong wind. The resulting interference layers are a combination of these two wavefronts and so are also twisted and turned inside the emulsion. However, if you controlled both the object wavefront and the reference wavefront, so that they were planar wavefronts, then regardless of the physical shape of the hologram itself, the layers inside the emulsion would also be planar and flat. Light diffracting off such flat layers inside the emulsion would also be flat and so you'd have the hologram behaving as a flat mirror, no matter what the actual physical geometry of the entire emulsion - curved in yuor case.
