Ron Michael Mon Jul 17, 2006 1:22 am #2
Two reasons.

Direct reflection hologram is taken instead of a H1/H2 setup.

The person sits further back (a requirement of this setup regarding reference beam).

Both of these contribute to a higher demand on spatial frequency

resolution of the film.

This limitation causes very slight natural diffusion of the subject image. Instead of seeing pores etc of a person's face you have have a nice smoother image of that person. Detail is there.

I use glamour lighting technique to enhance my subject's features. I've been a photographer since high school and I tried instead to use artistic lighting and technique to give my subjects a more natural look

I don't use use a harsh lighting technique but opt for softer fill light in addition to the main light.

If you take pictures underwater with a simple flash camera you get a lot of backscatter from the particles in the water. But at a 45 degree angle lighting technique (similar to glamour lighting) you get the pictures on Scuba magazines. Similar if you pancake light someone you get more of the deeper reflections of the IR lightning. So it's a technique issue as well.

I guarantee you can come and look at these personally and you will find natural lighting appearance of the subjects as if it was taken in white diffused light very similar to natural photography, very good detail without overt razor sharpness and in using a processing chemistry like SM-6 and a reversal bleach I get the subject playback back toward red-orange for a more brighter playback.

I've seen some holographers' work and they have managed to over contrast the image, or pancake light them, managed to give them a dead look, I could count the pores in the face and yes in that pancake look very waxy. Yet I have seen images of others' ruby work which was excellent and are famous images.

With ruby you need brighter playback for display, so you need to get the playback frequency down to orange which reversal bleaches can do instead of TEA preswell and the extra trouble of that. Green laser can give you the hulk look, and you don't want to preswell or reverse bleach them unless you want to look like papa smurf. I will start off by offering my opinion of this image. Contrast is somewhat higher in this image that others taken earlier and this may be related to the lost of sensitivity of this film plate. The plates are much less sensitive that the film I have.

Some loss of detail especially along the lips is related to the light (can't change physics).

Additionally some loss of detail is related to the reflection setup. In another image where a person holds a toy helicopter up toward the plate, you see in excellent sharpness the helicopter, but as the distance increases down the arm and toward the person a general loss of resolution begins.

This particular image has some processing bath scum in it.

On the other side, overall the person has a natural look, the glamour lighting is apparent by the slight nose shadow.

In images too stark real (high resolution H1/H2) people looked dead because it looked so real that it was unnatural for them not to be moving or breathing in the image.

In portraits, it is important to make people look natural, I think the artist in Hans' portrait achieved that balance with good image detail.

I too have been tempted to do H1/H2 to improve image quality because the transmission can have better image quality and I can move the image a little closer in the H2 process to relieve the spatial resolution requirements of the reflection setup. I just want to avoid the dead guy look.
BobH Mon Jul 17, 2006 3:43 pm #7
> and taking advantage of the lower resolution requirements, since in reflection setup with this film you are limited to about a foot depth of field.

The "depth of field" is determined by the reconstruction source, transmission or reflection, not by the film's resolution.

> With ruby you need brighter playback for display, so you need to get the playback frequency down to orange

If a hologram is dim, you need a brighter light to play it back. If it's dim because it's too red, however, I agree that shrinking it will make it brighter. Again, though, this has nothing to do with the choice of red versus green as the recording wavelenght. If a green laser is used to make an H1, a red laser can be used to make the H2 and the color can be set by the same processing methods.

Personally, I don't think either ruby or YAG are good lasers to use for portraiture. Ruby light soaks into the skin, and washes all detail out of the eyes and lips. Green light is too harsh, and highlights all flaws on the skin. In my opinion, pulsed laser holography of people is doomed to the failure it's experienced to date until an RGB laser source is developed, and the image can be reduced in size (even slightly). Most people are "weirded to the bone" when they see a pulsed holographic portrait, regardless of the artistic design of the scene or its lighting.
