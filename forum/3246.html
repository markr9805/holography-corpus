I am looking for a theoretical understanding for the effect of time smear in a multiplex hologram. Can anyone explain it to me or provide me with a reference?

For those who do not know, time smear is where the motion (in a hologram that is best described by the terms "multiplexed holographically encoded stereogram") blurs because the motion is faster than can be portrayed by the medium.

Classic examples are Liti's work as well as Loyd Cross' work.

Kaveh Sun Mar 20, 2005 7:49 am #4
Multiplex holograms produce a 3D effect using stereopsis, nothing else. In other words each eye sees a different view of a scene. Actually a multiplex holgoram is "autostereoscopic", which means that as you move your head around you automatically see the correct view. Obviously this is more realistic than just two views.

You can make a multiplex hologram where the image doesn't move at all, so the views are all of the same scene, but from different angles. The classic way of recording these views is to put the subject on a turntable and rotate it while taking the different views with a movie camera. (Or rotate the camera around instead.)

But people realised they make more interesting holograms by having the subject make movements during the shooting. So as you go from frame 1 to frame 100, say, you see the 3D image, but the subject seems to move too. So it's a cross between a 3D image and a movie.

Here's the problem. To see the 3D image, your left eye might be seeing through frame 30, your eye through frame 35. So you see 3D because the views are from different angles. But the subject has moved between the two frames, so each eye sees a different time of recording. Subject's hand might be down in the left eye view, but up in the right eye view. So there is confusion in the mind of the observer.

This is called time smear. The way to reduce it is to have very gentle movements so that two frames looked at by the two eyes are never that different.

Hope this is clear (and accurate!).

Personally, I prefer the "still" image variety.
Dinesh Sun Mar 20, 2005 2:01 pm #5
There is one other component. Sometimes, people try to put too many frames into the hologram. In movies as well as multiplex's, there has to be a certain "blank field" between frames because the effect relies on persistence of vision. If the frames are too crowded in the hologram, the field due to one set of frames (30 and 35 in Kaveh's explanation) is still visible while the hologram rotates to frames 31 and 36. The edges of the frames are superimposed on the hologram. You can't make the lines too narrow either, which limits the duration of the sequence to the length of the hologram. When I was at ABNH, the "lenticular" guy (we called them "lenticulars" at ABNH) was always complaining that the film sequences he was given were too long. If anyone has ever seen the series of cards we did for a restaurant chain, which depicted a few seconds of a sports event as a multiplex, sorry lenticular, on a standard collector's card, there's a good example of motion blur due to too many scenes.

If you see time smear with only one eye, Kaveh explanation makes sense if you moved your eyes quickly enough.. If two slits were imaged onto the same eye, I'd expect that the brain would convolve the two images and you'd get just a blur as opposed to a recognised set of two distinct images with an "overlap" blur. If you moved quickly, the blur may not be noticeable as such but a smear effect. That is, you see frame A, then blur, then image B. The intermediate blur may be interpreted as a smear of either A or B. There's also the factor that the brightness variations in both images may not be the same. If they're not, then the brighter ("brighter" being defined photometrically and not radiometrically) will predominate. But with two frames, I'd expect there wouldn't be much difference in brightness.

I haven't read Michael Halle's paper yet, so everything I say may be blown out of the water by it.
