Colin Kaminski Mon Oct 04, 2004 3:42 pm #5
I am no expert in this by any means and I only know what Dinesh and Joy have taught me as well as what gets posted here.

Broadband is created by having the fringe spacing vary through out the depth. Narrow band is made by making the spacing consistent throughout the depth. I have only used Dinesh's plates and they are coated to a consistent depth.

I don't have my notes handy but if I remember correctly, longer exposures and slower drying makes for narrower bandwidth. I am not sure what I can share of Dinesh's process so I will defer this discussion to him so he can decide what part of his commercial process he is able to share.
JohnFP Mon Oct 04, 2004 5:12 pm #6
Colin yes your are correct in that, you want differnt fringe spacings throughout the emulsion depth. In DCG, to get a broadband hologram you can shrink the different layers of fringes at different rates and then lock then in that state, though carful control of alcohol temperature and the difference of temperatures from one bath to the next. There are also other techniques and combination of techniques that may be used to get broadband. AmDi concentration, gelatin concentration, exposure time, emulsion thickness, bloom strength, hardness (age) of emulsion, wavelength/s all play a part in obtaining that hue of broadband light wanted in the replay.

[quote:fc52d4c483]I just want to know how deep a DCG Denisyuk can be made before I spend a lot. I don't think I ever saw one more than maybe 2" deep.[/quote:fc52d4c483]

I'm assuming the depth of the image and not the depth of the emulsion. If the former, then the depth of the image is dependant on the coherence length of the laser. Again, assuming that you're thinking of a single beam Denisyuk, the greater the coherence length the greater the difference between end of object and front of object. If you're thinking of an H1/H2 setup, then the depth of the image is dependant on the depth of the laser with which you made the H1. Once the H1 is made, it reconstructs the wavefield of the object beam, however deep that may be, and is now independant of the coherence length of the laser shooting the DCG. That is to say, the image depth of the DCG H2 derived from the H1 is independant of the coherence length of the laser shooting the DCG H2. Distance considerations in this case depend only on the difference between bs and H2 plate via H1 plate. The object itself no longer matters. So, in our case, we have a working coherence length of about 4 feet. We can make an H1 4 feet deep and make an H2 of that using a laser that has say a 5 inch coherence length. The question of viewing the hologram is another question entirely. The depth to which you can view a reflection hologram has nothing to do with H1 or H2, but with how narrow your source is. However, there are other factors. Assume you have an extremely narow band hologram. This means that all the fringes are all of the same seperation and so they all contribute to the image. The image will be very bright and pretty much infinitely deep, depending on the number of fringes. As the bandwidth increases, you get a statistical variation in fringe seperation, so much of this seperation, so much of that etc. Now the depth and the brightness become pretty dependant on the emulsion depth as well. The deeper the emulsion, the more fringes it can carry and so the greater the distribution can be. There's also a factor having to do with the fact that a deep image has a higher spatial frequency. So if your shooting a flat object a long way back, you need to capture higher frequencies and so you need to have a deeper emulsion.

In our case, we try to avoid deep images because our holograms are designed to be seen in white light, so we minimise dispersion. However, the deepest we've made is about 4 in. Our emuslion depth is approx 10 microns, our central frequency (central lambda) is about 570 and our bandwidth is about 50 nm at FWHM.This means that they come up about orange-green.

[quote:fb4a6fa35e]I am not sure what I can share of Dinesh's process so I will defer this discussion to him so he can decide what part of his commercial process he is able to share.[/quote:fb4a6fa35e]

You can pretty much say what you like, Colin. Nothing you learned here was in any sense a "secret commercial process" It was by and large standard. Any paper will give you a method similar to ours. There are things I'd rather not talk about, such as pseudo-colour and a DCG technique for flattening the Gaussian, but I if tell someone about it, I also say that I'd rather keep it between ourselves.

However, DCG is a strange beastie. There is no "way" of doing it. There are some who think if you want to do this, you need to do that. But that depends on the person's gelatin, the way it's prepared, the way it's developed etc. Another bloom strength, another brand with the same bloom strength or even anoth batch with the same manufacturere and bloom strenth may be diferent. DCG is very process-orientated, but you really need to understand and trouble shoot it. It's easy in one sense and not so easy in another. If you do single beam denisyuks, you go one way, if you do an H2 from a transmsiion H1 you go another and if you do an H2 from a reflection H1, you go yet another way.

After twenty years of using DCG, I think I've got a handle on it, but it still bites now and again.

In case anyone is not clear about this, this is a very difficult process to debug. I was in Dinesh's lab with his plates and he made the exposure and I was simply processing them with him standing over my shoulder and doing everything he said. It still took me three tries to simply recreate his results!

If you undertake this process make sure to record everything and be meticulous. Also, don't get discouraged when things don't work. Remember it is all about how hard the geletin is and start by learning all of the factors affecting the hardness of exposed and non-exposed reasons.

Just some advice from someone who has dipped a toe into DCG.
