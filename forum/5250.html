BobH Mon Dec 12, 2005 4:07 pm #10
> I thought he was saying, in the past holography was non shared.

His statements regarding this were in the present tense, despite the existance of this forum. Twenty-five years ago, I wanted to learn as much as I could about making DCG holograms. There weren't many computers on people's desks, and no internet as we know it, but I had no problem finding all the information I needed.

For example, the paper by Steve McGrew on color control. Also, communication with Rich Rallison (the original DCG eyeglasses and belt buckle maker). Rich has always been very open to sharing information about how to make holograms, especially DCG. All of the information about micro-cavities in DCG were published by the SPIE. All of the work on stereograms and Ultragrams were available via SPIE and/or the Lake Forest Symposia proceedings. As was everything you always wanted to know about making embossed holograms (see the paper by Jody burns in the 1985).

The hard-to-find information is not about how to make holograms, it's about what to do with them after they're made! A 3D computer graphics artist, for example, can build their own holographic printer and create beautiful images with it. Full color! And light them with high powered LEDs. So you have an excellent holographic image in one hand, and a great light source to replay it in the other hand. What are you going to do with them? Why would anybody want to buy them from you? Why will they value the image enough to go through the incredible hassle of displaying the hologram properly in their homes or offices?

I want to pipe in on this rock thing as it is one of the rare instances where content actually has been mentioned during my brief (~half dozen) visits to holographyforum - and I actually have experience imaging rocks (well....a rock). First-off: Bob, the magic in a moon rock is where it's been - not in what it looks like. I have a beach full of rocks and if I was to toss a lunar rock out there, I would bet dollars against dimes that no one would be able to spot it. It's magic, in my opinion, is not conveyed by pictoral representation and I believe Andy is correct. I feel that holography like photography must be able to stand on its own - if you have to tell me why this image of a rock is to be treasured ("it came from the moon") then the artcontent - like paintings done by elephants - is suspect at best. I have more than a few friends in the art business and they love to talk about "pride of ownership" as a necessary ingredient to instill in would-be buyers. Content and/or establishing a link to the creative process (connecting with the artist) are the primary reasons that people purchase art - that and a very visceral feeling that often accompanies exposure to an artwork - something that can't be explained. None of these factors uniformly permeates any technique - except at the point of introduction wherein an element of novelty ("I gotta have this while its still hot") overcomes buyer reluctance. Novelty, fortunately or not, establishes a fairly low price ceiling and therefore the "singing bass fishes" are not likely to command a high resale value. Rocks can be made interesting however.... and for those who are familiar with my work, I'll just leave it at that.

I generally agree with what you both are saying. I'm a technical holographer, though, and see a broader value to the technique than a means for artistic expression (whether significant or kitch). It's presence.

The moon rock's value is in knowledge of its story. And considering our everyday bombardment with pictures of things, I'd say pictorial representation would transmit very little of its magic despite the viewer's knowledge of the image's identity. A wide-angle, full-parallax, full-color, undistorted and properly displayed hologram of it conveys the gravity of its presence, however, as well as its image to the viewer. And because this is a passive presence, I think it works better than all other 3-D techniques that might compromise parallax or require viewing aids.

I have many holograms in my collection (mine and other's) which simply show a piece of sculpture in a black plate. In some, the sculpture was made annonymously. For others, it was made on commission (perhaps for up to $10,000). On one, the sculpture is by Fritz Scholder (a well known artist). What's the difference between these and the hypothetical moon rock hologram? I'd say nothing, because the goal of technical holography is to facilitate the presence of something; good, bad or ugly. Bad holography makes the good look bad, the bad look hideous, and the ugly impossible to appreciate for its ugliness.
