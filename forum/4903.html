Dinesh Thu Jun 16, 2005 8:59 pm #2
In general this would not work very well. It can be made to work with specific objects in spefic geometries, but it'd still be a poor alternative to direct lighting. The reason is that the object light must hit the plate and be co-incidental as far as possible with the reference light, ie the centre of the object illumination (the brightest part) must be co-incident with the centre of the reference beam. In your case, the light reflecting off the plate will hit the front of the object and reflect away from the plate, mostly. If the object is sufficently diffuse some of the scattered light may hit the plate, but this will be weak compared to the light being thrown away. Also, if you're hitting the plate with so much power that some significant part of the reflected light does come back to the plate, you're ratios are probably going to be too high, or too low, depending on what you mean by "ratio" - too much reference at any rate. It's possible to have a geometry where the reflected light does go back to the plate. A pyramid leaning towards the plate might do this, for instance, but you're pretty much limited with both objects and the geometry.

If you put a mirror behind the plate, you have the same problem that the object will reflect the light away from the plate, depending on object and geometry. In addition, you'll also be creating a holographic mirror which will be much stronger than the display hologram. Since this is stronger, it'll

diffract more of the reconstruction light than the "object fringes" which will result in a dim hologram and/or a noisy one. If the mirror is dirty, you'll have additional noise problems.

Most single-beam transmission geometries don't work very well for the reason you gave - very little to no light hits the plate. It helps a little to have a pretty wide beam coming in from behind the plate and hitting the object through the plate. Say, from the left bottom of your diagram and through the plate. Now place a mirror at the top right hand side of the diagram so that some of the beam coming in from the bottom is reflected back onto the plate. You'll need a fairly wide beam. If you adjust it properly, the laser-object-plate distance can be made prettyy near the laser-mirror-plate distance. The object needs to be a little ways from the plate to allow the beam to widen sufficently to envelop the mirror as well as the object.

Another possible geometry is to set up as in your diagram, but place a mirror at an angle to the plate on the left hand side and in front of the plate (basically off the edge of the plate). This mirror then reflects some light onto the object. However, for this set-up, you do need good coherence light. Reference path is:laser-plate while object path is laser-mirror-object-plate. In this case you want the object as close as possible to the plate.

I think it should be feasible to get something like the effect you want by making a holographic mirror/diffuser on a holoplate and using it as the front glass.

The holographic mirror would be made by making a Denisyuk reflection hologram of a reflective diffuse object (white card?) at some distance from the plate (closer = wider reflected beam spread) with the reference beam angle set to be the same as the final lighting angle. Processing would have to be done using a non-shrinking developer/bleach so that the hologram reconstructs well at the original laser wavelength. Then use the plate "backwards" as the front glass, emulsion side toward the object so that the pseudoscopic (real) image is projected toward the object, lighting it up.

I vaguely recall Jeff Blyth writing something (in "The Holographer"?) about using a hologram of a styrofoam cup for shaped illumination of scenes...
Dinesh Fri Jun 17, 2005 12:58 pm #4
Once again, the light from the diffuser hologram is going away from the hologram. Any light going from plate to object will cause object light to reflect away from the plate unless the object were such that the impinging light retro-reflected. In this scheme, assuming a reasonable 30% efficency for the diffuser shot in silver and 5mW laser, the diffracted diffuser image will be (5-losses)0.3 mW/sq cm. The loss term could quite easily be some 40% for a reasonable reference angle and polarisation ( rja4096 mentions an angle of 30 degrees to horizontal on glass. assuming glass of index 1.5 and s polarisation, we have a reflectivity of 18% on Fresnel reflection alone). Thus the object light, the intensity of the image of the diffuser, is about 0.3(5 - 0.45) = 0.9 mW/sq cm. This now has to reflect from the object. If the object has a high albedo, the reflection will be highly directional and coming from the plate, so unless the object is a mirror, it'll reflect away from the plate. If the object is a diffuse object, it'll have a low albedo and so very little light is reflected at all. Even less is directed towards the plate.

The nice thing about Jeff's mthod was that the " illumination image" (ie the styrofoam cup) directed light inward towards the object by surrounding the object. Thus the direction of the object light came in in such a direction as to reflect towards the plate.

In this case, the transmission image of the H2 would appear as a reflection image in the final H1. When viewing the transmission image on the final H1 hologram, the reflection image would rob light from the reconstruction process and so cause noise. This would be the same as putting a mirror behind the plate:

If you put a mirror behind the plate, you have the same problem that the object will reflect the light away from the plate, depending on object and geometry. In addition, you'll also be creating a holographic mirror which will be much stronger than the display hologram. Since this is stronger, it'll diffract more of the reconstruction light than the "object fringes" which will result in a dim hologram and/or a noisy one. However, if you're trying to use the pseudo of a reflection hologram to illuminate an object for another reflection hologram, then the faint transmssion image of the illuminating hologram will show up as a transmission in the final hologram. In either case, the same problem arises: illumination of an object by the pseudoscopic image of another hologram will scatter light away from the final plate. Unless, as in Jeff's scheme, the pseudoscopic image impinges onto the object by surrounding it, as with a styrofoam cup. Another method is to shoot an object on curved film, but curved away from the object, ie outward. Now reconstruct the pseudo, for which the film will now be curved inward, and place the object to be illuminated at the center of curvature. Shoot the final film also curved with the final film curved inward between the object and the illuminating hologram.

Here's another half-baked idea submitted for demolition:

Use a partially silvered plate, say a large 50-50 metallic beam splitter as the front plate (metallic side toward the object). This would bounce some proportion of the incoming light back up to the object to help light shadowed portions, of which some would be reflected back to the plate - not as much as for direct illumination but a lot more than zero.

Light from the incoming reference beam, the directly illuminated part of the object, and the part of the object lit by reflection would all be more or less equally attenuated by the metallic coating, so the beam ratio would remain the same, though an increased total exposure time would be needed.

The metallic reflection should be independant of polarization, so it should be possible to use the standard Brewster angle illumination technique for a polarized laser to reduce internal reflections in the plate stack. One problem with the original poster's suggestion was that to get any signifiicant reflection off the glass back up to the object, you needed to choose the worst polarization angle which would create lots of internal reflections. These would be seen as "wood-grain" in the hologram.

Presumably the incoming reference beam to the hologram is coming in at some angle. If the beamsplitter were partially metallic, some of the light would pass through it and reference the hologram behind it. However, some would undergo Fresnel reflection and bounce off at the opposite angle. So, (Jeez! Where's an envelope when you need it!)...If we set up a co-ordinate system with the reference point(s) along the y axis and the plate along the x axis, then we have two situations: a collimated reference and a diverging reference beam. In both cases,let's have the ends of the plate are at (x_p,0) and x'_p,0) and an object point at (x_o, y_o). Let's tackle the collimated case first:

The reference beam can be modelled as two rays coming from (0,y_r) and (0,y'_r) where y'_ r > y_r, and coming in at an angle r. Thus the equations for the two rays (the left hand edge and right hand edge of the collimated ref) are:

y=-(tan r)x + y_r

y=(tan r)x + y'_r

Thus in order to be illuminated by a Fresnel reflection off a semi-metallic surface at the plate, any object point must have:

[(y-y_r)/tan r < x_o < (y-y'_r)/tan r]

[(xtan(r) + y_r) < y_o < (xtan(r) + y'_r]

This places an upper and lower bound on the placement of the object. For the diverging reference beam, let the point of divergence (the origin) of the reference beam be a point (0, y_r). Thus the beam expands to hit either end of the plate at angles r and r' and, by a similar argument, place the upper and lower bounds of the object in the divergent reference beam setup as:

[(y+y_r)/tan(r') > x_o > [(y+y_r)/tan(r)]

[xtan(r') - y_r > y_o > x*tan(r) - y_r]

Dinesh Mon Jun 20, 2005 1:38 pm #14
Sorry, Bob. But Michael is right. My native tongue is mathematics. My first impulse, when I read Tom's concept, was that the object must be inside the reflected beam. Then I started thinking as to what that meant, exactly. Obviously, what that meant was that there had to be limits imposed on the objects position. So, what limits? Clearly inside the rays bounding the reference beam. Three cases, then: Ref is diverging, ref is converging and ref is collimated. I decided to ignore the converging case, since this is pretty rare. So then it was relatively easy to draw up the equations of the bounding line of the reference and, from there, the limits on the co-ords of the object fell out. The more interesting, and a little more complicated, problem was the limits on the orientation of the faces necessary to reflect the reference back onto the plate. I modeled the object as a polygon and started to create face and edge vectors, a la computer graphics methods, and tied them to the vectors of the reference beam so as to create the appropriate limits when it became apparent that the far more important problem was that if Joy didn't get her morning coffee pretty soon I'd be in deep <scatological substance> Thus the lofty ruminations and deep insight came crashing down on the cry of: "Coffeeee!"
