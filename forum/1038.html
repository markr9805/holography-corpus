From BBC News:

Graham Saxby died in March.

Graham Saxby did much to champion the science of holography, often in the teeth of opposition from the photographic establishment. The technique of 3D imaging was first developed in the 1940s but it took the arrival of lasers to advance the process. Saxby began his photographic career in the Royal Air Force before becoming a teacher in Wolverhampton where he built a holography laboratory and taught students from a wide variety of science courses. He went on to become a visiting lecturer at the Royal College of Art where he was one of the driving forces behind the establishment of the college's holography unit. In 1988 he published his book, Practical Holography which, nearly three decades on, is still seen as the definitive work on the subject. In 1998 he endowed the Royal Photographic Saxby Award for achievement in the field of three-dimensional imaging.

While not a holographer himself, his books certainly contributed a lot to the knowledge of holography. I think Graham's book and Fred's book are pretty much the bibles for display holographers. I met him in the early 80's, when we both showed up at the RPS Holography Group meetings. He was already a member of RPS, and, I think that he and Edwina were primarily responsible to have holography accepted by the RPS group in Britain. I seem to remember there was a certain amount of resistance amongst some RPS members because they didn't think of holography as a photographic method. I believe Graham smoothed the path for holographers. I remember Peter Miller was talking about the (then) new method of swelling to get colour (pseudo-colour). A lot of the audience was having trouble with the concept, but Graham pretty much got it right away.

Din Mon Apr 06, 2015 12:38 pm #12
As usual with holographic history, there's a lot of memories and recollections of who did it "first" {How many holographers does it take to change a light bulb? 10. One to change the bulkb and nine to say, "I did that years ago!"}

However, Bob probably has the best grasp of history of all of us.

My own memory is that Peter Miller introduced it to Britian, and I claim to be the first British holographer to make pseudo-colour holograms, with Paul Newman the second British holographer.

In 1982 Peter was working for Richmond Holographics, having been brought over by Edwina Orr. At that time, Peter had worked for Fred U (In fact, he made the eye hologram that adornes the back of Fred's book). At any rate, Peter told me he learnt pseudo-colour from Fred (Note to Martin: the technique he used was to pre-swell and do second and third shots). Anyway, Peter showed us his "Crystal City", which was a hologram of a small set of buildings in red, with a circle of light surrounding the buildings in green - he achieved this by lighting a lens at the edge. He also showed us a set of keys dangling from a car dashboard in pseudocolour. Of course, we were all intrigued by this (myself, Edwina Orr Dave Trainor, Paul Newman and Rob Mundy all worked at Richmond back then). This was 1982, so if there was anything published earlier, as Bob says, we didn't see it.

I and Paul Newman ended up being Peter's assistants and I particularly wanted to understand this pseudo-colour technique, so Peter had me working on some pseudocolour shots, while Paul worked on the commercial shots for Richmond's customers. No one I know of back then had seen these colour techniques, so everyone was intrigued. After that Paul started doing pseudo also. Then Dave Trainor and Edwina started wondering about colour mixing with this technique and ended up making those achromat reflections that they were known for. I can't quite remember who proposed the idea for Peter to give a talk at the RPS, it was either Edwina or Graham, but in the end, Peter did give a talk at the RPS, and, as I mentioned only a few understood the technique fully.

As for myself, I ended up with my own studio in Van Nuys, California, in 1987. I continued with the idea of colour mixing using pseudocolour, and managed to get hues that were not so saturated, pinks, cyans and browns, colours like that. I even experimented with, and created, a reflection rainbow hologram in silver, which i thought was impossible because silver does not have the bandwidth necessary. While I was at POC, around 1987, I managed a pseudocolour dcg hologram, using pre=swelling and multiple exposures.
Din Mon Apr 06, 2015 12:48 pm #13
> Martin: By the way, thinking again about TEA color control, back in the 80s some seemed to have attributed that method to Hariharan also.

Hariharan's book, "Optical Holography" has a section on pseudocolour (Chapter 9, section 6, if anyone has the book). He references his own paper in 1980: "Pseudocolour images with volume reflection holograms", Optics Comm., 1980, 35 pp 42-4. The book was publishged in 1984 and was one of the bibles of technical and display holography along with Collier, Burchhardt and Lin's book, "Optical Holography". There is no reference to pseudocolour holograms in Collier that I can find, so it's quite possible that if technical holographers in commerce or academia used these two books, they would have thought that Hariharan created the method. To my memory, there was little or no communication back then between the tech/academic guys and the display holographers.
