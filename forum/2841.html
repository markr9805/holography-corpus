How do you mean? Objects far away appearing the same size as objects close? I don't think so unless you come up with some way to make the film see this effect as your making the hologram.

> What happend, when the background of the hologram change e.g. black or white or retroreflection?

How do you mean?

> What happen when one change vary the color of the light that beams on the hologram?

After it's developed? For transmission holograms, it simply plays back in the color you are using to illuminate it. Some distortions happen because of the different color, but it's hard to notice unless you are viewing with multiple colors at the same time.

For reflection holograms, you only will see the hologram when it is illuminated with light that is the same color as the playback color. (The playback color is usually the same as the light used to make the hologram, but it can vary if using special treatments such as with 'TEA' or sugar to swell the emulsion.)

> What happend when you change the angle of the light beamed on the hologram?.

When viewing a finished hologram? You get a decrease in brightness and some visual distortion, and then you see nothing as the angle increases. For best playback, you need the light coming in at the same angle and direction as the reference beam used to make the hologram.

A special exception is if the light is coming in at 180 degrees from the reference beam (in other words, the film in flipped over). Then you get playback again, but sort popping out at you and inside out. (Pseudoscopic)

[quote:f7aff5f6f5]What happend, when the background of the hologram change e.g. black or white or retroreflection? [/quote:f7aff5f6f5]

Assuming that you're talking about reflection holograms, the reconstruction light reacts with the Bragg planes inside the emulsion to reconstruct the obbject wavefront. However, because the hologram is not 100% efficent, some light goes through the hologram. This light is called the "zero order" light It's possible to make a 100% efficent hologram, or very close to this with some materials such as DCG, in which case there is no zero order. Most holograms made on silver halide are about 20 to 30% efficent and most DCG are about 70% ecfficent. Note that I'm talking photometric efficency and not radiometric (there are bandwidth issues involved - DCG is not so much more radiometrically efficent than silver halide but it has a much broader bandwidth) Anyhow, using silver as a model, 70% of the light goes through the hologram as a zero order. Once through the hologram, it will behave exactly as any source impinging on any surface. On a black surface you'll get mostly absorption (hence holographer coat the back of the hologram with black paint), on a white surface it'll reflect as either Lambertian or specular or both, depending on the surface features. Not sure what you mean by "retroreflection" but the light will react as if the hologram were'nt there. Now this reflected or scattered zero order will go back through the back side of the hologram. In this case, since this reflected/scattered zero order is not at Bragg, it'll scatter further inside the emulsion. The net effect is that the viewer sees the holographic image but superimposed on that image is the scattered light from the returned zero order. This produces a glare which makes the image difficult to see. If the hologram is 100% efficent or close to it, then the back surface does not matter. However , it does matter which side you view the hologram. If you view it from the emulsion side, then there may be scatter from the front surface since the reconstruction light scatters as it penetrates the emulsion. If the front surface of the emulsion has a high lambertian scattering coefficant, even a 100% efficent hologram will glare when viewed from the emulsion side.

[quote:f7aff5f6f5]When the refernce wave has a wavelength of 200 nm and I want to reproduce the hologram after development. What light with what wavelength can I use or is it independent, when? [/quote:f7aff5f6f5]

It deopends on the angle of the reference beam and the object beams. It also depends whether you'rre making an optic (at 200nm I have to believe you are!) or a display piece. Very simply, the object and reference form planes inside the emulsion. These planes are oriented to a direction that's the bisector of the two beam directions and seoperated by half the wavelength. So, let's say, you shoot two plane, collimated beams at 200nm, one at normal incdence and one at 60 degrees from plate normal (ie a line perpendicular to the plate - holographers love 60 degrees!) The planes, called Bragg planes , are oriented at 30 degrees - the perpendicular of the planes points to 30 degrees - and are seperated by 100 nm. Now, any reconstruction source oriented at 60 will "reflect" (it doesn't really, it diffracts. A lot of people take this metaphor of reflection too far) at 30 degrees, provided that the reconstruction source is also at 200 nm. The basic idea is that the Bragg planes act as interference filters at the Bragg angle. Any wavelength not at 200nm will destructively interfere as it goes through the Bragg plane structure. However, if you launch the reconstruction source through another angle, you'd have to also change the wavelength so that the reconstruction wavelength at this new angle and wavelength, lambda see planes seperated at lambda/2. There's also the question of holographic aberrations in display holography. The image will aberrate as you go off Bragg. The usual aberrations are barrel and pincushion, caused by mismatch between the angular spread of the recording and reconstruction wavelengths, and coma and astigmatism caused by twisting the reconstruction beam off the recording beam axis axis

[quote:f7aff5f6f5]For example when you use 200 nm for reference wave and 180 nm for reproduction wave: will you see nothing or is it a gaussian attenuation, or no attenuation?[/quote:f7aff5f6f5]

If the Bragg planes were all exactly seperated by 100 nm (lambda/2) and the emulsion were very, very thick, you'll see nothing. There is a concept known as "Bragg selectivity" which determines the reconstructed intensity. The image intensity falls off as a Gaussian around the Bragg wavelength and angle. if you're exactly "on Bragg", the image is at it's brightest (by the way, I'm using the word "image" as a metaphor for reconstruction intensity. Clearly if this is a Holographic Optical Element, then there is no "image" per se). As you go "off Bragg, the image brightness falls off as a gaussian. However, the width of the gaussian depends on the thickness of the emulsion relative to the Bragg plane seperation. For a given Bragg plane seperation, the thicker the emuslion, the narrower the gaussian. Similar for a given thickness, the narrower the plane seperation, the narrower the gaussian. This is what enables holographic data storage. By using crystals of 1cm x 1 cm x 1cm you have a very deep emulsion and so a very high Bragg selectivity. Thus you can have a very large number of images, since each image only lasts for less than degree or so before it's "Bragg extinguished". The wavelength dependancy is also gaussian and also depends on the ratio of plane seperation and emulsion thickness. In display holography, generally you have a wide variety of plane directions, since the object light is now diffused with a large number of Fourier coefficants, however you still have the same plane sepration (half lambda of the recording wavelength). But on processing, the emulsion tends to alter which causes the average plane seperation to change and also to increase the distribution of the spread around the avergae - effectively you vary the sigma. This causes a greater number of wavelengths to be on Bragg and so a greater number of wavelengths are diffracted. The spread of wavelengths diffrcated is known as the "bandwidth" of the hologram. It's pretty dependant on the material. The actual Bragg wavelength will also decrease as a result of emulsion shrinkage.

So, let's say you make a display hologram at 633. On development ithe emulsion swells by a factor 15% (not uncommon) and also the Bragg seperation distribution increses by about 2%. Now the hologram reconstructs with a wavelentgth of 539nm and has a bandwidth of about 20nm. All wavelength from 519nm to 559nm will be diffracted along the Bragg angle.As I said above, silver has a pretty narrow bandwidth - about 20 or so - while DCG can have a bandwidth of the entire visible spectrum.

[quote:f7aff5f6f5]What would happen when I have a fix viewpoint and I move the lightsource? What would I see from the hologram? [/quote:f7aff5f6f5]

It depends on the angular spread of the reference beam and the object beam. If the objet were throwing off light through a very large angle and the reference beam were well collimated, then as you twist the plate the azimuth angle of the reconstruction source changes and you get increasing coma and astigmatism. As Tom found out, the image distorts along the plane of the twist - side-to-side if you twist it thus and up-and-down if you twist it that way. Of coursse this also depends on the bandwidth of the hologram. If you twist it too far relative to the bandwidth, you'll go off Bragg and the image disappears. If you restrict the object beam divergence (with an H1/H2 setup for instance, as opposed to a single beam Denisyuk) then the image may show coma/astigmastism if the object aperture is large, but usually, the image disappears before this happens.

Most holographic theory centers around the Kogelnik paper: H. Kogelnik, "Coupled wave theory for thick hologram gratings" Bell Syst Tech J. 48:2909-2947, 1969

Dinesh Wed Oct 27, 2004 9:07 pm #10
[quote:7d4e3fc445]What would happen when you use as reproduction light (using a reflection or a white light reflection hologram) that isn"t diffuse but directed (parallel lightrays) (normal light - no laser of course)? [/quote:7d4e3fc445]

If the hologram is reconstructed in white light, the Bragg planes will "choose" the appropriate wavelength and replay with that, all other wavelengths will destructively interfere. So, if after shoting and processing, the Bragg planes are about 225 nm apart, the hologram will reconstruct in green/yellow (~550nm) when reconstructed in white light.

If the reconstruction beam is not physically similar to the reference beam, you'll get aberrations and/or the image will disappear. Basically, you've got to match the spatial coherence of the reference as closely as possible.Since the hologram was referenced with a laser it needs to be reconstructed with a light source of almost zero dimension. The laser itself has a spatial coherence of less than 10 seconds of arc. Since this is not possible for most common sources, you need to get the source size to be as small as possible. An ordinary incandescent bulb placed close to the hologram has a fairly large filament size, with reference to the hologram, and so every point on the filament will reconstruct it's own image and the image is blury. If the bulb is taken far away, the filament size is reduced with reference to the hologram and the hologram is sharper. If a smaller source, such as a halogen bulb, is used then you can approach closer to the hologram. If you use a diffuse source, then every point on the diffuse source will create it's own image and you get massive blurring, since a diffuse source is a collection os a large number of point sources. The ideal source is the sun. It subtends an angle of about 30 seconds of arc and so is smaller than most sources. it's still not considered spatially coherent - you need about 10 seconds of arc for that.

If the reconstruction source does not have the same geometry as the reference, you get distortions of the image due to aberrations. There's a pretty large body of work on aberrations, but basically you have coma, astigmatism, barrel distortion and pincushion distortion. If the reference was collimated and the reconstruction is also collimated but coming in at a different angle from the reference you get coma. The image will seem to elongate and fuzz. If the reference was not collimated, then any mismatch between the reconstruction beam and the reference beam will produce barrel or pincushion distortion. Also, remember that the image in an H2 is actually the conjugate image and so needs the conjugate of the original reference. Ignoring that (so assuming you're making a single beam Denisyuk), if the reference beam was diverging by 30 degrees and the reconstruction beam is diverging at 60 degrees, it'll have barrel distortion. This means that the image will appear to be on the surface of a sphere or barrel. As you move the hologram it "rolls" (as Kaveh says). If the reconstruction is 15 degrees, it'll have the opposite pincushion distortion. The image will appear to be on the inside of a sphere and roll inwards. In either case, the image also exapnds or shrinks, depending on the mismatch between angular spread of ref and recon. In a refelection hologram there's no chromatic aberration since the Bragg selectivity wil "select it out". This is not true for a transmission hologram, especcially an achromatic open aperture hologram.

Dinesh Fri Oct 29, 2004 8:13 pm #12
It "stores" the "wavelength of an object" by means of the Bragg seperation. Unless the object(s) are colored (watch this space!), the wavelength of the reflected laser light and the reference beam combine to create Bragg planes. These planes are set a certain distance apart. This is due to the recording geometry and the wavelength. When you reconstruct under "white light", these planes filter out any other wavelength except for those set of wavelengths that match the plane seperation. If the hologram shows up as red or reddish under red light, it'd also show up as red under any colored light that has a red component - for example, purple light will show a red image as well as red light (note, I'm talking about the brightness of the hologram - this depends on how much red light you're throwing at it). The blue component of purple light will not survive the passage through the Bragg planes because the inter-plane distance is wrong for any component but red. In a color hologram, there are many sets of Bragg planes. The "red" Bragg planes show red images, the "blue" Bragg planes show blue images, the "purple" Bragg planes show red and green images etc. For this you do need white light, ie all the components for which there are Bragg planes in the hologram.

Kaveh Tue Nov 02, 2004 2:42 pm #15
> erich: What is chemically / physically the difference between a full-color hologram and a monochromatic one?

There are different types of full-color holograms, but let's assume you are talking about reflection holograms. A hologram made with a single wavelength only "knows" about the reflectivity of the different parts of the object or scene, at that wavelength, so there is no color information. This reflectivity or brightness is encoded in the emulsion, with the fringe spacing being tuned to that wavelength. So assuming no shrinkage, when we shine white light on the hologram, we get an image in the colour of the recording wavelength.

To get color information, we need to use at least another wavelength. So we get two or more sets of fringes (or Bragg planes) recorded independently in the same emulsion. When you look at the hologram, the colours mix in a way as to give colours approximating to the original colours of the object.

Hope this is clear. If not, ask again.
Dinesh Tue Nov 02, 2004 5:24 pm #16
A reflection hologram made with one laser of one color, say HeNe at 633, will make Bragg planes seperated at an average spacing of 633/2 or 316nm. However, because the object reflects light in different directions, the planes are oriented in different directions. Also, due to processing effects, not all planes are seperated at 315nm. So imagine there are 1000 planes in the emulsion. Some of them, say 600 are oriented "into" the reconstruction (which should be identical to the reference) and spaced at 316nm , say 200 are oriented at 10 degrees off the ideal reconstruction angle and, of these 200 say 50 of them are not seperated at 316 but at 290nm, let the remaining 200 planes be oriented at 26 degrees off the reconstruction angle and say a quarter of these are seperated at 225. Now the main reconstruction, the 600 planes, will give you a monocolor, red image at the correct angle when seen in white light. However, there are those fringes off the correct angle. So if you twist the hologram a little, the off-angle ones reproduce. Some of these are oriented at 290nm, so there's a hint of orange in the red image (2x290 = 580nm - orangeish). If you twist the hologram a loittle more, the other off-angle holograms show up. Since there are fringes of seperation 225, there'll be a hint of green in the image. However, twisting the hologram this way also introduces distortions of the image - called "aberrations", so there's a trade-off of sorts.

So, ideally you should see the entire image at one, or a small range of, angles and in one color. However, various physical and chemical effects caused by and due to the fact that you're putting a large lump of jelly in various different liquids and sloshing it about, will alter this idealised fringe structure to give "new" fringes. These new fringes enable the hologram to have a variety of colors at a variety of angles. In the picture from The Holographer, he basically takes the reconstruction beam and twists it a little to give a slightly different color or set of colors. This phenomenon of a varying set of colors in a reflection hologram is called "bandwidth". The greater the bandwidth, the more the colors and the greater the angles through which it can be viewed. Also, the softer the emulsion, the greater the bandwidth, because the softer emulsion exapnds more in the processing liquids.

In color, there are two or more sets of Bragg planes. So, if you shot the hologram with 633 and 514 you get, ideally, two sets of fringes at 316 and 257nm. However, due to the additive property of color, there'll be a lot of other colors caused by the mixing of red and green, yellow at 590 nm for example. These additional colors, like the yellow, create their own planes, so you have planes seperated at: 316 from the HeNe, 257 from the green and 295 from the (color mixed) yellow. When reconstructed, the 316 planes "take out" the red component of white light and you see that part of the image as red, the 257nm planes "take out" the green part of white light and you see that image as green and the 295 planes will show a yellow image. Take into account the bandwidth increase due to processign as well as any emulsion collapse in the propcessing stage and you get a whole new set of colours.

[quote:dd76625b94]'m sorry, but how does the hologram know which color it has to show?

E.g. you make a hologram of a green object and and you use a red laser the produced hologram (and the object) you see under white light is red.

Now you use a red and a green laser and take a hologram of the same object. Now it is green? Why?[/quote:dd76625b94]

The color of an object has to do with the wavelengths it reflects in white light. A red object is red becasue it only reflects red light. Now nothing in nature is that precise, so a "red" object may be mostly red with a little orange thrown in. When seen in yellow light therefore, it may look yellow. You may have seen the effect in some science museums where you stand in front of multi-colored lights and your shadows are all coloured. However, assuming that a real object is truly "red" it reflects only in the range of say 620 to 670. Any light that does not have those components, like green light, will show the object as black. So, if you shone a green laser on a red object, the object will not reflect the green light and will appear black. You won't get a hologram and will see nothing under white light (there's nothing to see!) If you use a red and green laser, the object will reflect the red laser and you'll get a red hologram (assuming no shrinkage in development). The green laser may as well not be there, it'll contribute nothing.

How does the hologram know? Because of interference with the reference beam. The reference beam comes in with it's crests and troughs and so does the object. Since both the object and reference are mutually coherent, these troughs and crests from both beams will re-inforce and destroy at half wavelength intervals. The "half wavelength intervals" will be half wavelength for the appropriate wave. A red laser and a red object will create troughs and crests at intervals of 316nm . Where they reinforce, the emulsion reacts by going dark, where they destroy, the emulsion does not change. Thus the reinforcement and destruction of these waves at half wavelength intervals is mirrored by a physical effect inside the emulsion . The emulsion, after processing, is dark every 316nm and light every 316nm. Thus you get a series of planes seperated by 316nm, or whatever the wavelength of the laser is.

When you shine a reconstruction white light at the hologram, the white light goes into the hologram. However, the white light consists of many components with different wavelengths. As the white light goes in, it sort of "bounces" in between these planes. If the component of white light has the same half wavelength of these plane seperations, the "bounced" light becomes very strong and exits the hologram, but if the component of white light that does not have the same half wavelength goes in these "bounces" are "off synch" and eventually very little or nothing comes out of the hologram. So you have your "red" hologram with these planes seperated by 316nm. Since, even in an ideal world there has to be some bandwidth, we'll say the the planes are between 316nm and 340nm. White light comes in with all of it's components. The components with wavelength between 680 (twice 340) and 633 gets reinforced with each "bounce" and come out strongly as "image" light while all the other components lose energy at each "bounce" and eventually gets lost. Thus the image is strongly red since all that comes out from the white light is the red components between 633 and 690.

The answer is basically to do with lighting of the object and aberrations. The image from the hologram is caused by diffracton, not reflection. Whatever wavefront went into the hologram, the object light due to lighting the object in a particular way, will come out of the hologram, assuming no aberrations. Say you illuminated the object - the wall, say - with a sideways light going from left to right and a particular surface feature on the wall threw some light going left to right, then you brought the reference in from the top. When you reconstruct from a beam coming in from the top, the wall throws back only that light that illuminated it. The light that went left to right in the illumination of the object will go left to right, despite the fact that the "wall" is apprently being illuminated from the top.

By the way, realistic lighting is one of the most difficult parts (and the most under-rated!) of holography. Danny is expanding on his talk on lighting at PCGGI in PCGGII. Danny 's background is photography and he showed us some nice photographic work last time in Texas.
