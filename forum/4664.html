I was always under the impression that a spatial filter removed things like the rings in a HeNe laser beam, causing a "cleaner" hologram. However, I extended that to understand that the resulting hologram would be therefore freer of distortions like random rings and artifacts. Recent discussion has led me to believe that a spatial filter increases the smoothness of textures, the ability to resolve detail, and the overall clarity of the hologram.

Which if any of these notions is correct?
Colin Kaminski Tue Mar 29, 2005 7:19 am #2
Having a uniform brightness in the reference beam improves the quality of the reconstructed image. If you place a white card in the film holder and look at the reference beam it should be uniform with no rings. You can achieve this by having no dust in the system and no spatial filter. However, with more than a couple of optics it starts to be easier to have a spatial filter or 5 .

For those newbies out there, always place a white card in your plate holder (or frosted glass) and examine the reference beam. This and good squeegee technique are the two simplest paths to clean holograms.

The first, if anything. Lets say the beam goes through a transparency with the letter "A" on it, and is the focused. The focused beam, at the focal point, is the fourier transform of the "A". It appears to be a dot because it's so small, but if you examine it, the dot has some form of structure, it's not a clean dot. Now, if there is dust in the beam, this is equivalent to a clean beam going through a transparency with lots of little dots on it. Thus the focused beam has the fourier transform of dots. This happens to be a set of rings. If these rings are blocked off and the beam re-expanded, you've "spatially filtered" off the effect of the dust and you get a clean beam - "spatially" because you've place an aperture j-u-u-st around the focused spot in space so that the central dot can go through but the rings are blocked off. All the other things about texture and detail are a by-product of the clean beam. The object reflects a clean, uniform beam and so seems to have a smooth texture. Everyday "white light" is never this clean and uniform. Largely, any thermal source like a bulb, radiates non-uniformly - there are areas of shadow within the illumination area called the penumbra, there's diffraction effects and scattering effects. So when you normally look at things in an "everyday" enbvironment you never see the smooth reflectivity off them that a smooth uniform laser gives. This gives the impression that the pinholed beam "enhances" texture and detail. The speckle effect also enhances the apparent resolution of detail, since you see the object's speckle as "detail" and speckle can be very small.

In the case of the letter "A", a pinhole cut out to resemble the perimeter of the fourier transform of that A would be the most efficient and effective, of course considering it is sized correctly.[/quote:ab4ab5e077]

Well, the fourier transform is not just a "shaped" dot with a precise perimeter. The two slopes of the A will give a series of dots and the straight bar will give a serier of dots. Basically, the ft of the A is a matrix of dots extending to infinity in x and y. You can't just cut a "shape of the ft of A" out since there's no perimeter, a fourier transform is a function derived from an integral! If you truncate the integral with some sort of screen, you're effectively truncating the intensity profile. If you eliminate any aspect of the matrix of dots, you'll effectively transform the A into something else. This is the basis of Optical Processing.

If you simply put an aperture the "shape of a cross", you won't be filtering anything. All you'll do is limit the intensity, ie the power spectrum. You can put apertures all over the landscape if you wish. You can dot them over the countryside like windmills! However, this takes us a long way from the original point. It's possible to aperture anything to provide any function. However, the act of spatial filtering in a display holographic setup "works" because of the nature of the gaussian profile and it's fourier transform.. Any other profile is not easily apertured to carry out the same function. Note: easily apertured. You have to take into account the effect of the modulus of the fourier transform as well as the phase factors.

The rule is, the shorter the focus length of your lens is, the smaller should be the hole. If it is too large, the filtering effect gets diminshed. Actually I use a 40x objective, and found a big improvement when switching from a 20um to an 8um diameter pinhole. Now there are no visible ripples any more across the area.

A few observations/tips in this context:

1) One wants to match have the beam nicely filling (or overfilling) the parabolic collimation mirror. On the other hand, the distance to the spatial filter is fixed and is given by the focus length of the parabolic mirror. So for making optimally use of the light, one would like to have things such that at the right distance from the spatial filter (ie, the focus length), the divergent beam has the desired diameter. For randomly optained surplus parts, this won't happen unless one is lucky. So what to do ? Simple thing:

place a mildly diverging or converging lens (depending on what one wants to achive) before the spatial filter and adjust its distance to the filter such that the beam diameter is right. Then place the pinhole back and adjust the spatial filter.

2) The other day I suddenly noticed large chaotic power fluctuations of my argon laser. I thought oh no, something bad is happening, is the power supply over the hill. are there plasma oscillations killing my tube, oh my ! It turned out that back reflections of my spatial filter into the laser caused this - just reshufflling/readjusting things a bit cured this problem. I don't know how to safely avoid this situation, though (maybe a blackened pinhole helps?)
