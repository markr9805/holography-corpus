It's possible to create a hologram purely from a computer. Zebra Imaging ( [http://www.zebraimaging.com/](http://www.zebraimaging.com/) ) does this. It's a fairly difficult technique involviing pretty high end computers. The idea is based on the fact that in standard holography you superimpose two beams - reference and object - on a photosensitive emulsion and capture the resultant interference fringes. However these fringes are the Fourier Transform components of the object wavefront. Thus, if you can calculate the FT of an object, you can image these FT components directly onto a photosensitive emulsion in what's basically a photographic process. This is pretty simplified since you're still trying to image detail at 2000 lines/mm and the calculation of an object involves a large amount of "number crunching". However, this technique need not use a laser

There are other ways of transferring CAD fies into a holoram. One way (the "traditional" way) is to use what known as a "multiplex" or "lenticular" holographic technique. In this technique, the image consists of frames from a video or a series of still taken from a camera or a series of stills taken fom a CG object at different views. So, in CG, you place your virtual camera at some point, render and import as a JPEG or some 2D format. Then you revolve the object or rotate the camera, take another view, import into 2D format etc. You then take all these 2D views and, using lenses, collapse the 2D image into a very narrow line image. In other words, pass these 2D images through a cylindrical lens so only one dimension collapses and you have a line. You then holographically image these lines on a holographic film next to each other. This creates a hologram of a series of lines, each line breing one entiore frame/2D image. When this is viewed your eye sees more than one frame simultaneously and so the CG object shows up as 3D. You can also image the full frames behind each other to multiplex into the film. A company known as Voxel uses this technique to create holograms from CAT and MRI data ( [http://www.voxel.com/](http://www.voxel.com/) ). In both the above techniques you need a laser

Yes and no. It's possible to reconstruct a hologram far away from a plate if you reconstruct with a laser. Without a laser, you cannot reconstruct outside of a few inches. The reason has to do with the kind of source used to reconstruct the hologram. Holograms start to disperse, ie the image breaks up into a rainbow, at a distance depending on how far behinf\d the plate the image is. The further the image, the more the breakup. If you see a hologram 2 or 3 inches (approx 5 - 7cm) from the plate it will look like coloured fringes around the image If a source is "narrow" (the filament or light source is very small) then you can get get better imaging because the colour breakup is less. The narrowest source possible is a laser, so with a laser you can see objects many meters behind the plate. However, it's possible to disguise the plate so that the viewer does not realise he/she is looking at a plate. A famous example is the Macy's hologram. The department store Macy's had a hologram of a hand holding a jewelled necklace. The plate was disguised as a store window and so passers-by saw an apprently floating hand sticking out of the department store window. Apparently someone thought it was the devil's work and started hitting the "hand" with an umbrella. The story goes that when the umbrella passed right through the "hand" she almost fainted. This is probably an "Holographic Urban Myth", a nice story but probably not true! The type of hologram you're referring to is known as a "Princess Leia" hologram, from the movie "Star Wars" and is so far impossible.That's not to say you cannot create 3D techniques that will give the appearance of a floating 3D object, but they're not holographic. Floating images can be made using projection techniques where the image is near a large concave mirror. The concave mirror then magnifies and projects the image into space. Unfortunately most people think this is what holography is.

[quote:aea82155a9="Asterix"]Moreover in viewing the Hologram process the reproduced image is 1:1 with

the original or it is possible to view duble and so on.. just using more

powered lasers? [/quote:aea82155a9]

Usually the hologram is the actual size (1:1). It is possible to reduce or expand the image. The technique mentioned earluer, multiplex or lenticular holograms, uses 2D frames. So, it's possible to reduce every frame and make the whole image smaller by using smaller frames. Another way is to shoot the hologram with a very converging beam and reconstruct with a very diverging beam, this will make the hologram larger, or vice versa (shoot diverging - reconstruct converging) to make it smaller. This technique involves other distortions so you have to choose your object carefully. Still another way is to shoot with one wavelength, say lambda_1, and reconstruct with another, say lambda_2. Then the magnification will be lambda_1/lambda_2. However if you choose both lambda_1 and lambda_2 to be visible, then the magnification will be small. It's not possible to magnify by just using a more powerful laser. More power in the laser will create a brighter image, not larger or smaller.

Dinesh Wed Feb 16, 2005 3:40 pm #7
I suspect that one problem between the laserjet and the inkjet may be the way the ink is splattered. With an inkjet the "pixel" or ink drop is probably spread out a little. This may result in non-sinusoidal fringes and hence non-lnear reconstruction. It may be interesting trying an inkjet with different typers of papers. My understanding is that the size of the ink drop varies on different types of paper due to differential absorption. Another possibility may to mirror or silver-foil back the transparency and view in reflection mode. Another problem may be that the inkjet simply does not print dark enough, perhaps due to absorption in the transparency, and so there is too little fringe contrast between the low and high fringe intensities to diffract.
