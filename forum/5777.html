The first interferometry group that pops into my head is one of our members, Polaris Research Group,

[http://www.polarisresearchgroup.com/](http://www.polarisresearchgroup.com/)

Another group doing it is an old friend, graduate of Lake Forest College, John Newman, and his site is

[http://www.laserndt.com/](http://www.laserndt.com/)

An interesting project that another friend of mine, Lou Spoto did for in-house evaluation at ITW was to use high speed cameras to look at real time fringes of a dynamic event. (This was about 10 years ago!) ITW was making a nail gun, and they wanted to see the stresses. He set up a real time system by making an exposure of the gun, processing and replacing the plate kinematically, then looking through the plate with the high speed camera. Triggering the gun would cause the fringes to move, faster than the eye could see, but with the high frame rate the fringes could be studied at his leisure.

My first full-time professional holography job was for a company that was one of the first non-destructive testing organizations, Magnaflux. If you have ever worked in a big machine shop, you might be familiar with the technique of Magnafluxing, where a part is magnetized, then "painted' with a kerosene based vehicle containing iron oxide particles colored with Day-Glo paint. These rust particles would accumulate in hair line crack that would be quite vexing to see with the unaided eye on a shiny metal part, but are very evident under a black light.

So they thought they would be selling Magnaflux machines forever, but since a lot of applications didn't use iron or steel they had to get into new technologies. I answered an ad in the Chicago Tribune for Holographer Wanted, and of all the people they interviewed, engineers and Ph.D Physics types, I was the only one who had not only made a hologram but brought one to the interview! So I got the job since they knew I had the goods!

Unfortunately I got laid off since Magnaflux was a little behind the times in getting into new technology and they were in financial hot water. ITW eventually bought them, as well as the last company I worked for as a full-time holographer, CFC!

A professional society ([http://www.lynceetec.com](http://www.lynceetec.com)) provides digital holographic microscopes, measuring at nanoscale the height (reflection mode) or the optical path length (transmission mode) induced by specimens. The different applications to use this types of microscopes are life sciences, micro optics, microtechnology, semiconductor...

You could find a brief history of digital holography, tutorials, basis recontructed images and different publications on this internet site.
