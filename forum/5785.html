I use an overhead flat mirror quite often and have the spatial filter about 6 feet away from the overhead mirror. It is then another 3 or 4 feet to the plateholder in a single beam reflection hologram. All seems well and it is not noticable that the holographic image is a slight bit different size then the original. But I have been playing with a lens in the hologram in which I want to magnify something. Well, in displaying that hologram the image magnified by the lens is not magnified anymore. The diverging reference beam in making the hologram changes the magnification properties of the lens in the hologram when it is replayed with a standard white light (diverging of course) such that its magnification power is reduced dramatically.

I know this is probably well know in theory, math, reading and basic understanding. I thought I would just mention a direct observaton. I just never had to bother with it before. I guess it's time to permanantly mount the collimator in an overhead fashion.

Also, what I am currious about is, if I make a Reflection H1 to reflection H2 proximity hologram of that hologram again with a diverging beam, will it restore the lens' properties back to it's original state or actually make the image though the lens even smaller. Hummm.

dave battin Fri Sep 15, 2006 9:31 pm #4
Hi john, the use of collimated light is always best , attempting to use a converging reference beam means you have to have a LARGE collimating mirror. i use a 10 dia. 56" fl. parabolic located about 36" from my spatial filter, and located between the two i have a negative meniscus lens to "fool” the collimator into thinking the spatial filter is located at 56". i don’t think the H2 is as sensitive to the collimated light as the image is usually close to the film plane. If you use a lens as an object (example to show a dog under a magnifier glass) be careful to not have it to close to the focus of the lens, if its to close and your NOT using collimated light or a conjugate reference beam ,it might distort your magnified image.. Wow does that make sense?
dcgman Fri Sep 15, 2006 10:21 pm #5
If the lens is magnifying a diffuse object, then the only parameters that matter are the distance between the object and the lens, and the distance between the holographic (or virtual) lens and the observers eye. How can the laser lighting geometry change the focal length of the lens? If you're not getting any magnification, try changing either your viewing position, or try changing the position of the lens relative to the object you're trying to magnify, & re-shoot the hologram.
Ed Wesly Sat Sep 16, 2006 2:19 pm #6
This looks like a job for Benton Math equations Magnification and Horizontal Focus!

[http://www.holographyforum.org/HoloWiki ... 281.2FR.29](http://www.holographyforum.org/HoloWiki/index.php/Holography_Transmission_Equations_Part_II%0A%0A%0A%0Aand%0A%0A%0A%0Ahttp://www.holographyforum.org/HoloWiki/index.php/Holography_Transmission_Equations_Part_I#Horizontal_Focus_.281.2FR.29)

In this case, m, order will drop out, since we are looking at the first order. (This equation applies equally well to reflection as well as transmission!) The lambda over lambda also drops out under the assumption that this reflection holo is processed narrow band enough so that it will light up under the laser light that made it and reflects that laser wavelength as the dominant color in its replay. This might not be the case in John's hologram, but it makes life simpler for us now.

Magnification compares the original object's positin with it position in the hologram, so we first have to solve the horizontal focus equation. Plugging in 3 meters for reference illumination (adding up the 6 feet plus 3 feet = 9 feet about 3 meters in the original post), an educated guess of of 30 mm (about 1 1/4") for the object position's distance from the holo plate. This object position is the location of the magnified virtualimage as seen through the lens, not the actual object.

Then it just becomes a job of plugging and chugging to get the new image position when the replay reference beam beam position moves in to 1 meter away, for instance.

The result is the that the 30 mm away object is now located 29.4 mm behind the plate! Which doesn't seem to jibe with John's observations!

Is the object really magnified in the actual object space? As dcgman points out, the object should be less than one focal length behind the lens to forma magnified virtual image which is further behind the lens than the actual object. (Magnifying glass optics)

If the hologram is processed so that a virtual image can be seen under the light that recorded it, and laid back in the recording position, the objects should be reconstructed one to one life size. Then pulling the hologram back toward the spatial filter while observing the reconstruction should show when there is no longer a magnification. Could be I did the wrong equation!
