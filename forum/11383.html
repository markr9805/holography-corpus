To make part of the image come out of the plane, the H2 (the white light viewable hologram) has to straddle the image. In other words, if you make a laser transmission (H1) hologram, then, by flipping the plate around, the image appears to float in mid air in front of the plate. If you now place a second plate "inside" (straddling) the image and make a second hologram (H2), part of the image comes out of the plate. There are other methods, such as reflecting an object in a curved mirror, which will cause a reflected image in free space; and you can now place a plate into the image for a second hologram. This is usually called "one step image plane holography" because you don't need an H1.

For the microscope, or any other 'key hole" effect, you make a double exposure. First, you make a hologram of the entire object - the microscope, in this case, on half a plate (this means exposing half the plate - blocking the reference beam from one half of the plate). Then, on the other half of the same plate, without moving the microscope, you cover the entire microscope and leave a small aperture - a 'key hole" - over the opening, the eyepiece of the microscope, in this case. Now you develop the plate and the image is split into two images. As you move your head, you see the entire microscope in one position and the eyepiece only in another position. If you now make a secondary hologram (H2), if you place your eye on the eyepiece, you'll see through it, as if it were a real microscope.

It seems I was wrong about how the microscope was made - sort of. It is a two-H1 hologram, but not on the same plate.

I was talking to Joy (wife), who set up all our display holograms, and she said it's made with two H1's. The first H1 was the complete microscope, then the second H1, a second plate, was just the eyepiece opening of the microscope. So, basically, you illuminate the entire microscope and shoot it on one plate, you then swap plates for a second H1, cover up the microscope (so no light hits the plate from the microscope) and have an opening - a keyhole - over just the eyepiece so that the only light hitting the second plate is through the eyepiece. Ideally, you should place an object at the base of the microscope and back-illuminate the object, so that only light passing through the microscope tube and exiting at the eyepiece hits the second H1. However, we have a telescope hologram with the same effect, and Joy had never seen the Walter Spierings microscope. When she saw it on a you tube video, she thought that the "eyepiece" was just a white disc placed at the eyepiece of the microscope, so as to simulate light going through the tube. The H2 is then shot with the first H1, then the second H1 is placed and recorded on the same H2. The effect is now a hologram with the entire microscope (first H1), and, if the eye is placed at the eyepiece, light from the the eyepiece alone (second H1) so it looks like light coming through the tube, because the relative positions of the eyepiece in the microscope (first H1), and the eyepiece alone (second H1) is identical.

As for placement, you can't have the object too close to the H1s, because you have to illuminate the entire object so that the light from the object hits the H1. If the object is too close to the H1, it's impossible to illuminate it. However, because the relative positions of eyepiece of the microscope and the eyepiece alone is the same, it doesn't really matter where the microscope is relative to the H1's. In the H2, you probably want to "slice" the H1 images so that the eyepiece is near the image plane, because the further away any part of the image is from the image plane, the more blurrier it is.
