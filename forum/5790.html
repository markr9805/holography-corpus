> JohnFP: Humm, I was wondering. How do you make a full color hologram of an object that has true yellow in it? That is, the paint reflects nearly all 555nm and hardly non of the green or reds lines found in a laser?

If, say 457, 532, and 633nm were used, then I don't think yellow rendition should be a problem if everything else is done correctly. As I recall, someone reported (maybe Bjelkhagen) that a big improvement in color rendition was noted when a doubled YAG laser was used at 532 for the green, instead of argon's 514. I think it was the yellow rendition that improved significantly, but I'm not sure. This was a long time ago, and I think nowadays 532nm is the staple green wavelength for color holography.

Yellow paints seem to reflect a lot of longer wavelengths also. Just by looking at the graphs in the Mayer book, it doesn't seem obvious that these paints appear as yellow. Almost all of them show rather even reflectance through yellow and into red. I guess they actually do reflect all those colors (yellow through red and beyond) but when those pigments appear in normal sunlight, they appear as yellow.
Ed Wesly Thu Sep 21, 2006 7:27 pm #6
It's not nature but our eyes that create the yellow. There is an overlap between the sensitivity of red and the green cones in our eyes, and when both are stimulated that provides the appearance of yellow.

See for instance p. 272 of Seeing the Light. Also [http://www.glenbrook.k12.il.us/gbssci/P ... 12l2b.html](http://www.glenbrook.k12.il.us/gbssci/Phys/Class/light/u12l2b.html), which give the yellow range from 577 to 597 nm.

> The peak response for the long cones is at 570 nanometers (yellow), medium at 543 nanometers (green), and short at 442 nanometers (between violet and blue). But the three cones are sensitive over broad, overlapping wavelength ranges, which means two different spectra can cause the same response in a set of various cones.A good example of this is yellow. There is a certain narrow range of wavelengths that we might call "pure" yellow (or another for "pure" blue, and so on). However, the same set of cones that reacts to a light of pure yellow also responds to the superposition of pure red and pure green light.
