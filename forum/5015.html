Acoustic holography has been around a long time. Someone gave me his collection of SPIE journals from the 60's (hard, black binders!) and I was surprised how many papers there were on acoustic holography. It seems the technique got nowhere because apparently there was no way to get a medium to maintain the fringes. There was a device called Carver Sonic Hologram out many years ago. It seems that that company is no longer around, but Bob Carver, the inventor is still active, his current company is linked below:

[http://www.sunfire.com/](http://www.sunfire.com/)

but it doesn't seem like the holographic feature is offered anymore.

I remember hearing a demonstration, hearing who else, The B-52's, (one of the few bands ever to be holographed!) and it did kind of seem to give a z direction to the source of the music, i.e. instruments were in front of or in back of the speakers. The beauty of it was that it only used two speakers.

In the early '70's there were some 4 channel reel to reel tapes made, so that you could use two sets of amplifiers and speakers for left and right front and back channels. But that kind of fizzled, since you needed 4 replay heads.

And I remember Sansui had a "Quadraphonic" system which would encode 4 channels onto a vinyl disc, circa mid '70's. I have two Sun Ra albums, "Space is the Place" and "Discipline 99", never did enjoy them in Quad, but did hear the system demonstrated at a music store when I was in college in Urbana, IL. This device utilized 4 speakers. People could bring in your own discs to hear how the new system improved things on the old stereophonic system. At the store I was at, the Sansui rep's brought the Japanese inventor on tour, (or was he just a shill, we wondered?) and of all the stuff he had to endure that day he really like what I had brought, Hot Rats by Frank Zappa, the Gumbo Variations in particular.

Most of these systems work off of phasing. An old audiophile trick was to hook up a third speaker to the amplifier using the two "hot" (not common) wires going out to the l-r speakers. (The "derived" third channel, it was called.) This would pick up the out of phase components of the two sides. You might have to put a potentiometer in one of the wires to control the volume. If you put it in the back of the room it would be a great effect! There was a way to hook up a 4th speaker, but I forgot how to do it to get the common sounds.

This worked really well, especially on old-fashioned stereo discs, engineered in the days when you could buy either mono or stereo versions of albums. There was a lot of out of phase things put on the stereo disc so that those records would sound wierd when played with monophonic cartridges. I don't know how it would sound with today's music which seems to be a muddy mix of everything in the center, but why not give it a shot! It would be easier than trying to find this antique audio equipment!

The methodology seems to be that the more spatially coherent the source, the narrower the "beam". There are all kinds of fourier theorems about restricting the spread by cutting down the frequency spectrum. In order to get a high spatial frequency, you need a narrow source. For example light is considered highly spatially coherent if the source subtends approx 10 minutes of arc. However if you want a narrow source with respect to the origin, you need small wavelengths, hence ultrasound. Since you can't hear ultrasound, you sort of hope that there's some non-linear effect that'll double the wavelength and put it in the audio range. Ordinarily, the response of air to the kind of (very, very) low amplitudes of noise is linear. The sound energy pushes the air - the air contracts and exerts pressure on the succeeding bit of air, which then contracts and puts pressure...etc. The pressure wave propagates transversely as a linear response to the sound. If the volume of the ultrasound, or any sound although unltrasound probably has more energy, is high, the pressure amplitude is high and this will create a non-linear pressure response, just like nonlinear effects in materials using lasers. This will generate frequencies in the audible range.
