Kaveh Thu Feb 23, 2006 8:48 pm #3
For me, a true color or full color hologram is where the colors in the holograms are identical to or approximate those of the real object.

A pseodocolor or "controlled color" hologram is one where this relationship does not necessarily exist - you choose what part is what color.

Kaveh Fri Feb 24, 2006 1:26 pm #11
I think John is using a method which is half-way between pseudocolor (I don't like this term either as it sounds cheap) and natural color.

Pseudocolor is made using a single laser wavelength, and other tricks to control the color. The final color is not, in general, related to the color of the object.

Natural color uses more than one wavelength, and the final color is related to the color of the object.

John uses more than one wavelength, but he is controlling the color, not producing natural colors.

Kaveh Sat Feb 25, 2006 12:50 am #21
You're opening a big can of worms here, John.

Color science in general is very subtle and very complex as it involves several disciplines. For example, if I say something is yellow, and you say it is orange, who is right? How do we prove who is right? That's just an example of the subtlety of color.

Back to the CIE chart. I intend to write a comprehensive explanation of my reasoning in color holography, but for now, here are some thoughts:

1. You need not cover all the area of the triangle. Everyday objects lie well within the triangle and towards the center. In fact most of the triangle is so saturated that there are no objects in existance which correspond to it! Most papers in color holography ignore this fact.

2. When looking at a real object in daylight, we are getting the reflectance value throughout the spectrum. But when using, say, 3 laser wavelengths, we are only testing the reflectance at those discrete wavelengths. You might expect that there is no chance of getting a good color rendition, but it turns out that there are three "magic" colors, namely around 450, 540 and 610, that give a superb color rendition, as good as any color photograpy process.

So, using these three wavelengths we should be able to get extremely high color quality.

I published all this in 1989 (Proc SPIE vol 1051, Practical Holography III, pp 6-11). People have come up with all kinds of wavelengths since then, but I still stand by mine.

Ed Wesly Sat Feb 25, 2006 4:17 pm #25
To kind of paraphrase one of Kaveh's papers, the one that was in the LFC ISDH of 1991, there is "Wintringham's gamut" of everyday colors. Wintringham plotted the reflectances of everyday objects for color TV research, and it is a really small part of the CIE chart. Kaveh's paper is the only place I've seen it (in my own library).

If you have the classic everyman's optics text, "Seeing the Light", they have a CIE chart with the gamut of reproducible colors via printing inks, cathode ray monitors (TV's), and color photos. Apparently we have been quite happy without the full CIE range of colors.

No matter what three laser lambdas are used, a big problem for color saturation/accuracy is the bandwidth of the replay colors. Looking at the replay of a specular highlight in a reflection hologram, especially a deep one, or a hologram of a mirror replaying a white light source, not only can you see a spectrum with a bright replay centered at the desired replay color, but a " dispersive spike" of other colors pointing toward the mirror image of the replay source. Usually there are black spots in it, unlike the complete spectrum of a transmission element.

Satisfying the Bragg diffraction equation for the wavelength of interest gives the brightest replay color. Other colors can fit into the Bragg structure, not utilizing every plane, but some kind of common multiple of recorded fringe spacing and reconstructed wavelength to give weaker constructive interference. The structure can also give rise to destructive interference of some wavelengths which explains the black holes in the replayed spectrum.

So using a broadband white light source will give rise to other weaker images from each of the primary color exposures. This will desaturate the replay colors. Using lasers or LED's to illuminate will cut down this effect.

I am in the camp that says that true color or accurate color comes from RGB analysis of the scene and RGB synthesis of the scene, just like in color photography. (Although that system uses RGB analysis with three layers and CMY synthesis.) Everything else wuld be pseudo-color tricks.

But there isn't anything wrong with that! As long as there is something that looks like a real object, it's cool! Like flesh tones look real, skies are blue, grass is green, oh, that's the benchmarks for color photo!

Kaveh Mon Feb 27, 2006 11:13 pm #35
OK. I think we can simplify things by forgetting holography for this discussion. I think that we can do this in order to choose our wavelengths. And I wish other authors discussing wavelengths for color holography would also see that we can do this.

Tell me if you disagree, but I think that the argument is, if we were to illuminate a scene with a light source, continuous or discrete, what does it look like to the eye?

The question is, is there a set of discrete wavelenths which, when used in this illumination, shows the scene as we expect to see it, e.g. your yellows as yellow, etc.

Well, the standard measure for what you are seeking is the "color rendering index", or the CRI of a light source. So is there a small set of wavelengths, 3 say, which have a CRI comparable with tungsten light or sunlight?

This is the question I tried to find the answer to when I was doing my research in color holography. I realised that the answer is nothing to do with holography at all, and I searched the literature and asked color scientists. The answer came in a paper written by W A Thornton in 1971 (Luminosity and color-rendering capability of white light, J Opt Soc Am. 1971 61 pp1155-63.) He took a general look at what combination of wavelengths have the best CRI. Amazingly, he found that three narrow bands around 450, 540 and 610 were very special, and had a disproportionately high effect on CRI. His conclusion was that a light source having very narrow wavebands around these three points had better CRI than even a flat, continuous spectrum.

My conclusion was that this is great news for holography, as we can just use these three wavelengths, and get color rendition even better than conventional photography. At least the theory says that.

The holographic process, if we neglect noise, shrinkage, etc, just records the reflectivity at each of these wavelengths and replays them. That is why I think we can forget holography when choosing wavelengths.

So I would say you should look at the reflectivity in these three wavelengths, and see how the two yellows compare. I would be interested to know that. I know for a fact that 514 is bad for rendering yellows.
