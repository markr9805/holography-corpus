Guest Tue Nov 02, 2004 2:27 pm #4
I have purchased a similar laser, and have discussed others offered by ebay vendors. If they don't list the "measured" output power, they claim the "rated" power. Sometimes they just show a picture of the label. Be sure to look up the specs from the laser manufacturer before bidding.

The one I bought was a Melles Griot, rated at 15mW, but actually putting out about 7mW. On top of that, the output is random polarized ' ' I only paid $35 for it so I don't loose much sleep, but it's not what I expected from a listing that said "15mW Hene, good for holography..."

Note: the random polarized laser does make holograms, as long as polarization is not an issue for you. See: [http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=25](http://holographyforum.org/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=25)

I've heard of a lot of these HeNe's being offered putting out half the rated power (Maybe thats when OEMs deem the laser needs replacing in whatever equipment they have used it in and decide to send it to the surplus market?)

BobH Thu Nov 11, 2004 5:13 am #6
It's either circular or random. You can use a quarter wave retarder to test. If it's circular, a quarter waveplate will make it linear. If it's random, the quarter waveplate will have no effect.
Dinesh Thu Nov 11, 2004 3:43 pm #7
It's highly unlikely to be circular. It's probably random. Could you split a circularly polarised beam and still make interference lines? I'd have thought that unless the path length difference was exactly a multiple of the circular period, the two waves would have different polarisations, since the polarisation state is now a function of distance.
BobH Thu Nov 11, 2004 5:04 pm #8
It's my understanding that circularly polarized light would interfere as you suggest. At all points downstream from the beamsplitter, some of the light of each beam would be "S" (the S component), and some would be "P". The resultant polarization rotates, but it can be broken down into its components, and they interfere.
