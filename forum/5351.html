The Royal Photographic Society Holography Group

is pleased to announce a talk entitled

"Catch that Plankton: Underwater Holography"

by

Henry Nebrensky

Oceans cover nearly 70% of the world's surface and provide food (from

fisheries) and climate regulation (by ocean currents and CO2 absorption);

yet many oceanic processes important for our survival remain poorly

understood. Holography, by recording 3-d images of volumes of seawater,

allows us to study not just the nature and number of individual

microscopic marine organisms but crucially the spatial relationships

between them, opening a new window of understanding on to the world.

The recording of plankton has thus been a continuous activity since the

early days of holography while Holomar, a recent European project led by

Aberdeen University, was even listed in the 2002 Guinness Book of Records

as "the world's most advanced underwater holographic camera."

This talk will describe the history and design of underwater holocameras,

with emphasis on the Holomar system; show some of the results obtained;

and briefly survey the next generation of instruments.

Date: Saturday February 25th 2006 2pm

Location: The Camera Club, 16 Bowden Street, London SE11 4DS

Admission: £2 to non-members

Tony Mon Jan 30, 2006 12:28 pm #3
Probably using a pulse laser. Some types of lasers emit a very short burst of light, instead of a continuous beam. If the pulse is short enough, it can reduce or eliminate the effects of motion. Sort of like a flash strobe on a camera.

The pulse might be 4ns long (four billionths of one second). This pulse of light would be about 4 feet long in air!
